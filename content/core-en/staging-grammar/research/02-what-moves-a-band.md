## 0. The ranked answer

If the module could only teach fourteen things, these, in this order. Ranked by **band movement per
hour of learner effort** — not by how a grammar syllabus is conventionally organised. A syllabus
ordered by payoff and a syllabus ordered by tradition are not the same document, and the owner
asked for the first one.

1. **Sentence boundaries.** Where a sentence stops. Fragments, run-ons, comma splices. This is the
   cheapest fix in the module, it is scored inside GRA *and* silently drags Coherence, and it is
   the one error type that makes a reader lose the argument rather than merely notice a slip.
2. **Subject–verb agreement, including across distance.** High frequency, fully rule-governed,
   fixable in a week, and visible in every single sentence — which means it sets the *density*
   number the band-6/7 line is drawn on.
3. **Articles and the generic-reference decision** (`a` / `the` / zero). The single highest-count
   error category in learner corpora. Not fully fixable — but three rules recover most of it, and
   the remaining 20% is not worth the learner's time.
4. **Tense choice against a stated time reference** — above all present perfect vs past simple.
   The forms are trivial; the choice is what learners get wrong, and getting it wrong is one of the
   few local errors that can turn global (it contradicts the time word in the same sentence).
5. **The five safe complex structures** (§3.1 Tier A: non-defining `, which`, fronted `while/
   although` concession, `whereas`, agentless passive, `tend to` / `is likely to`). This is the
   band 6→7 lever in its most concentrated form: maximum "range" evidence per unit of risk.
6. **Tense consistency across a paragraph.** Individually each verb may be defensible; the drift is
   what marks the script. Cheap to teach as a *proofreading pass*, not as grammar.
7. **Verb patterns and dependent prepositions as chunks** (`suggest + -ing`, `a solution to`,
   `an impact on`, `depend on`, `discuss ∅`). These are lexical, not rule-driven, which means they
   belong in the SRS with the whole chunk as the card — the direct hand-off to the vocabulary work.
8. **Countability and quantifiers** (`information`, `advice`, `research`, `equipment`; `fewer/less`,
   `number of/amount of`). Small closed list, high frequency, one week.
9. **The second conditional as a fixed frame.** The highest-yield single "advanced" structure,
   because Task 2 solution paragraphs and Speaking Part 3 speculation both demand it and neither
   can be answered well without it.
10. **The passive, taught as a topic-choice decision** rather than a transformation drill. Required
    for Academic Task 1 processes; natural in academic prose; and the *reason* to use it (what this
    sentence is about) is the part every course skips.
11. **Modality as strength-of-claim** — `must / should / could / might / tends to / is likely to`.
    Pays into GRA and simultaneously fixes the over-generalisation that the band-7 Task Response
    descriptor names as the residual band-7 weakness. Two criteria, one lesson.
12. **Relative clauses: defining vs non-defining, and the resumptive-pronoun trap.** High reward,
    two rules, and the comma genuinely changes the meaning — which makes it a "when to use which"
    point, not a punctuation point.
13. **Word form / part of speech families** (`economy · economic · economical · economically`).
    Learners feel this as grammar; examiners usually record it under Lexical Resource. Teach it
    here, credit it there — and build it as vocabulary cards, not grammar drills.
14. **Sentence-opening variety.** Not a grammar point at all in the traditional sense, but the most
    countable proxy for "range" that exists (§7), and the one thing our app can measure and show.

Everything below either supports one of these fourteen or explains what to *stop* teaching.

## 1. Where grammar is actually scored, and where it leaks

Facts first; these are freely usable statements about the scoring scheme.

- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy is one of four equally weighted criteria in Writing and one of
  four in Speaking.** It is therefore 25% of the Writing sub-score and 25% of the Speaking
  sub-score — roughly **one eighth of a reported overall band** when the four skills are averaged.
  Stated that way it looks small. It is not, for the reason in the next bullet.
- **Grammar leaks into the other three criteria, and it is never labelled as grammar when it does.**
  This is the most important structural fact in this briefing and it should shape the module's
  copy, because it is what makes a grammar module worth building at all:

  | The grammar thing | The criterion it is actually scored under | Why |
  |---|---|---|
  | Punctuation, sentence boundaries | GRA (writing) **and** Coherence & Cohesion | Scored inside GRA, but a fused or fragmented sentence also makes the reader lose the thread, which is a coherence event |
  | Hedging and modality (`tend to`, `is likely to`, `in most cases`) | Task Response **and** Lexical Resource | The band-7 criterion-1 residue is over-generalising; the fix is grammatical but the credit lands on task response |
  | Referencing and substitution (`it`, `this shift`, `does so`, `the former`) | Coherence & Cohesion | Pronouns and pro-forms are grammar; cohesion is where they are marked |
  | Word form (`economic` vs `economical`, `responsible` vs `responsibility`) | Lexical Resource, mostly | Learners experience it as grammar; examiners record it as word choice |
  | Dependent prepositions (`a solution **to**`, `pay attention **to**`) | Lexical Resource (collocation) | It is a collocation problem wearing a grammar costume |
  | Mid-clause stalling while you assemble a structure | Fluency & Coherence (speaking) | Speaking penalises the *repair*, separately from the error |
  | Register grammar — contractions, `you`, imperatives | Task Achievement (GT letters), Lexical Resource / general appropriacy (Task 2) | Register is scored, and half of register is grammatical form |

  **Consequence for our module: never scope a grammar point to "this improves your GRA."** Every
  point should carry the criterion(s) it pays into, and several of the highest-value points pay
  into two. In speaking, a grammar gap costs *twice* — once as an error, once as the hesitation
  produced while you try to avoid it.
- **Writing has two descriptor tables (Task 1 and Task 2), but the GRA wording is identical across
  them.** Only criterion 1 differs by task. So a grammar point taught once serves both tasks; what
  changes between them is *which* structures the task pulls (§6).
- **Speaking has a single GRA scale across all three parts.** The parts differ only in what they
  give you an opportunity to produce (§6.4–6.6).
- **Reading and Listening have no productive grammar criterion at all** — they are scored on raw
  correct answers. Grammar still earns marks there, receptively, and nobody teaches it. See §6.7;
  this is a genuine gap and it is cheap to fill.
- Each criterion is a whole band 0–9. Task band = mean of its four criteria. The Writing sub-score
  weights Task 2 roughly double (widely reported, not printed in the public descriptor document —
  see the writing briefing §7.1/§9.1 and keep the same hedge in our copy).

## 2. Grammatical Range and Accuracy at bands 5, 6, 7 and 8, in our own words

Clean-room paraphrase, written **behaviourally** so an author can turn a cell straight into a
teaching note or a report line. This is the calibration reference for the whole module.

### 2.1 Writing

| Band | What the script does |
|---|---|
| **5** | A narrow set of patterns, used over and over. Complex sentences are attempted and come out **visibly worse than the simple ones** — that gap is the diagnostic, not the error count. Punctuation is unreliable: sentences run into each other, or stop before they have finished. Errors are frequent enough that the reader spends real time reconstructing what was meant. |
| **6** | Simple and complex forms are both genuinely present. Errors appear in every paragraph, but they are the kind a reader steps over rather than trips on. The complex repertoire is small and reused — a `because`, a `which`, an `if`, an `although`, and that is the inventory. Punctuation mostly works; comma splices survive. |
| **7** | A real variety of complex structures, **and whole sentences that come out clean often enough that the run of them is noticeable**. Control is the word: the structures are chosen because the meaning needed them, not sprinkled. A residue of errors persists and each one is individually minor. |
| **8** | Wide structural range, and *most* sentences in the script are error-free. What remains is an occasional slip, or a phrasing that is grammatical but not the one a proficient writer would have reached for. Punctuation is invisible because it is right. |

### 2.2 Speaking

| Band | What the candidate does |
|---|---|
| **5** | Basic patterns survive. Anything more ambitious usually breaks, and sometimes breaks badly enough that the listener has to reconstruct the sentence. Long stretches are strings of short clauses joined with `and` / `and then`. |
| **6** | Simple and complex forms both occur, but the complex ones are a small fixed set deployed the same way each time. Errors inside complex sentences are frequent; they rarely stop the message. Repair is frequent and often backwards — the whole clause is restarted. |
| **7** | A range of complex structures used with some flexibility — the same idea can be built more than one way, and the second way arrives without a stall. Whole error-free sentences occur often, though a residue of errors persists throughout. Repairs are quick, forward-moving and rare. |
| **8** | A wide range used naturally and to purpose; most utterances come out clean; what remains is the occasional slip or an unnatural-sounding choice rather than a systematic error. Structures are launched confidently even when the speaker does not yet know how the sentence will end. |

### 2.3 Where writing and speaking genuinely differ — ranked by teaching consequence

The two scales are worded very similarly. The *skills* are not the same, and treating them as the
same is one reason grammar practice fails to transfer.

1. **Punctuation exists only in writing, and it is scored inside GRA there.** Nothing in the
   speaking scale corresponds to it. This makes sentence-boundary control a **writing-only unit**
   in our sequence — but the highest-value one (§0 item 1). Official guidance names using a comma
   where a full stop belongs as a leading accuracy fault.
2. **Writing gives you time to build a structure and then repair it; speaking gives you none.**
   Speaking Part 1 and Part 3 offer roughly zero planning; Part 2 offers one minute. So the *same
   structure* is two different skills. Speech needs structures that can be **launched before the
   speaker knows how the sentence ends** — `The thing is…`, `What I'd say is…`, `I suppose it
   depends on whether…` — because they commit to a frame and leave the content slot open. Writing
   needs structures that survive a re-read. Our drills must therefore have a **timed production
   mode** for the speaking-facing structures, and an untimed build-and-check mode for the
   writing-facing ones. Accuracy under no time pressure does not predict accuracy under pressure.
3. **Spoken grammar is a different grammar, and generic grammar books do not say so.** Ellipsis
   (`Been there twice.`), tails (`It's expensive, that flat.`), fronting, contracted forms,
   `sort of` / `kind of` as hedges, and clause chaining with `and` are all **correct spoken
   English** and would be errors in an essay. Meanwhile the essay's nominalised subjects and
   agentless passives sound stilted out loud. **Every structure in our module must carry a register
   tag — SPOKEN / WRITTEN / BOTH.** A zero-knowledge learner taught a single undifferentiated
   "English grammar" will produce written grammar in Part 1 and spoken grammar in Task 2, and lose
   marks in both directions.
4. **Self-repair is visible in speaking and scored elsewhere.** A backwards restart costs Fluency &
   Coherence even when it fixes the grammar. Teaching rule to ship: *repair only when the error
   changes the meaning; otherwise keep moving* — which makes the global/local distinction in §8 a
   **live in-exam decision**, not just a study-order heuristic.
5. **Range is demonstrated over ~400 written words versus ~11–14 minutes of speech.** In writing you
   can plan the range; in speech it has to be automatic. This is why the module's practice has to
   run past "can produce it correctly" all the way to "produces it without deciding to".
6. **Contractions flip.** Required in speech (their absence sounds wrong and can read as memorised),
   wrong in a Task 2 essay, register-dependent in a GT letter.
7. **Error tolerance at the top *feels* different.** Coaching consensus is that speech is judged
   more leniently, and there is an obvious mechanical reason — speech is assessed once, in real
   time, while a script can be read twice, so an error that survives a second reading is more
   visible. We should give the reason and **not** claim a different published threshold (§10.5).

### 2.4 One structural fact about the scale that changes how we teach

**Band 7 GRA is defined by a positive, countable property, not by an absence.** The scale asks for
frequent error-free sentences — not for a script without errors. A learner who believes 7 means
"no mistakes" writes short safe sentences and lands at 6, because range is marked too, and official
guidance states plainly that a script of short error-free sentences cannot score highly. Frame
every GRA lesson as *"produce more clean complex sentences"*, **never** as *"make fewer mistakes"*.
This is the single most useful sentence we can say to a plateaued learner and it should appear in
the module's opening screen.

## 3. The band 6 → 7 lever

### 3.0 Stated precisely

> Band 6 already attempts complex sentences — the descriptor says so, and the band-5 descriptor
> says the attempts come out worse than the simple sentences. **So attempting complexity is not the
> upgrade. Landing it is.** The band 6→7 lever is to raise the number of *complete, error-free
> complex sentences per hundred words*, and the cheapest way to raise it is to widen range using
> structures with a **small error surface** — not by adding harder structures.

Think of it as two dials, Range (R) and Accuracy (A). Band 6 is R-medium, A-medium. There are three
ways out and only one of them works:

- **The safe route** — A up, R down. Short clean sentences. Capped at 6, because range is marked.
  This is what "I'll just avoid mistakes" produces and it is the commonest self-inflicted ceiling.
- **The reach route** — R up, A down. Long ambitious sentences that break in the middle. Also 6, or
  lower, because now the complex sentences are visibly worse than the simple ones — the band-5
  signature.
- **The lever** — R and A up together, achieved by choosing structures whose *risk per unit of
  impressiveness* is lowest. That is a selection problem, and it is solvable. §3.1 solves it.

**Error surface** is our term for it, and it should be shipped to learners: *the number of
independent things that must all be right for the sentence to come out clean.* `whereas` has an
error surface of 1 — put it between two full clauses and you are done. Negative-adverbial inversion
has an error surface of 4. Both read as "complex". Only one of them is worth a band-6 learner's
nerve on test day.

### 3.1 The risk/reward tiers

**TIER A — best ratio. Teach first, drill to automaticity, expect these in every script.**

| Structure | Why it reads as range | Error surface | The one trap | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Non-defining relative `, which …` | adds a comment without a new sentence; unmistakably subordinate | 2 — comma **before and after**; `which` (not `what`) | learners open the comma and never close it: `Fees rose in 2019, which few families had budgeted for cut demand.` | BOTH |
| Fronted concession `While …, …` / `Although …, …` | concession is simultaneously a band-7 Task Response move | 2 — one comma at the clause join; no second connector | `Although it is expensive, but it works.` — the double connector | BOTH |
| `whereas` between two full clauses | pure contrast, no other machinery | 1 | almost none; occasionally used where `while` (time) is meant | BOTH (formal-ish in speech) |
| Agentless passive `X is/was + past participle` | the register of process description and academic prose | 2 — right `be` form; right past participle | built on an intransitive verb: `the population was risen` | WRITTEN (and Part 3) |
| Hedged generalisation `tend to` / `is likely to` / `is unlikely to` | buys Task Response credit at the same time | 1 | `tends to be increase` — the bare infinitive after `to` | BOTH |
| Second conditional as a **fixed frame** `If + past simple, would + bare infinitive` | the natural grammar of "what should be done" and "what would happen if" | 2 — past in the `if`-clause; `would` only in the main clause | `would` inside the `if`-clause | BOTH |
| Purpose clause `so that …` / `in order to …` | shows a reason relation without `because` again | 1 | `for build` / `for to build` instead of `in order to build` | BOTH |
| Comparative set `twice as many as`, `far higher than`, `the gap narrowed` | Task 1 is unwritable without it | 2 — one comparative marker only; right `as … as` frame | `more higher`, `twice more than` | BOTH |
| Present perfect for unfinished time `has risen since 2010` | the single tense that most reliably signals control | 2 — auxiliary agreement; past participle | pairing it with a finished time marker (§4.4) | BOTH |
| `-ing` participle clause for compressed cause `Facing a shortage, …` | one clause where band 6 writes two | 3 — shared subject, correct participle, comma | the dangler: the subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause | WRITTEN |

**TIER B — good value, medium risk. Teach after Tier A is automatic.**

- **Defining relative with the pronoun omitted** — `the policy the council introduced`. Reads as
  fluent control. Trap: the resumptive pronoun (`the policy that the council introduced it`).
- **Reduced relative** — `households living within walking distance of a station`. Trap: using it
  with a finite verb (`households live within…`).
- **Cleft** — `What the figures actually show is a slow recovery.` Genuinely useful once per essay
  for emphasis. Trap: it becomes a verbal tic (§3.2) and it hedges away the writer's own position
  when used for opinions.
- **`not only … but also` WITHOUT inversion** — `The scheme not only reduced waiting times but also
  cut costs.` Trap: unparallel elements either side (`not only reduced waiting times but also the
  cost was cut`). This is the safe cousin of the Tier C inversion.
- **Third conditional** — `If I had applied earlier, I would have had a choice.` Very high value in
  Speaking Part 2 (regret narratives) and low value in writing. Trap: `would have` migrating into
  the `if`-clause.
- **Past perfect for background in a narrative** — `By the time the funding arrived, most of the
  staff had already left.` Trap: over-application (§3.2 item 7).
- **Extraposed `it`** — `It is difficult to argue that the policy has failed.` Trap: dropping the
  `it` (`Is difficult to argue…`).
- **Modal + perfect for past speculation** — `The subsidy may have contributed to the rise.` High
  value in Task 1 (careful causal language) and Part 3. Trap: `may contributed`.
- **`used to` vs `would` for past habit** — `We used to walk; my father would insist on it.` Trap:
  `used to` for states in `would` slots (`I would live in Lagos` is wrong for a state).

**TIER C — costs more than it earns. Do not put these in front of a band-6 learner.**

| Structure | Why it is a trap | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Negative-adverbial inversion — `Not only does …`, `Rarely has …`, `Under no circumstances should …` | error surface 4 (fronted adverbial + auxiliary/do-support + inverted order + a matching second clause), and only two or three contexts in the whole exam suit it. Teachers report students learning the form and then forcing it in. | the Tier B `not only … but also` without inversion, or two clean sentences |
| Cleft on every paragraph | one is emphasis; four is a tic, and clefts make an opinion sound like someone else's report | one per essay maximum, in the conclusion or at a genuine pivot |
| Mandative subjunctive — `insisted that he be informed` | correct, invisible to most readers, and `insisted that he should be informed` is fully acceptable — so the marginal credit is near zero | `should` + bare infinitive, or an ordinary indicative |
| Mixed conditionals — `If she had studied medicine, she would be working abroad now` | the meaning distinction is real, but the credit over a plain second conditional is tiny and the failure mode is tense soup | pick second or third and land it |
| Inverted conditionals — `Were the government to act`, `Had they invested` | error surface 3, and it reads as memorised when it appears once in an otherwise band-6 script | `If the government acted`, `If they had invested` |
| `whom` | using it wrongly is more damaging than not using it; `who` is acceptable almost everywhere in modern English | `who`, or restructure |
| Chained nominalisation — `the implementation of the reduction of the provision of…` | grammatical, unreadable, and it costs Coherence | one nominalised subject per paragraph, then verbs |
| Any sentence over ~35 words | risk scales faster than reward; the payoff curve for "long" flattens at about 25 words while the error probability keeps climbing | split it; a 12-word sentence next to a 28-word one is *more* range than one 40-word sentence |

**The rule to ship, in our words:** *one correct complex sentence outscores three broken ones.*
And the operational corollary, which our app can actually enforce: **if you cannot produce a
structure correctly three times in a row in a drill, it is not exam-ready — leave it out.** That
turns risk tiering from advice into a measurable gate, and it is exactly the kind of thing our SRS
scheduler can own.

### 3.2 The structures learners attempt and get wrong so often that attempting them costs marks

Ranked by (frequency of attempt × probability of failure × visibility of the failure).

1. **Negative inversion.** Half-formed: `Not only the cost is high, but also it takes years.` The
   auxiliary is missing and the failure is loud because the sentence was reaching. → Two clean
   sentences, or the non-inverted `not only … but also`.
2. **Conditionals beyond the second.** `If the government would ban it, the air will be cleaner.`
   Two errors, both structural, in a sentence type that appears in nearly every solution paragraph.
   → Drill the second conditional as a *frame with two slots*, never as a rule.
3. **`Despite` / `In spite of` followed by a clause.** `Despite the cost was high…` — an extremely
   common single-token error. → `Despite the cost` / `Despite being expensive` / `Although the cost
   was high`. Teach the pair together; the choice is grammatical, not stylistic.
4. **`The reason why … is because`.** Doubly redundant and very frequent in Task 2 body paragraphs.
   → `The reason … is that`.
5. **Relative clauses with a resumptive pronoun.** `The problem which the council is trying to solve
   it has no cheap answer.` The learner has correctly built the clause and then re-supplied the
   object. → Delete the stray pronoun. One-token fix; teach it as a *deletion habit*.
6. **Past perfect used as a decorative past simple** (speaking especially). `Last year I had gone to
   Istanbul and I had visited three museums.` Nothing is being sequenced. It reads as a structure
   deployed for show, which is worse than the past simple it replaced. → Past perfect only when one
   past event must be shown as earlier than another already-mentioned one.
7. **Passive built on an intransitive verb.** `The number was increased sharply.` `The accident was
   happened.` The learner has learned "passive = advanced" and applied it where there is no object
   to promote. → Test: can the verb take an object? If not, no passive.
8. **Fronted participle clauses that dangle.** `Having introduced the charge, congestion fell by a
   fifth.` The congestion did not introduce the charge. → Match the subjects, or use a full clause.
9. **`Not only … but also` with unparallel elements.** → Make both sides the same grammatical shape.
10. **`Both … and` / `either … or` scope errors.** `Both the cost and it takes time.` → Same fix.

Every item here is a candidate for an explicit **anti-pattern card** in the module: the broken
version, why the learner built it, the smallest repair, and the safe substitute. Teaching only the
correct form leaves the broken form in place, because the broken form was produced by a *plausible*
reasoning step that has to be named and interrupted.

## 4. The highest-frequency errors, ranked

**How this ranking was built, honestly.** No single authoritative frequency table for IELTS-style
scripts exists. This synthesises three things: (a) large-corpus learner-error evidence — the
Cambridge Learner Corpus (~130,000 scripts, ~29m words, ~80 error codes) is consistently reported
as putting **word choice, prepositions and determiners at the top after spelling**, with
determiners alone around a tenth of coded errors at upper-intermediate level, and secondary
reporting of the Cambridge English Corpus puts articles, verb tense, subject–verb agreement,
prepositions and homophones together at roughly three-quarters of all errors; (b) the IELTS-specific
corpus study cited in the writing briefing (70 essays, 589 coded errors: word choice ~24%, verb form
~18%); (c) our own weighting toward errors that are **visible to an examiner and fixable in a week**.
It is an informed synthesis, not a measurement. Say so in learner copy if we ever publish the order.

For each: the error, an authored wrong→right pair, **why it happens** (this is the part that
determines the drill type), and **the smallest correction that fixes it** — a one-token edit the
learner can rehearse, not a rule they have to reason from.

| # | Error | Authored wrong → right | Why it happens | Smallest correction | Hits | G/L |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Articles / determiners** | `Government should fund research into renewable energy.` → `Governments should fund…` or `The government should…` · `In the modern society, the technology changes fast.` → `In modern society, technology changes fast.` | L1 transfer above all: Russian, Polish, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai and Turkish have no article system, so there is no L1 slot for the decision. Then over-correction: once `the` is taught, it goes everywhere. | Insert or delete **one word**. Three rules only: (a) a singular countable noun always needs a determiner; (b) generalisations take bare plural or bare uncountable; (c) `the` once the thing has been identified, and with superlatives and ordinals. | GRA | L |
| 2 | **Subject–verb agreement across distance** | `The number of applicants have fallen.` → `has fallen.` · `One of the main causes are cost.` → `is cost.` | The verb agrees with the nearest noun instead of the head noun. It is a *processing* error, not a knowledge error — which is why it survives long after the rule is known. | Change **one letter**. Head-noun trick: cover everything between the head and the verb, then read it. | GRA | L |
| 3 | **Verb form and tense choice** (incl. present perfect vs past simple) | `I have visited Berlin in 2019.` → `I visited Berlin in 2019.` · `Since 2015 the rate dropped.` → `Since 2015 the rate has dropped.` | The learner knows both forms and has no working model of *time reference*. Many L1s map tense–aspect differently, so the English distinction has no anchor. | Look at the **time expression** and let it decide. Finished time word → past simple. `since` / `for` / `so far` / `over the last decade` / no time word at all → present perfect. | GRA (can go global) | L→G |
| 4 | **Countability and quantifiers** | `The report contains many useful informations and several advices.` → `…a great deal of useful information and several pieces of advice.` · `less people` → `fewer people` | The countable/uncountable boundary is arbitrary and language-specific; nothing in the meaning tells you `advice` is uncountable. It is lexical knowledge disguised as grammar. | Swap **one word** (the quantifier) or delete **one `s`**. Ship the uncountable list **with its counter phrase**: `a piece of advice`, `an item of equipment`, `a body of research`. | GRA + LR | L |
| 5 | **Prepositions (dependent)** | `They discussed about the impact to local businesses.` → `They discussed the impact on local businesses.` · `a solution of the problem` → `a solution to the problem` | Not rule-governed. Prepositions are stored with the word they attach to, so a learner who learned the word without its preposition has nothing to retrieve. Compounded by direct L1 translation. | Swap or delete **one word**. Learn inside the chunk, never from a table — this is an SRS card, not a grammar lesson. | LR (mostly) | L |
| 6 | **Sentence boundaries: run-ons, comma splices, fragments** | `Cities are growing fast, this puts pressure on housing.` → `…fast. This puts…` / `…fast, and this puts…` / `…fast; this puts…` · `Because more people work from home. Traffic has fallen.` → `Because more people work from home, traffic has fallen.` | Two causes, and they need different fixes. (i) In many L1s a comma legitimately joins clauses, so the splice is transferred. (ii) The fragment appears when a learner fronts a subordinate clause and then punctuates it as a sentence — an *over-reach* error, produced by trying to be complex. | Change **one punctuation mark**. The test to teach: *if both sides could stand alone as sentences, a comma alone is not enough.* Three legal repairs: full stop, semicolon, comma + coordinator. | GRA + CC | **G** |
| 7 | **Word form / part of speech** | `The economical growth was depend on foreign investment.` → `Economic growth depended on foreign investment.` · `The government should take responsible.` → `…take responsibility.` | The learner knows the *stem* and guesses the suffix. English derivational morphology is irregular, and some pairs (`economic`/`economical`) are a meaning distinction rather than a spelling one. | Change **one suffix**. Learn word families in fours: `success · succeed · successful · successfully`. Build these as vocabulary cards. | LR (felt as GRA) | L |
| 8 | **Tense consistency across a paragraph** | `The chart shows visitor numbers from 2000 to 2020. Numbers rise until 2008, then they fell sharply, and after 2015 they are recovering.` → `…rose until 2008, then fell sharply, and after 2015 they recovered.` | Not a rule failure — a *monitoring* failure. Each verb was chosen locally and nobody checked the paragraph as a unit. It gets worse in the second half of a timed task, when monitoring capacity is gone. | Change **one verb ending**, usually. The habit, not the rule: write the intended tense at the top of the plan, and in the proofread pass read **only the verbs**, in order. | GRA + CC | L (G in aggregate) |
| 9 | **Relative pronoun choice and resumptive pronouns** | `Students which live far from campus, they spend two hours travelling.` → `Students who live far from campus spend two hours travelling.` | `which`/`who`/`that` is a small rule that is simply not automatic. The resumptive pronoun is L1 transfer — several languages require the pronoun that English forbids. | Change **one word** (`which`→`who`) and delete **one word** (the stray pronoun). | GRA | L→G |
| 10 | **Conditional mixing** | `If the council would reduce fares, more people will use the buses.` → `If the council reduced fares, more people would use the buses.` | Two rules collide: `will/would` feels like the marker of the unreal, so it gets copied into both clauses. Reinforced by L1s that do mark the conditional on both verbs. | Move **one word**. `would` lives in the main clause only. Drill as a frame with two slots. | GRA | L→G |
| 11 | **Word order — adverbs, and embedded questions** | `I know always the answer.` → `I always know the answer.` · `Nobody knows why did the scheme fail.` → `Nobody knows why the scheme failed.` | Adverb placement is genuinely different across languages. The embedded-question error is over-generalisation: the learner applies question inversion in a clause that is no longer a question. | Move **one word**. The embedded rule: after `why / how / whether / what`, use statement order. | GRA | L→**G** |
| 12 | **Verb patterns** (`suggest`, `allow`, `enable`, `make`, `let`, `recommend`) | `I suggest to build more housing and to allow that people rent it cheaply.` → `I suggest building more housing and allowing people to rent it cheaply.` | Lexical, not rule-driven: which verb takes `-ing`, which takes `to`, which takes an object first. Nothing predicts it. | Swap **one form**. Store the whole frame as a chunk: `suggest + -ing`, `allow somebody to`, `enable somebody to`, `make somebody do`. SRS material. | GRA + LR | L |
| 13 | **Plurals, possessives and apostrophes** | `Many student's struggle with it's cost.` → `Many students struggle with its cost.` | Three different `-s`/apostrophe functions collapse into one. Aggravated by the fact that speech does not distinguish them. | Delete or move **one apostrophe**. Three-way sort drill: plural / possessive / contraction. | GRA | L |
| 14 | **Modal misuse** | `Governments must to act, and citizens should to help.` → `Governments must act, and citizens should help.` · `Students must study harder` where advice is meant → `Students should study harder` | Form error (`must to`) is over-generalisation from `have to`/`ought to`. Meaning error (`must` for advice) is a strength-of-claim problem, and it is the one that costs Task Response by over-generalising. | Delete **one word** (`to`), or swap **one modal**. Teach modality as a strength scale, not as a list. | GRA + TR | L |

### 4.1 The three causes, and why they dictate three different drill types

Naming the cause is not academic — it determines what practice works. This should drive the
module's exercise-type selection and it is the direct link to the SRS design:

| Cause | Which errors | What actually fixes it | Exercise kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L1 transfer** — the L1 has no slot, or a differently shaped slot | articles, resumptive pronouns, comma splices, adverb order, countability boundaries | **noticing + contrast.** The learner must first see that a decision exists. Minimal pairs where only the disputed element changes, with the meaning difference made visible. | discrimination pairs; "which of these two is right, and what changes?" |
| **Over-generalisation** — a rule was learned and is being applied too widely | `must to`, embedded question inversion, past perfect as decorative past, passive on intransitives, `the` everywhere | **boundary drills.** Not more of the rule — the *edge* of the rule. Items deliberately placed just outside it. | sort-into-two-boxes; "does the rule apply here?" |
| **Chunk ignorance** — the item is lexical and simply not known | dependent prepositions, verb patterns, word-form families, uncountable nouns | **spaced retrieval of the whole chunk**, in a sentence. Never the bare word, never the bare rule. | cloze inside a real sentence; use-in-sentence; collocation cards |

The third row is the hand-off to the vocabulary module: **items 5, 7 and 12 in §4 are vocabulary
problems and belong in the SRS bank, not in a grammar lesson.** That is a concrete recommendation
for the design agent — roughly a fifth of the "grammar" a learner needs is actually chunk knowledge
and should ride on the existing FSRS scheduler with `collocation` and `cloze` exercise kinds.

## 5. Common errors that are NOT heavily penalised

Attention is the scarce resource. Every hour spent here is an hour not spent on §4 items 1–6. This
list exists to be *deleted from* the learner's worry set, and the module should say so explicitly —
a learner who is anxious about the Oxford comma and relaxed about comma splices has it exactly
backwards.

Ranked by how much learner attention the myth currently wastes:

1. **British vs American spelling.** Both accepted. Consistency reads better; mixing is not a
   published penalty (§10.7). Not worth a lesson — worth one line.
2. **The serial/Oxford comma.** Optional. Never scored either way.
3. **"Never split an infinitive", "never end a sentence with a preposition", "never begin with
   `And`/`But`/`Because`."** Not rules of English. `Because` can open a sentence provided the main
   clause follows — the *fragment* is the error, not the word. Teaching these as rules actively
   produces the fragment error in §4 item 6.
4. **`which` vs `that` in defining relative clauses.** Both are standard in British English. The
   thing that *is* scored is the comma (defining vs non-defining), which is a different question.
5. **Contractions in speaking** — correct, and their absence sounds memorised. In a friendly GT
   letter — correct, and their absence sounds wrong. Only in the essay and the formal letter are
   they a slip. Our error-detection copy must flag them **by register**, never globally.
6. **`whom`.** Optional in nearly all contexts. Using it wrongly is worse than not using it.
7. **"Too much passive" as a stylistic sin.** Not scored. What *is* scored is using the wrong voice
   for the genre — active narration of an industrial process, or passive throughout a personal
   Part 2 answer.
8. **A single instance of anything.** The scale is about pattern and control. One missing third-person
   `-s` in 280 words is invisible; the same error eight times is a band. Teach learners to hunt
   **their own repeat offenders**, not to read for errors in general — which finds nothing.
9. **`I` in a Task 2 opinion essay.** Not a slip. A clear position is required. What is worth
   teaching is *varying* the stance marker, not avoiding the first person.
10. **Fillers and discourse markers in speech** — `well`, `I mean`, `you know`, `actually`. Natural
    spoken English and not grammar errors. Only *mid-clause stalling* costs, and it costs Fluency,
    not GRA.
11. **Informal spoken forms that are non-standard but idiomatic** — `there's two things`, `less
    people` in speech. Real speakers produce them constantly. Not worth a band-6 learner's attention
    while §4 items 1–6 are open.
12. **Pronunciation of grammatical endings** (`-ed`, `-s`). Real, and scored under Pronunciation,
    not GRA. It belongs in the speaking module, not this one — but the module should say where it
    went, so the learner does not think we forgot it.

**The line to ship:** *these are not "correct" — they are cheap. Spend your attention where the
density is.*

## 6. Which structures earn marks where

The module's connective tissue. A grammar point is worth learning because it appears in a specific
place in a specific task; every teaching point should name at least one. All model sentences below
are authored for BandReady.

### 6.1 Academic Task 1 — chart, table, process, map

| Function the task forces | Structure | Authored model | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| describe a manufacturing/technical process | **agentless passive**, present simple | `The pulp is screened for contaminants before it is pressed into sheets.` | passive on an intransitive verb; switching to `they do X` mid-process |
| sequence the stages | passive + sequence adverbials | `Once the sheets have dried, they are trimmed to size.` | tense drift between stages |
| state a trend over past dates | past simple + adverbs of degree | `Enrolment fell sharply between 2004 and 2009.` | present simple because "the chart shows" is present |
| state a level vs a change | `by` vs `to` | `Output rose **by** 12% **to** 340 tonnes.` | the near-universal `by`/`to` swap |
| compare two series | comparative + `whereas` / `while` | `Rural clinics lost a third of their staff, whereas urban ones grew slightly.` | double comparatives |
| express proportion | `accounted for`, `made up`, fractions | `Housing accounted for just under a third of the total throughout.` | `occupied 30%` |
| describe a projection | `is expected to` / `is projected to` | `Demand is projected to level off after 2030.` | `will` stated as fact |
| describe a map change | passive + position language | `The southern car park was replaced by a landscaped square.` | active voice with an invented agent |
| overview sentence | present perfect or past simple + superlative/ordinal | `Overall, the two categories moved in opposite directions.` | putting figures in the overview |

**Grammar headline for Task 1: the passive is not decoration here, it is the register.** And the
tense is decided by the *data's dates*, not by the chart.

### 6.2 General Training Task 1 — letters

| Function | Structure | Authored model | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| polite request (formal) | `I would be grateful if you could …` / `I would appreciate it if …` | `I would be grateful if you could confirm the revised date in writing.` | `I want you to…` |
| indirect question | statement order after the wh-word | `I would like to know whether the deposit is refundable.` | `I would like to know is the deposit refundable.` |
| complaint without aggression | passive + hedged modality | `The room had evidently not been cleaned before we arrived.` | flat accusation in the active |
| offer / suggestion (semi-formal) | `Would it be possible to …?` / `I wondered whether …` | `I wondered whether Thursday might suit you better.` | `Can you…?` in a formal letter |
| informal warmth | contractions, phrasal verbs, `going to` | `I've been meaning to write for ages — we're finally moving in June.` | Latinate formality to a friend |
| explaining a past reason | past perfect for background | `By the time I called, the office had already closed for the week.` | past perfect everywhere |

**Grammar headline for GT Task 1: register is half grammatical.** The contraction, the modal, the
phrasal-vs-Latinate verb and the directness of the request are all form choices, and they are the
fastest thing to lose a band on.

### 6.3 Task 2 — the essay

| Function | Structure | Authored model | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| concede then hold your position | fronted `While …, …` | `While congestion charging is undeniably regressive, the alternative distributes the cost even less fairly.` | the double connector `Although…, but…` |
| hedge a generalisation | `tend to`, `is likely to`, `in most cases`, `broadly` | `Households on lower incomes tend to spend a larger share of income on essentials.` | dropping the hedge and over-claiming — the named band-7 residue |
| speculate about a solution | **second conditional** | `If tuition were funded from general taxation, participation would almost certainly widen.` | `would` in the `if`-clause |
| speculate about a cause in the past | modal + perfect | `The subsidy may well have accelerated a decline that was already underway.` | `may accelerated` |
| depersonalise a claim | agentless passive | `Recycling targets have been revised downwards twice.` | inventing an agent to avoid the passive |
| recommend | modality by strength: `must` > `should` > `could` | `Regulators should require disclosure; they could go further and cap the fee outright.` | `must` for everything, which reads as shouting and over-generalises |
| add a comment without a new sentence | non-defining `, which …` | `Fees were introduced in 2011, which coincided with the first fall in applications for a generation.` | unclosed comma pair |
| contrast two groups | `whereas` | `Older workers value security, whereas younger ones consistently rank flexibility first.` | `whereas` used for time |
| emphasise a pivot | cleft (once) | `What makes the policy unpopular is not its cost but its visibility.` | one per essay; more is a tic |
| compress a result | `, leaving/making/allowing …` | `Demand outstripped supply for six years, leaving waiting lists twice as long.` | dangling participle |
| define scope precisely | reduced relative / prepositional post-modification | `Households living more than five kilometres from a station drove a third more.` | finite verb inside the reduced clause |

**Grammar headline for Task 2: conditionals and modality are not "advanced grammar" here — they
are the argument.** An essay that cannot speculate cannot answer a `what should be done` prompt,
and an essay that cannot hedge over-claims its way out of band 7 on Task Response.

### 6.4 Speaking Part 1 — short personal answers

| Function | Structure | Authored model | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| habit and general truth | present simple + frequency adverbs | `I usually cycle in, unless it's raining.` | adverb placement (§4 item 11) |
| current temporary situation | present continuous | `I'm working from home three days a week at the moment.` | the simple/continuous contrast is a "when to use which" point, not a form point |
| experience up to now | present perfect | `I've never really got into cooking.` | this is where present perfect vs past simple pays first |
| past habit | `used to` | `I used to play a lot more before the job.` | `would` for states |
| preference with a reason | `because` / `since` + comparative | `I prefer trains, mainly because you can actually do something with the time.` | one clause only is a band-6 tell |

Part 1's grammar payoff is small but the **cost of getting it wrong is high**, because Part 1 is
where the examiner forms a first estimate. Two structures reliably produced beat five attempted.

### 6.5 Speaking Part 2 — the long turn

This is the **narrative tenses** slot and it is the clearest structure-to-task mapping in the whole
exam. Four tenses, deployed deliberately across two minutes:

| Tense | Job in the story | Authored model |
|---|---|---|
| past simple | the spine — the events, in order | `I applied on the Friday and heard back the following week.` |
| past continuous | the scene the events interrupt | `We were still unpacking when the call came through.` |
| past perfect | anything that happened *before* the spine | `I had already turned down two other offers, which made the decision harder.` |
| past perfect continuous | duration before a past point | `I'd been commuting for nearly two hours a day, and it had stopped being sustainable.` |

Plus, at the end of the turn, the **reflective shift**: `Looking back, I'd probably do the same
again` / `It's the sort of thing you only appreciate afterwards` — a move out of narrative into
present evaluation, which demonstrates range across time frames in one answer. Traps: past perfect
used decoratively (§3.2 item 6), and the whole two minutes delivered in the past simple.

Not every cue card is a story. For **description** cue cards, the payoff structures are relative
clauses and comparatives; for **explanation** cue cards, purpose and result clauses.

### 6.6 Speaking Part 3 — abstract and hypothetical

The heaviest grammar load in the exam, and the one candidates prepare least.

| Question type | Structure it demands | Authored model |
|---|---|---|
| speculation about the future | `is likely to`, `will probably`, `could well` | `Remote work is likely to keep spreading, though probably not as fast as people expected.` |
| hypothetical present/future | **second conditional** | `If cars were priced off city centres, I think the retail side would suffer before the traffic improved.` |
| hypothetical past / consequence | third conditional or modal + perfect | `If that funding hadn't arrived, the whole scheme would have stalled.` |
| generalisation about people/society | bare plural + `tend to` | `Younger workers tend to change jobs far more readily than my parents' generation did.` |
| comparison across time | present perfect + `whereas` / `used to` | `Attitudes have shifted a lot: people used to expect a job for life, whereas now nobody plans past five years.` |
| concession | `Although` / `Admittedly …, but` | `Admittedly it costs more up front, but the running costs are lower.` |
| cause and effect | `which means`, `so that`, `leads to` | `Housing costs push people further out, which means longer commutes and less time at home.` |
| evaluation under uncertainty | modal + perfect, hedged | `It may have had more to do with the economy than with the policy itself.` |

**Grammar headline for Part 3: `would` is the single most load-bearing word in the exam.** Every
hypothetical question is answerable with a second conditional, and a candidate who cannot build one
under time pressure will retreat to the present simple and cap the answer at band 6.

### 6.7 Reading and Listening — the grammar that pays receptively (nobody teaches this)

Neither test scores grammar productively, so it looks irrelevant. It is not: several question types
are **decided by a grammatical distinction**, and a learner who cannot parse the structure cannot
get the mark no matter how good their vocabulary is. This is a cheap, distinctive addition to our
module and an obvious "gap the owner didn't name".

| Where it pays | The grammar | Why it decides the answer |
|---|---|---|
| True / False / Not Given | **hedged modality and quantifier scope** — `may`, `some`, `most`, `is likely to` vs `is` | The commonest FALSE trap is a statement that strengthens a hedged claim; the commonest NOT GIVEN is a claim the text hedges *differently*. This is grammar, not reading speed. |
| True / False / Not Given | **defining relative clauses** | `Students who completed the course found work` and `Students, who completed the course, found work` have different truth conditions. Test writers exploit this. |
| Matching headings / information | **nominalisation and passive paraphrase** | Question stems nominalise what the passage verbalises (`prices rose` → `the rise in prices`) and passivise what the passage actives. Recognising the same proposition in two shapes *is* the skill. |
| Summary / sentence completion | **word class from the slot** | The gap's grammar tells you whether you need a noun, an adjective or a gerund before you have understood the content — which halves the search space. |
| Any completion task | **negation scope** — `not only`, `rarely`, `few` vs `a few`, `hardly` | `few applicants` and `a few applicants` point at opposite answers. |
| Listening form/note completion | **tense and number of the spoken sentence** | Distractors are usually corrections spoken later (`actually, make that three`); the tense and the correction marker are the signal. |
| Listening matching | **referencing chains** — `the latter`, `that one`, `hers` | Speakers substitute rather than repeat; a learner who cannot resolve the pro-form loses the mapping. |
| Reading comparatives | **comparative and superlative forms** | `no fewer than`, `twice as many`, `second only to` — small closed set, decisive in matching-information items. |

## 7. What "range" means concretely at band 7+, in a 250-word essay

The descriptor says "a variety of complex structures". Learners hear "long sentences". Here is the
concrete version, written so an author can turn it into a checklist and so our app can measure it.

**No published count exists** and we must not invent one (§10.1). What follows is a described
*profile*, not a threshold.

A band-7 250–290-word essay is typically 12–15 sentences. What distinguishes it from a band-6 essay
of the same length:

1. **Four to six different subordination types across the essay** — not six copies of one. A band-6
   essay often has six subordinate clauses and they are all `because` or all `which`. The count is
   the same; the *variety* is not. **This is the actual meaning of "range" and it is countable.**
2. **At least one non-finite clause** — a participle clause, a reduced relative, or an infinitive
   of purpose. Non-finite clauses are the clearest single marker that a writer is compressing
   rather than chaining.
3. **At least one passive used for a reason** — to keep the paragraph's topic in subject position,
   or because the agent genuinely does not matter. Not a passive dropped in to look academic.
4. **Two or more hedged generalisations.** They are grammar and they double as the Task Response fix.
5. **One concession–rebuttal pair**, usually a fronted `While …, …` — this is the sentence that
   most reliably reads as "band 7" to a marker, because it does two jobs at once.
6. **One explicit comparison or contrast structure** (`whereas`, `far more … than`, `unlike`).
7. **Deliberate sentence-length variance.** Band 6 is often uniform — everything between 15 and 22
   words. Band 7 puts a 7-word sentence next to a 28-word one *on purpose*. A short sentence after a
   long one reads as control, not as simplicity.
8. **Varied sentence openings.** The single most countable band-6 tell: 10 of 13 sentences start
   with the grammatical subject. Band 7 opens sentences with a subordinate clause, a fronted
   adverbial, a participle, a prepositional phrase. **Our app can count this and show it.**
9. **One nominalised subject at most per paragraph**, then verbs. Nominalisation is range; a chain
   of it is unreadable.
10. **Modality that varies in strength** across the essay rather than `should` eleven times.

### 7.1 A worked range map of a four-paragraph essay

This is directly implementable as a product feature — the "range map" of the learner's own draft,
compared against the target profile. Roles, not sentences:

| Paragraph | Sentence | Structural job | Structure that does it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intro | 1 | reframe the question | nominalised subject + present simple |
| | 2 | thesis | `While X, Y` concession, or a cleft |
| Body 1 | 3 | topic sentence — a claim | simple or one-clause complex; **short on purpose** |
| | 4 | mechanism | `because` / `which means` / result participle |
| | 5 | consequence | comparative or `so that` |
| | 6 | support | reduced relative or non-defining `, which` |
| | 7 | qualification | hedge: `tend to` / `in most cases` |
| Body 2 | 8 | topic sentence — the counter or second reason | fronted `Although …, …` |
| | 9 | mechanism | passive, agent dropped |
| | 10 | speculation | **second conditional** |
| | 11 | support / limit | `unless` or `whereas` |
| Conclusion | 12 | restate the position | modality at chosen strength |
| | 13 | close | short simple sentence — the contrast in length is the point |

Thirteen sentences; eight distinct structure types; one of everything in §7's list. **Nothing in
that map is above C1, and none of it is Tier C.** That is the entire argument of this briefing in
one table: band 7 range is achievable with safe structures placed deliberately.

### 7.2 The anti-pattern: range by decoration

Adding a `Moreover`, a `plethora` and an inverted `Not only` to a band-6 essay produces a band-6
essay with three new errors. The test to ship, and it is a good one because a learner can run it on
their own draft:

> **Delete the structure and see whether the meaning changes.** If the sentence means exactly the
> same thing without it, it was decoration, and decoration is where errors come from.

Range that survives that test was chosen because the *meaning* needed it. That is what the
descriptor's word "flexibly" is actually pointing at.

## 8. Errors that block meaning vs errors that merely mark a script as inaccurate

The distinction is old and well established in error analysis — **global** errors damage the
structure of the sentence or the relations between its clauses, so the reader cannot recover the
intended meaning; **local** errors sit inside one constituent and leave the meaning fully
recoverable. Research on error gravity consistently finds that raters hierarchise errors rather
than counting them, though the hierarchies are not universal and vary with the rater's background
(§10.4). We use it as a **teaching-order heuristic**, not as a claim about how the band is computed.

### 8.1 Global — teach first

These cost twice: once under GRA, once under Coherence, because the reader has to stop and rebuild.

1. **Sentence-boundary failure** — a comma splice or run-on that fuses two propositions, or a
   fragment that strands one. The reader loses the argument, not just the sentence.
2. **Missing or wrong main verb** — `The main problem the cost of housing.`
3. **Clause-order / word-order breakdown** — `Is important that the government the funding
   increases.`
4. **Wrong logical connector** — `Although the scheme was cheap, it saved money.` Every word is
   correct English; the relation asserted is the opposite of the one intended. Devastating and
   almost never taught, because it is not "an error" in any single word.
5. **Relative clause attached to the wrong noun** — `The report on the schools which was published
   last week…` (which was published, the report or the schools?). Changes who did what.
6. **Negation scope errors** — `All the proposals were not accepted.` Ambiguous between none and
   some.
7. **Pronoun with no findable antecedent** — `This has made it worse.` after a paragraph containing
   four candidate referents. Scored under Coherence; caused by grammar.
8. **Conditional tense pattern that makes real vs unreal unreadable** — the reader cannot tell
   whether the writer is proposing something or reporting it.
9. **Tense that contradicts a stated time reference** — `Since 2010 the rate dropped by half`
   contradicts itself; `In 2010 the rate has dropped` more so.
10. **Passive on an intransitive verb** — `The recession was happened in 2008.` The sentence has no
    coherent argument structure left.

### 8.2 Local — teach second, by frequency

Articles. Third-person `-s`. Plural `-s`. Preposition choice. Countability. Comparative form.
Article with a superlative. Spelling of an inflection. Punctuation of a non-defining clause.
Apostrophes. Adverb placement (usually).

### 8.3 The asymmetry that decides the sequence

> **Global errors are cheap to eliminate and expensive to keep. Local errors are individually cheap
> to keep and expensive to eliminate — but eliminating them is exactly what gets you past 6.5.**

Both halves matter and they imply different things:

- Global errors are **few in number, rule-shaped, and teachable in days**. A learner can stop
  producing comma splices in a week. Keeping them caps the script, because "errors that cause the
  reader difficulty" is precisely how the band-5 line is described. **So global errors come first
  in the sequence — always.**
- Local errors are **many, partly lexical, and the work of months**. They do not individually cost
  much. But *density* is what the band-6/7 boundary is drawn on, and density is made of local
  errors. So the module cannot skip them; it must schedule them — which is the argument for putting
  them in the SRS rather than in lessons (§4.1).
- **In speaking this becomes a live decision.** Repair a global error mid-answer; let a local one
  go. Stopping to fix a missing article costs more in Fluency than the article was worth in GRA.
  Ship this as an explicit exam-strategy rule, because it is counter-intuitive and learners do the
  opposite by default.

A useful diagnostic for the app: **a script whose errors are mostly global is a band-5 script
regardless of its error count; a script whose errors are all local is a band-6 script regardless of
its error count.** The mix, not the total, is the signal — and our evaluator already annotates
errors by type, so it can compute this.

## 9. What this implies for the module (recommendation to the design agent)

Not the design agent's decision to make, but this briefing exists to inform it, so:

1. **Sequence by payoff and dependency, not by tradition.** §0 is the payoff order; the design
   agent must reconcile it with the zero-knowledge dependency order (nothing taught before its
   prerequisites). Where they conflict, the dependency order wins and the payoff order decides
   *how much time* each point gets.
2. **Every teaching point should carry, as structured fields:**
   `when_to_use` (meaning-first, before any form) · `contrast_with` (its nearest rival — this is
   where "when to use which" lives) · `register` (SPOKEN / WRITTEN / BOTH) · `risk_tier`
   (A / B / C from §3.1) · `error_surface` (an integer) · `pays_in` (which task/part, from §6) ·
   `criteria` (which criteria it moves, from §1) · `trap` (the single commonest failure) ·
   `gravity` (global / local, from §8, which sets correction priority).
3. **"When to use which" must be its own item type, not a footnote on a form.** The owner named it
   twice and it is the hard part. The decisions that matter most, ranked:
   1. present perfect vs past simple (the time-reference decision)
   2. which conditional — reframed as *how likely do I think this is?*, not as a numbered list
   3. active vs passive — reframed as *what is this sentence about?*
   4. `must` / `should` / `could` / `might` / `tends to` — strength of claim, and assert vs hedge
   5. defining vs non-defining relative — the comma changes the meaning
   6. `will` vs `going to` vs present continuous for the future — evidence and arrangement
   7. present simple vs present continuous — permanent vs temporary, and the state-verb boundary
   8. `used to` vs `would` vs past simple — habit vs state vs single event
   9. `a` vs `the` vs zero for generic reference — the three ways English generalises
   10. `-ing` vs `to` after a verb — lexical, so it is an SRS item, and saying so is part of the
       teaching
4. **Three of the fourteen top items are vocabulary problems** (dependent prepositions, verb
   patterns, word families) and should ship as SRS cards with sentence-level cloze, not as lessons.
   That is the concrete grammar↔vocabulary bridge.
5. **Drills must reach production speed, not just accuracy** (§2.3 item 2 and §3.1's three-in-a-row
   gate). A recognition-only grammar module will not move a speaking band.
6. **Anti-pattern cards are as important as teaching cards** (§3.2). The broken form was produced by
   a plausible reasoning step; naming that step is what stops it.

## 10. Where the evidence is thin — be honest about this in our copy

1. **No published threshold exists for "how many error-free sentences make a band 7."** Coaching
   sources offer figures (one widely repeated claim is ~80% of sentences; another prescribes 6–7
   complex sentences in a 12–13-sentence essay). None of these is published by the test partners.
   **We describe the property qualitatively and never quote a percentage or a count.** §7 is
   deliberately written as a profile, not a threshold.
2. **Error-frequency rankings differ by corpus, by exam level, and by first language.** §4's order
   is our informed synthesis (method stated at the top of §4), not a measurement. Any learner-facing
   presentation should say "commonest in our experience", not "the most common errors are".
3. **Claims of the form "Cambridge examiner training says X"** appear on several commercial pages
   (we saw it asserted for passive-voice selection in Task 1). We could not verify any of them
   against a published source and **none of them is used as authority in this file.** The
   passive-in-processes point stands on its own as a register fact, not on that claim.
4. **Error-gravity hierarchies are not universal.** Published reviews find they vary with the
   rater's background, discipline and whether the rater is a native speaker. We use global/local as
   a *teaching-order* heuristic and must not present it as how examiners score.
5. **The speaking-vs-writing tolerance difference is coaching consensus, not published policy.** We
   may give the mechanical reason (speech is assessed once, in real time; a script can be re-read)
   and must not claim different published thresholds.
6. **"Man-made processes take the passive, natural processes take the active"** is a serviceable
   heuristic and not a rule — natural-process descriptions frequently take the passive too
   (`the water is drawn up through the roots`). Teach it as a default with the reason (focus on the
   process, not the agent), so the learner can override it.
7. **Mixing British and American spelling** is not a published penalty; consistency is a signal of
   reliable spelling, not a rule.
8. **Self-correction in speaking** is not itself penalised, but frequent correction damages Fluency.
   The "repair global, ignore local" rule in §8.3 is our synthesis of that, and it is coaching
   consensus rather than published guidance.
9. **The "78% of errors in five categories" figure** attributed to the Cambridge English Corpus
   reaches us through secondary reporting; we could not open the primary Cambridge page (HTTP 403)
   and should treat the number as indicative. The safer, better-attested claim is the CLC ordering:
   after spelling, **word choice, prepositions and determiners** carry the highest proportions.
