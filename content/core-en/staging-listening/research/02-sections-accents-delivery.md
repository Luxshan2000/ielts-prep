| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[OFFICIAL]** | Stated by ielts.org, British Council, IDP, or Cambridge English. Treat as fact. |
| **[CONVERGENT]** | Not on an official page, but stated consistently by several independent, competent teaching sources and consistent with published practice material. Safe to author against. |
| **[DERIVED]** | Computed or measured by me from other facts in this document. Arithmetic is shown. |
| **[CONTESTED]** | Sources disagree, or the only sources are low-quality SEO pages. **Do not build on this.** Flagged explicitly so nobody quietly promotes it to fact. |

**The one thing to keep in your head while reading.** Everything in §1–§4 describes a paper whose
audio plays **once**. The exam's design intent is not "did you understand the passage" — the reading
paper already tests that. It is "can you catch a specific item of information, in real time, at the
moment it goes past, while a speaker is actively making it hard for you." That is why the situation
inventory in §4 matters so much: each situation type carries with it a *characteristic set of things
that can be asked* and a *characteristic distractor move*. Choosing the situation is 60% of writing
the item.

### 2.0 The two axes of escalation

The paper escalates on **two independent axes**, and conflating them is the classic authoring error:

- **Axis A — register / social context.** Parts 1–2 are *everyday, social, transactional*.
  Parts 3–4 are *educational and training*. This axis flips **once**, between Part 2 and Part 3.
  **[OFFICIAL]**
- **Axis B — number of voices.** Parts 1 and 3 are *conversations*; Parts 2 and 4 are *monologues*.
  This axis alternates: dialogue, monologue, dialogue, monologue. **[OFFICIAL]**

So the difficulty curve is not a straight ramp. It is a lattice:

```
              DIALOGUE                    MONOLOGUE
EVERYDAY   ┌──────────────────┐      ┌──────────────────┐
           │ PART 1           │      │ PART 2           │
           │ 2 speakers       │      │ 1 speaker        │
           │ transactional    │ ───▶ │ informational    │
           │ concrete data    │      │ layout / options │
           └──────────────────┘      └──────────────────┘
                                              │
                                              ▼   (register flip)
ACADEMIC   ┌──────────────────┐      ┌──────────────────┐
           │ PART 3           │      │ PART 4           │
           │ 2–4 speakers     │      │ 1 speaker        │
           │ evaluative       │ ───▶ │ expository       │
           │ opinion & stance │      │ dense, no reset  │
           └──────────────────┘      └──────────────────┘
```

The learner-facing consequence: **Part 3 is hard for a different reason than Part 4 is hard.**
Part 3 punishes you for losing track of *who thinks what*. Part 4 punishes you for losing your
*place*. A learner who is weak at Part 3 needs stance-tracking practice; a learner weak at Part 4
needs note-structure and recovery practice. Our drills must not treat "Parts 3–4 are the hard ones"
as a single diagnosis.

### 2.1 Part 1 — transactional everyday conversation (Q1–10)

**Content.** A conversation between **two speakers** in an everyday social context — officially
exemplified as "a conversation about travel arrangements." **[OFFICIAL]** In practice, overwhelmingly
one of two shapes:

1. **The service transaction.** A member of the public and a person behind a counter/phone. One
   party wants something (a booking, a place on a course, a replacement card, a quote); the other
   collects or supplies details. Very often explicitly framed as a **telephone call** — which is a
   convenient authoring device, because on the phone it is natural to spell names, repeat numbers,
   and read information back. **[CONVERGENT]**
2. **The peer-planning chat.** Two friends/colleagues arranging something jointly (Cambridge 20's
   Test 1 opener is described publicly as restaurant recommendations; a 2026-era practice publisher's
   Part 1 is two friends and a food festival). This shape is less form-shaped and leans on dates,
   prices and preferences. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Speakers.** Two. Almost always one male + one female, so voice identity alone disambiguates who
is speaking — the exam does not test speaker discrimination in Part 1. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Register.** Semi-formal to informal. Full of the machinery of service English: *"Can I take your
name?" / "Bear with me a moment." / "Let me just check that."* Hesitation, backchannelling
(*mm-hm, right, OK*), and — critically — **read-backs and confirmations**, which is where the
distractors live.

**What is asked.** Predominantly **form / note / table completion**: name, surname spelling, address,
postcode, phone, email, date, time, price, membership number, reference code, quantity, duration.
Occasionally a short-answer or a small multiple choice near the end. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Answer character.** Concrete and *closed*: proper nouns, numbers, dates, times, money, and single
common nouns. This is the only part where a large share of answers are things you have to *spell
from dictation* rather than *recognise from vocabulary*. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Difficulty position.** Officially the entry point; described by teaching sources as "designed to
be the 'easiest' part." **[CONVERGENT]** But this is precisely why it is the highest-yield part to
coach: the items are easy to *hear* and easy to *lose*. Every mark dropped in Part 1 is dropped to
spelling, to a word-limit violation, or to a self-correction, not to comprehension. A Band 6.0
candidate typically drops 2–3 marks in Part 1 that a Band 7.0 candidate does not, and none of that
gap is a listening-ability gap.

**Characteristic trap.** The **read-back correction**: a value is given, the other speaker repeats
it back, and it is corrected — *"so that's the fourteenth" / "the fourteenth, yes — sorry, no, the
fortieth… I mean the twenty-fourth."* Also the **number-shape confusion pair** (13/30, 15/50,
40/14), and the **alphabet confusion set** for spelled names (see §8.3).

### 2.2 Part 2 — everyday monologue (Q11–20)

**Content.** One speaker, everyday social context. Officially exemplified as "a speech about local
facilities" and "a talk about the arrangements for meals during a conference." **[OFFICIAL]** In
practice: a person with institutional authority informing an audience about a place, a service, or
an event. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Speakers.** One. **[OFFICIAL]** The monologue nearly always has an *implied live audience* — the
speaker addresses "you", refers to "over here", "as you can see", "if you'd like to follow me" —
which is what makes map/plan labelling natural.

**Register.** Prepared-but-spoken. Rehearsed structure, spontaneous delivery. This is the single
easiest register to render badly in TTS, because it is the one that most resembles written prose;
an author must consciously insert the marks of live speech (see §10.3).

**What is asked.** The part where **plan / map / diagram labelling** most often appears, because
tour and facility monologues describe a physical layout. **[CONVERGENT]** Also matching (which
facility has which feature), multiple choice, sentence completion, and table completion.

**Answer character.** Mixed: labels from a box (matching/labelling → the answer is a *letter*, and
spelling is irrelevant), plus short completions. This is the part where the answer format changes
most between question groups, which is itself a source of lost marks.

**Difficulty position.** Harder than Part 1 on two counts: (a) no interlocutor asks the clarifying
questions for you — nobody says "sorry, was that fifteen or fifty?"; (b) monologue means no turn
boundaries to hang your place on. Compensating: the content is concrete and the speaker's structure
is usually explicitly signposted.

**Characteristic trap.** The **enumerate-then-eliminate** move: the speaker lists several options
and rules most out — *"we used to run those on Tuesdays, and a lot of people still assume that's the
case, but it's Thursdays now."* Also the **spatial garden-path** in map tasks: the speaker starts
describing a route toward one landmark and redirects. Map tasks additionally test whether the
candidate has *oriented* the map before the audio starts (found the "you are here" marker, fixed
north, read the key).

### 2.3 Part 3 — academic discussion, up to four speakers (Q21–30)

**Content.** A conversation in an educational or training context. Officially: "a conversation
between two main speakers… for example, two university students in discussion, perhaps guided by a
tutor." **[OFFICIAL]** The widely-taught description is **"up to four people"**; two main speakers
with an occasional third is the norm, and four is rare but permitted. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Speakers.** 2–4 (2 typical, 3 common in tutorial-shaped items). When there are three, the
convention is that the third is *asymmetric* — a tutor with a different role in the discourse
(questioning, evaluating, redirecting), not a third peer. Three symmetric peers would be
unreasonably hard to track by voice alone. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Register.** Academic-informal. Students speak like students: hedged, self-interrupting,
overlapping, revising in mid-sentence. The tutor is more measured but still conversational. This is
the most *interactionally* complex register on the paper.

**What is asked.** The evaluative types cluster here: **multiple choice** (especially "what does X
say about Y", "why did X choose Z"), **matching** (attribute an opinion/feature to a person or an
item from a box), and **multi-select** ("choose TWO letters"). Completion appears but is less
dominant than in Parts 1 and 4. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Answer character.** Mostly **letters**, not words. Which means Part 3 marks are lost to
*interpretation*, not spelling — the exact inverse of Part 1. A learner's Part 1 and Part 3 error
profiles almost never share a cause.

**Difficulty position.** Consistently reported as a step change from Part 2, on grounds that are
worth naming precisely, because the coaching response differs for each:
- **Attribution load** — you must retain *who* said a thing, not only *what* was said.
- **Stance, not fact** — the answer is often an attitude (reservation, preference, concession),
  which is conveyed by intonation and hedging rather than by a content word you can catch.
- **Negotiated outcome** — a proposal is floated, resisted, modified, and agreed. The keyed answer
  is the **final settled position**, and it is typically stated *least emphatically*, because by
  then everyone agrees and agreement is spoken quietly and briefly.
- **Paraphrase density** — the option on the page and the words in the audio rarely share
  vocabulary; the audio's actual words are more often planted in a *wrong* option.

**Characteristic trap.** The **concession reversal**: *"I did think the sample size was the problem
— well, no, that wasn't really it; it was more that we ran it in the wrong week."* Also **agreement
theft**: speaker A proposes, speaker B objects at length, A concedes, B then agrees with A's
*original* idea — and candidates key the objection because it was the loudest and longest thing
said. And **option-word planting**: every distractor's exact wording is spoken aloud somewhere,
just not as the answer.

### 2.4 Part 4 — academic lecture (Q31–40)

**Content.** One speaker on an academic subject. **[OFFICIAL]** Typical fields reported across
recent practice corpora: natural sciences and animal behaviour, geology and earth science, history
and archaeology, business and management, psychology and social science, technology, the arts.
**[CONVERGENT]** Officially the topics are not specialised — no prior knowledge is needed and the
lexis avoids technical jargon that would advantage a subject specialist.

**Speakers.** One. **[OFFICIAL]** Almost always framed as an *extract* — "part of a lecture", "the
introduction to a module" — which is the device that lets it start and stop mid-argument.

**Register.** Formal-spoken academic. Longer clauses, nominalisation, explicit discourse structure,
but still spoken: the lecturer digresses, glosses their own terms, and flags structure aloud.

**What is asked.** **Note completion** and **sentence/summary completion** dominate, because a
lecture's argument maps naturally onto a hierarchical notes outline. **[CONVERGENT]** Matching or
multiple choice appears in Part 4 more often in recent papers than the older textbook stereotype
allows — practitioner sources describe Part 4 as having drifted away from being reliably
note-completion-only. **[CONTESTED as to frequency; CONVERGENT as to "it happens".]**

**Answer character.** Single content words and short noun phrases, frequently **topic-specific
lexis introduced in the lecture itself**. Word limits are tight (very often ONE WORD ONLY).

**Difficulty position.** The hardest part, for one structural reason above all: **Part 4 has no
mid-part pause.** All ten questions are previewed at once, then the lecture runs continuously from
Q31 to Q40. **[CONVERGENT — see §7.3]** In Parts 1–3, a candidate who loses the thread gets a free
reset halfway through. In Part 4 there is no reset. A single missed answer therefore cascades: the
candidate keeps hunting for an answer that has already gone past, and loses the next two or three
while hunting. **This is the single most important pedagogical fact in the whole listening paper**,
and it is what our "recovery" teaching payload exists to address.

> One SEO source attributes to "Cambridge Assessment English data (2024)" a precise claim that
> candidates score 0.5–0.8 marks fewer per question in Section 4 than Section 1. **[CONTESTED]** —
> I could not find that dataset, the statistic is dimensionally odd ("marks per question" on a
> 1-mark-per-question paper), and the page shows signs of machine generation. Do not repeat this
> number in our content. The qualitative claim (Part 4 is hardest) is safe; the number is not.

**Characteristic trap.** The **digression**: the lecturer leaves the outline for 20–30 seconds
(an anecdote, a caveat, a historical aside) and returns. Candidates who were counting on the notes'
visual order to keep their place fall off during the digression. Also the **restated definition**
(the term is glossed twice, once loosely and once precisely, and the gap wants the precise one) and
the **enumerated list whose numbers do not match the notes** ("three factors" spoken, but the notes
only gap two of them).

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 3. What actually escalates (and what does not)

Useful for calibrating our authored scripts. Sorted by how much it moves between Part 1 and Part 4.

**Escalates strongly:**
1. **Lexical density** — content words per clause. Part 1 is padded with phatic and procedural
   language; Part 4 is not.
2. **Distance between cue and answer** — in Part 1 the signpost and the answer are typically in the
   same clause; in Part 4 the signpost may precede the answer by a full sentence.
3. **Paraphrase distance** — Part 1 questions frequently share vocabulary with the audio; Part 3–4
   questions almost never do.
4. **Syntactic complexity** — subordination, passive, nominalisation, fronted adverbials.
5. **Answer abstraction** — from "a date" (Part 1) to "the reason a method was rejected" (Part 3) to
   "the term for a process" (Part 4).
6. **Cost of a miss** — see §2.4. Part 1 gives you a mid-part reset *and* an interlocutor who often
   repeats things. Part 4 gives you neither.

**Escalates mildly:**
7. **Speech rate** — real but modest; the perceived speed-up is mostly (1) and (4) rather than
   articulation rate.
8. **Number of speakers** — only in Part 3, and it is a *tracking* load, not a comprehension load.

**Does not escalate (important, because learners assume it does):**
9. **Accent difficulty.** Accents are distributed across the paper, not saved up for the end. A
   strong Australian voice is as likely in Part 1 as in Part 4. **[CONVERGENT]**
10. **Spelling burden.** It is *front-loaded*: Part 1 carries far more spell-from-dictation risk
    than Part 4. This inverts learners' expectations and is worth teaching explicitly.
11. **Audio quality / clarity.** Studio-clean throughout. Real background noise is not used.

range, drawn from official examples plus published descriptions of many practice tests. **They are
prompts for original authoring, not content to copy.** For each I give the shape, and — because

**Cross-cutting notes for Part 1 authors.**
- A Part 1 script that does not contain at least one **spelled-out proper noun** and at least one
  **number read aloud** is under-testing the part's defining skill.
- Prefer names whose spelling is *not* inferable from pronunciation, and which are culturally varied
  — this is a listening test, not a British-surname test.
- Postcodes/zip codes, reference codes, and email addresses are gold: they force letter-by-letter
  attention and they naturally invite read-backs.

**Cross-cutting notes for Part 2 authors.**
- If the situation has a *place*, it should probably carry a map/plan item — Part 2 is where
  BandReady's existing map asset earns its keep.
- The monologue needs an *audience-directed* voice. Second person, deixis ("just here", "behind
  you"), and explicit sequencing ("before we go in", "on your way out") are what make it sound
  live rather than read.
- The natural rhythm is **orientation → enumeration → practicalities**, and the two question groups
  usually straddle a shift between two of those.

**Cross-cutting notes for Part 3 authors.**
- Every keyed opinion should be attributable to exactly one speaker, and **the other speaker should
  have said something adjacent-but-different**, so that matching items are genuinely discriminating.
- Build in at least one **negotiated outcome** (proposal → objection → modification → agreement) and
  key the settled position, not the loudest turn.
- Real students **interrupt and overlap**. Our TTS cannot render true overlap (see §10.4), so
  authors must simulate it lexically: latching ("—yeah, exactly, and—"), abandoned clauses, and
  short reactive turns.

## 5. ACCENTS

### 5.1 What is officially claimed

> "Different accents, including British, Australian, New Zealand and North American, are used."
> — [ielts.org, Academic Listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening) **[OFFICIAL]**

IDP's part-level preparation pages phrase the same policy with Canada broken out: recordings
"include a range of accents, including British, Australian, New Zealand, American and Canadian."
**[OFFICIAL]** The two statements are compatible — "North American" = American + Canadian.

Three things follow, and all three are worth saying to learners:

1. **The variety is deliberate, not incidental.** It is a design feature of a test used for
   immigration and study across the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Canada and the US.
2. **The accents are "standard"/mainstream varieties**, not strong regional ones. Teaching sources
   are consistent: you will hear General Australian (the Sydney/Melbourne mainstream), mainstream
   NZ, mainstream Southern British and General American — not Glaswegian, not broad rural Australian,
   not Newfoundland. **[CONVERGENT]**
3. **All are first-language varieties of English.** Nothing official supports the claim that
   non-native accents (South Asian, African, East Asian, European) are used. See §5.5.

### 5.2 Actual distribution (best available estimate)

No exam board publishes accent proportions. What the teaching literature consistently reports
**[CONVERGENT]**:

| Accent | Rough share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| British (Southern/RP-adjacent, mainstream) | ~50–60% | The default and by far the most frequent |
| Australian | ~15–25% | Common enough that every candidate meets it; NZ and AU are often grouped |
| North American (mostly General American) | ~10–20% | US-standard rather than Canadian-standard |
| New Zealand | ~5% | Least frequent; appears for diversity |
| Canadian | small; rarely distinguishable from GenAm in these recordings | |

Sources: [Magoosh — accents to expect](https://magoosh.com/ielts/accents-expect-ielts-listening/),
[IDP India — listening accents](https://ieltsidpindia.com/prepare/ielts-listening-test/accents),
[IELTS Zone — listening with different accents](https://ieltszone.org/ielts-listening/listening-with-different-accents/).

**Distribution within a test.** Accents are mixed *within* a paper and sometimes within a part
(a British customer and an Australian receptionist is a perfectly ordinary Part 1). They are not
graded by part. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Implication for BandReady, stated bluntly.** Our current corpus is 3 UK scripts and 1 US script,
zero Australian, zero New Zealand. Measured against the paper, we systematically under-expose the
accent our learners find hardest and which matters most for the largest destination market after the
UK. Fixing this is not cosmetic.

### 5.3 What actually differs, per accent — comprehension-relevant only

Vowel-set names below use the standard Wells lexical sets (KIT, DRESS, TRAP, GOAT, PRICE, MOUTH,
NEAR, SQUARE) — the normal way to talk about accents without IPA-heavy notation.

#### Australian English (General Australian)

Sources: [Wikipedia — Australian English phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_English_phonology),
[Wikipedia — high rising terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_rising_terminal),
plus comparative overviews.

| Feature | Effect on a learner | Why it costs marks |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-rhotic** — no /r/ after a vowel (*car* → "cah", *harder* → "hahduh") | shared with British, unfamiliar to learners trained on US English | word-final *-er* and *-or* endings become indistinct; *four/for*, *card/cad* |
| **PRICE backed/lowered** — *time, like, five* drift toward "oy"-ish | *five* can be misheard as a different number | **numbers**: five, nine, five-nine confusions in Part 1 |
| **FACE lowered** — *day, eight, name* drift toward the PRICE region | *eight* ↔ *ate*; *day* ↔ *die* for untrained ears | **dates and times** in Part 1 |
| **GOAT fronted** — *no, boat, go* ≈ "uh-oo" | *no* less crisp | correction cues (*no, actually…*) are less salient |
| **KIT raised** toward "ee" (contrast NZ, below) | *fish* ≈ "feesh" | minimal pairs *ship/sheep*, *fill/feel* |
| **TRAP/BATH split like British** — *dance, chance, castle* with the long "ah" | learner expects the US short-a | vocabulary recognition delay |
| **Weak-vowel merger** — unstressed *-ed*/*-ate* endings centralise | *boxes* vs *boxed*-type distinctions blur | **plural/singular endings**, which are answer-critical |
| **High Rising Terminal (uptalk)** — statements end on a rise | learner hears a question where a fact was given | **catastrophic for distractor handling**: the learner treats a stated answer as tentative and waits for a "real" answer that never comes |
| **Intervocalic /t/ flapping** — *better, city, later* ≈ "bedder" | shared with US, unlike British | *waiting/wading*, *latter/ladder* |
| **Faster, more elided connected speech**; heavy vowel reduction in function words | fewer word boundaries | general |

**The single most important AU fact for our purposes is the high rising terminal.** It is a
*pragmatic* trap, not a phonetic one, and it is teachable in one paragraph: in Australian (and NZ)
English a rising tone on a statement is a *checking-in* device, not a question. A learner who does
not know that will hear an Australian receptionist give them the correct room number and then keep
listening for the "real" answer.

#### New Zealand English

Sources: [Te Ara — features of the NZ accent](https://teara.govt.nz/en/speech-and-accent/page-3),
comparative NZ/AU overviews.

| Feature | Effect |
|---|---|
| **KIT centralised** (opposite direction to Australian): *fish and chips* → "fush and chups" | The clearest AU/NZ discriminator. *Six* vs *sex*, *bit* vs *but* |
| **DRESS raised** toward KIT: *bed* ≈ "bid", *ten* ≈ "tin" | **numbers again**: ten ↔ tin, seven's first vowel |
| **TRAP raised** toward DRESS: *bad* ≈ "bed" | *bad/bed*, *man/men* |
| **NEAR–SQUARE merger**: *beer* ≈ *bear*, *cheer* ≈ *chair* — increasingly general among younger speakers | *chair/cheer*, *hair/here* in map and layout descriptions |
| Non-rhotic; HRT present as in Australian | as above |

Taken together, NZ's short front vowels rotate one step up relative to British, which is why
learners describe NZ as "flatter"/"clipped". Because the shift is *systematic*, it can be taught as
a rule rather than word by word — a genuinely satisfying 3-minute lesson.

#### North American (General American, and Canadian)

| Feature | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Rhotic** — every /r/ pronounced | Learners trained on British audio over-hear /r/-coloured endings |
| **Flapped /t/** — *water, better, twenty* → "wader, bedder, twenny" | *twenty* especially; **number answers** |
| **LOT/PALM/THOUGHT merging** (*cot/caught* merged for nearly all Canadians and much of the US) | *cot/caught*, *don/dawn* |
| **No TRAP/BATH split** — *dance, ask, path* with short-a | vocabulary recognition |
| **Yod-dropping** — *new* → "noo", *Tuesday* → "toozday" | **days of the week** in Part 1 |
| **Canadian raising** (Canadian, and parts of the northern US) — the vowels of *about, house, right, price* start higher **before voiceless consonants only** | *about* sounds like "a-boat", not "a-boot" (that stereotype is wrong); affects *out, house, price, night* |

Canadian and General American are close enough in these recordings that treating them as one
"North American" set is defensible for our purposes, and it is exactly what our `us` accent set
already does.

#### British (mainstream Southern / RP-adjacent)

Baseline for the paper. Non-rhotic; TRAP/BATH split; clear released /t/; GOAT and FACE close to the
"textbook" values most learners were taught. Notable learner difficulties are not phonemic but
prosodic: heavy weak-form reduction in function words (*to, of, and, for, have* → schwa) and
contraction-heavy connected speech.

## 6. TIMING AND DELIVERY — the current-trend question

### 6.1 The headline, stated precisely

| | Paper-based IELTS | Computer-delivered IELTS (incl. IELTS Online) |
|---|---|---|
| Where answers are written during the audio | on the **question paper** | **directly into the answer field** on screen |
| Audio duration | ~30 min | ~30 min |
| Extra time after the audio | **10 minutes to transfer** answers to the answer sheet | **~2 minutes to check** answers. No transfer time — "no extra time will be given to transfer your answers" |
| Total listening block | **~40 minutes** | **~32 minutes** |
| Plays | **once only** | **once only** |

Sources: [ielts.org — Listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening)
("Approximately 30 minutes (plus 10 minutes to transfer your answers to an answer sheet)");
[IDP — How IELTS on computer works](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-how-computer-delivered-ielts-works)
("2 minutes to review your answers at the end of the test", "No extra time will be given to transfer
your answers"); [IDP — computer vs paper](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-on-computer-vs-ielts-on-paper).

**Which is which — the mnemonic that stops the confusion.** *Paper gets 10 because paper has to
move the answers. Computer gets 2 because the answers are already where they need to be.* The
10 minutes is **not a bonus thinking period**; it is a mechanical clerical allowance. And the
2 minutes is **not a penalty**; it is what remains once the clerical task disappears.

**But the practical consequences are not symmetric, and this is the real coaching point:**

- On **paper**, you write into the question booklet, where you can be messy, use abbreviations,
  write two candidate answers, leave a mark to come back to, and *decide later*. The 10 minutes is
  when you tidy, choose between candidates, fix spelling, apply word limits, and check the answer
  sheet's numbering hasn't slipped. A paper candidate who uses all 10 minutes properly recovers real
  marks.
- On **computer**, you must commit to a spelling **at the moment you hear it**, while the next
  question is already being spoken. There is no scratch layer. The 2 minutes is only enough to
  revisit flagged items and fix obvious typos. Consequences: **typing speed and typing accuracy
  become part of the construct**, unresolved answers get abandoned rather than deferred, and the
  discipline of *typing while listening* is a distinct trainable skill.

**Screen lock.** Multiple teaching sources state that at the end of the check period the screen
locks and no further input is possible. **[CONVERGENT]** — plausible and consistent with the timed
delivery, but I did not find it on an official page. Phrase it in our UI as "the check period ends
and your answers are submitted", which is true either way.

**The audio still plays once in both formats.** No pause, no replay, no rewind, no per-question
audio. **[OFFICIAL]** Nothing about computer delivery softens this.

### 6.2 On-screen affordances of the computer test

From [IDP — How IELTS on computer works](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-how-computer-delivered-ielts-works)
and [British Council — IELTS on computer](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/take-ielts/book/ielts-on-computer) **[OFFICIAL]**:

- **Headphones with individual volume control**; a volume bar in the upper right of the screen, and
  a sound check before the test starts.
- **Countdown timer** in the upper middle of the screen.
- **Question navigator** along the bottom: all 40 question numbers, showing answered/unanswered,
  with forward/back arrows.
- **Review flag** (lower left): flagging a question changes its navigator marker from a square to a
  circle, so you can return to it in the 2-minute window.
- Answers are typed (completion/short answer) or clicked (MC/matching/labelling).

**Design consequence for BandReady:** our test runner should mirror this affordance set, because it
is now the *modal* exam experience — a timer, a 1–40 navigator with answered-state, a flag control,
and a check phase that is 2 minutes by default. The 10-minute paper transfer should be a selectable
mode, not the default. (Our existing UI already has a transfer/check step; what §6 argues is that
its **default duration and semantics** should follow the computer test.)

### 6.3 The pre-2020 example and the mid-part pauses — what actually changed, and when

This is where outdated coaching material misleads people, so, precisely:

**CHANGED — effective 4 January 2020. [OFFICIAL, via announcement quoted by two independent sources]**

Three changes were announced and took effect at the start of January 2020:

1. **The Part 1 example was removed.** Before 2020, the recording played a short worked example at
   the start of Part 1 — a snippet of the conversation with the answer already filled in — to
   demonstrate the task. That warm-up is **gone**. After the general instructions, the test goes
   straight into Part 1's real questions.
2. **"Sections" were renamed "Parts."** Part 1, Part 2, Part 3, Part 4.
3. **Page-number references were removed** from the spoken instructions ("Look at questions 1 to 5
   on page 3" → "Look at questions 1 to 5") — a necessary consequence of computer delivery, where
   there are no pages.

Sources: [Magoosh — IELTS Listening format changes in 2020](https://magoosh.com/ielts/ielts-listening-format-changes-in-2020/)
(quotes the board announcement: "We will be changing the word 'Sections' to 'Parts'", "We will be
removing the Part 1 example", "We are also removing the page number references"; gives the date as
4 January 2020); [Cullen Education / Key to IELTS — Changes to IELTS Listening](https://keytoielts.com/changes-to-ielts-listening/)
(independently reports the same two substantive changes, effective early January 2020, and states
explicitly that there were **no other changes**).

**NOT CHANGED — the mid-part pauses still exist.** Both 2020 sources state explicitly that nothing
else changed, and the pause architecture in post-2020 published practice material is unchanged: a
reading pause before each question group, a mid-part pause in Parts 1–3, a half-minute check after
each part. **[CONVERGENT — see §7 for the full structure]**

**Common misconceptions to correct in our content (all false):**

| Claim you will see online | Reality |
|---|---|
| "The example question was removed in 2023" | It was removed **4 January 2020**. |
| "There are no pauses any more" | Pauses to read questions remain, before every question group. |
| "Computer-delivered has different questions" | Same items, same recordings; only the input method and post-audio time differ. |
| "GT listening is easier / different" | Identical paper. **[OFFICIAL]** |
| "You get 10 minutes on the computer test too" | You get ~2 minutes to check. **[OFFICIAL]** |
| "Section 4 has a break in the middle" | It does not. **[CONVERGENT]** |
| "You can replay a bit if you missed it" | Never, in either format. **[OFFICIAL]** |

### 6.5 Durations, rates, and the arithmetic behind them

**[DERIVED]** — this is my own reconciliation, and it is worth showing because published figures
conflict badly (sources variously claim Part 1 is "2–3 minutes" and "4–6 minutes").

The 30-minute total is *audio file time*, and includes announcer speech and silent pauses. From the
pause structure in §7:

```
Per part (Parts 1–3):   reading pause 1  ~30 s
                      + reading pause 2  ~30 s
                      + end-of-part check ~30 s   = ~90 s of non-speech
Part 4:                 reading pause     ~40 s
                      + end-of-part check ~30 s   = ~70 s of non-speech
Whole-test intro + announcer lines                ≈ 60–75 s

Total non-speech ≈ (3 × 90) + 70 + 70  ≈ 410 s ≈ 6.8 min
Continuous speech ≈ 30 − 6.8 ≈ 23 min across 4 parts ≈ 5–6 min per part
```

At a plausible 140–165 wpm this gives roughly **700–950 spoken words per part**, ~3,000–3,600 for
the paper. Published "4–5 minute" figures are describing *speech only* and are on the low side;
"2–3 minutes" for Part 1 is simply wrong for a full ten-item part.

**Speech rate escalation [CONVERGENT, approximate].** Part 1 ≈ 130–145 wpm with visible pausing;
Part 2 ≈ 140–155 wpm with fewer pauses; Part 3 ≈ 150–170 wpm with overlap and false starts; Part 4
≈ 155–175 wpm, continuous. Teaching sources describe the same curve qualitatively ("in Part 1 the
speaker talks quite slowly, making pauses… in Part 4 the speaker talks quite quickly and uses a
wide range of vocabulary" —
[ielts-up](https://ielts-up.com/listening/ielts-listening-test.html)).

**Do not implement escalation by turning up the TTS speed knob.** Most of the perceived rate
increase is lexical density and syntactic complexity, not articulation rate. A Kokoro `speed` of
about 0.98 for Part 1 and 1.04 for Part 4 is the outer bound of what sounds natural; the rest must
come from the writing.

## 7. Audio conventions — the announcer, the framing, the pauses

This is the part that our renderer implements literally, so it is documented as a template.

### 7.1 The whole-test opening

The recording opens with general instructions before Part 1 (post-2020, with no example):
identification of the test, a statement that there are four parts, a statement that **the recording
is played once only**, and a note that there will be time to read the questions and check answers.
Then it goes straight into Part 1's framing. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Authoring template (write our own wording; do not copy any board's):**

```
[whole-test intro]  This is the IELTS-style Listening practice test.
                    You will hear four separate recordings and answer questions on each.
                    Each recording is played once only.
                    You will be given time to read the questions before each recording
                    and a short time to check your answers afterwards.
                    The test has four parts.
```

### 7.2 The per-part framing

Every part is framed by the same five-move sequence. Verified against a current practice-publisher
audioscript ([Auvoxi audioscripts, IELTS Practice 8 Test 01](https://www.auvoxi.com/audioscripts-ielts-practice-8-listening-test-01/))
and consistent with all published material. **[CONVERGENT]**

```
1. ORIENT      "Part one. You will hear <who> <doing what, to whom>."
                → one sentence naming the speakers and the situation.
                  This is the candidate's only context. It is not decorative.

2. PREVIEW     "First, you have some time to look at questions one to six."
                → [SILENT PAUSE ~30 s]

3. CUE         "Now listen carefully and answer questions one to six."
                → audio begins.

   ── first question group plays ──

4. MID-PART    "Before you hear the rest of the conversation, you have some time to
   PREVIEW      look at questions seven to ten."
                → [SILENT PAUSE ~30 s]
                → "Now listen and answer questions seven to ten."

   ── second question group plays ──

5. CLOSE       "That is the end of Part one. You now have half a minute to check
                your answers."
                → [SILENT PAUSE ~30 s]
```

and after Part 4:

```
   "That is the end of the listening test.
    You now have ten minutes to transfer your answers to the answer sheet."   [paper]
    — or, on computer —
   "You now have two minutes to check your answers."                          [computer]
```

### 7.3 Where the pauses fall, exactly

| Part | Question groups | Pauses |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | two groups (a 4–6 / 6–4 split; e.g. 1–6 then 7–10) | preview ~30 s, mid-part ~30 s, check ~30 s |
| 2 | two groups (e.g. 11–15 then 16–20) | preview ~30 s, mid-part ~30 s, check ~30 s |
| 3 | two groups (e.g. 21–24 then 25–30) | preview ~30 s, mid-part ~30 s, check ~30 s |
| 4 | **one group: 31–40, all ten previewed at once** | preview ~30–45 s, **no mid-part pause**, check ~30 s |

Part 4's single-group structure is confirmed by multiple independent teaching sources — "unlike
Sections 1–3, Section 4 has no break in the middle of the recording. You listen continuously and
answer ten questions… without any pause"
([learnenglishweekly](https://learnenglishweekly.com/ielts/listening/section-4),
[Cathoven](https://resources.cathoven.com/ielts-listening/section-4)) — and by the audioscript
structure above. **[CONVERGENT]**

**Pause length.** "Half a minute to check" is stated in the announcer's own words. Preview pauses
are not announced with a duration; ~30 seconds is the practical convention, with the longer Part 4
preview (all ten questions) commonly 40–45 seconds. **[CONVERGENT]**

**The pedagogy hiding in the pause structure** (this is the actionable part):
- The preview pause is not dead air. It is when **prediction** happens: read the gaps, decide what
  *kind* of thing goes in each (number? name? plural noun? verb?), note the word limit, and mark the
  signpost words in the question stems that will announce each answer. A candidate who reads the
  questions *passively* in the pause has wasted the only strategic resource the exam gives them.
- The mid-part pause in Parts 1–3 is the **recovery point**. If you lost an answer in the first
  group, the mid-part pause is where you write your best guess, abandon it, and re-anchor on the
  next group. Teaching this converts a 3-mark cascade into a 1-mark loss.
- Part 4 has no recovery point, so recovery must be done **in-flight**: the moment you realise you
  have missed one, skip to the next gap on the page and listen for *its* cue rather than continuing
  to hunt for the one that has gone. This is the single highest-value habit in the whole paper.
- The end-of-part 30 seconds should be used for **spelling and form**, not for reconsidering
  meaning: check plural -s, check the word limit, check that a spelled name matches the letters you
  wrote down.

### 7.4 How BandReady's existing scripts compare

Measured from `content/core-en/data/listening_scripts.jsonl` on 2026-07-28. Our four scripts already
follow the convention closely:

- `ls_t1_p1` — narrator orients and previews Q1–6 with a **30,000 ms** pause, mid-part preview for
  Q7–10 with another **30,000 ms**, closes with "That is the end of Part one." ✓ matches §7.2/§7.3.
- `ls_t1_p4` — single preview of Q31–40 at **40,000 ms**, **no mid-part pause**, closes with
  "That is the end of Part four. You now have half a minute to check your answers." ✓ matches the
  Part 4 exception exactly.

Two gaps worth closing:

1. **The CUE move is missing.** Our narrator goes from "you have some time to look at questions…"
   straight into the pause and then the dialogue, with no "Now listen carefully and answer questions
   one to six." That line matters: it is the audible signal that the pause has ended, and without it
   a candidate who looked away has no cue that the audio has restarted.
2. **There is no whole-test intro.** A test-level render should open with the §7.1 framing so the
   "played once only" rule is stated by the audio, as it is in the exam.

Both are pure-content fixes inside `script_json` (and, for the test-level intro, a narrator block on
the Part 1 script or a test-level preamble) — no schema change needed. `MAX_PAUSE_MS` in
`sidecar/bandready/audio/stitch.py` is 60,000, so 30–45 s pauses are comfortably in range.

## 8. Answer form: the rules that decide whether a heard answer scores

Not strictly "sections and accents", but it is inseparable from delivery, and it drives our matcher.

### 8.1 Spelling **[OFFICIAL / CONVERGENT]**

- **Spelling must be correct.** ielts.org: "you will lose marks for incorrect spelling and grammar."
  A correctly heard, mis-spelled answer scores **zero**. **[OFFICIAL]**
- **Both British and American spellings are accepted** (*programme/program*, *colour/color*,
  *organise/organize*, *centre/center*, *travelled/traveled*, *metre/meter*). **[CONVERGENT, and
  universally reported]** This is a genuine and under-taught fact.
- **Abbreviations are not accepted** where the full word was spoken.
- **Capitalisation is not penalised** in listening; answers in upper or lower case are accepted.
  (Writing everything in capitals is a common and safe paper-test tactic — it removes handwriting
  ambiguity for the clerical marker.)
Source cluster: [IELTS Zone — spelling, numbers, dates](https://ieltszone.org/ielts-listening-spelling-numbers-dates/),
[ielts.org listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening).

### 8.2 Word limits and form **[CONVERGENT]**

- Instructions specify the limit ("ONE WORD ONLY", "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER",
  "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS"). **Exceeding the limit scores zero even if the answer is present.**
- A **hyphenated word counts as one word**.
- **Numbers may be written as figures or words**; dates in any standard format; times as *9.30*,
  *9:30* or *half past nine*.
- Grammatical form must fit the gap: singular/plural, article, and tense. A plural heard and a
  singular written is wrong.

### 8.3 Dictation hazards worth building drills around **[CONVERGENT + practitioner knowledge]**

- **Spoken-alphabet confusion sets** (these are what make spelled names hard):
  `B/P/V/D/E/G/T/C/Z` (all share the "ee" nucleus in British English), `M/N`, `F/S/X`, `A/K/J/H`,
  `I/Y`, `U/W/Q`, and `Z` itself — **"zed" (UK/AU/NZ) vs "zee" (US/Canada)** is an accent-driven trap
  that hits directly.
- **Number pairs**: 13/30, 14/40, 15/50, 16/60, 17/70, 18/80, 19/90. Stress placement distinguishes
  them (thir**TEEN** vs **THIR**ty) and the distinction weakens in fast connected speech and
  disappears in some flapped-/t/ realisations ("twenny", "thirdy").
- **Double letters and "double" itself**: "double-oh-seven", "double L", "treble" (UK) vs "triple".
- **British/AU phone and postcode conventions** — grouped digits, "oh" for zero, "double" for
  repeats — vs North American grouping.
- **Email and web addresses**: "at", "dot", "underscore", "hyphen/dash", "forward slash".
- **Dates**: 3 April vs April 3; "the third of April" vs "April the third"; ordinal endings.
- **Currency**: "two-fifty" = £2.50 or $250 depending on context — resolved only by the situation.
- **Homophone traps to avoid keying** (as authors): *their/there*, *whether/weather*, *sight/site*,
  *stationary/stationery*, *practice/practise*, *principal/principle*. If we key one of these, we
  have written a vocabulary item disguised as a listening item.

**Two facts to surface in the UI because they change behaviour:**
- **There is no negative marking.** Never leave a blank. A guess with the right *form* (a plural
  noun, a two-digit number) is free expected value.
- **Band 7.0 costs 30 marks; Band 6.0 costs 23.** Seven raw marks separate them — roughly two
  answers per part. That reframes "I need a 7" from an abstract goal into "stop losing two marks a
  part to spelling and corrections", which is exactly what our teaching payload can fix.

## 9. What this means for authoring — constraints for the content agents

1. **Part 1 must be spelled and numbered.** At least one letter-by-letter proper noun and at least
   two numeric answers per Part 1 script, with a read-back somewhere.
2. **Every part needs at least one self-correction or negotiated reversal.** It is the paper's
   signature move and the module's signature teaching point. Vary the mechanism: read-back
   correction (P1), enumerate-and-eliminate (P2), concession reversal (P3), restated definition
   (P4).
3. **Two question groups in Parts 1–3, one group of ten in Part 4.** The group boundary should fall
   at a natural topic shift in the script, not mid-topic.
4. **The orienting sentence must name the situation and the speakers**, and nothing else. It is the
   candidate's entire context.
5. **Part 3 needs three distinct speakers often enough to be representative** — our current corpus
   has one three-speaker Part 3, which is about right per test.
6. **Part 4 must define any term it keys**, must have a visible skeleton, and should contain exactly
   one digression with no answers inside it.
7. **No answer may depend on world knowledge** or on a homophone judgement.
8. **Accent coverage across a test.** Aim to mix at least two accent sets per test and to give the
   Australian set real presence — see §10 for what "Australian" can honestly mean for us.
9. **Word limits must be checkable.** Every keyed answer must satisfy its own stated limit, and the
   instruction must match the keyed answers' form (an instruction saying ONE WORD with a two-word key
   is the most common authoring bug in this format).

## 10. Implications for BandReady — the honest engineering reading

### 10.1 The accent gap, quantified

Current corpus: `ls_t1_p1` (uk), `ls_t1_p2` (us), `ls_t1_p3` (uk), `ls_t1_p4` (uk). No Australian,
no New Zealand. Voice inventory: 9 UK Kokoro voices and 2 US in practice.

Against §5.2's distribution, a representative corpus should be roughly **55% British, 20%
Australian, 15% North American, 5–10% New Zealand**, mixed within tests rather than segregated by
test.

### 10.2 The uncomfortable truth about `au`

`sidecar/bandready/audio/tts_render.py` already knows this and says so in a comment:

```python
# 07 §3 — role x accent -> Kokoro voice id. Kokoro v1.0 has no Australian voices, so
# `au` falls back to British ones and the UI labels it "approximated".
```

and `ACCENT_LABELS["au"] = "Australian (approximated with British voices)"`. My search of Kokoro's
published voice inventory confirms it: Kokoro v1.0 ships American (`af_*`, `am_*`) and British
(`bf_*`, `bm_*`) English voices and **no Australian or New Zealand voice**
([Kokoro voice index](https://madeinoz67.github.io/madeinoz-voice-server/KOKORO_VOICES/),
[Soniqo Kokoro guide](https://soniqo.audio/guides/kokoro)). The `au` map currently reuses
`bm_george / bf_alice / bf_lily / bm_daniel / bm_fable` — i.e. British voices in different casting
slots.

**So we cannot deliver Australian phonology.** Pretending otherwise would be dishonest to the
learner, and the existing label is right to say "approximated".

**What we *can* deliver, and should, is everything about Australian English that is not segmental
phonetics:**

| Layer | Can Kokoro-with-British-voices deliver it? | How we author it |
|---|---|---|
| Vowel shifts, rhoticity | **No** | — |
| High rising terminal | Partially — a question mark induces a rise; unreliable | punctuate statement-final HRT as `?` sparingly, and *teach* HRT explicitly in the coaching payload |
| Lexis and idiom (§5.4) | **Yes, fully** | *uni, chemist, footpath, public holiday, mobile, TAFE, Year 12, unit, GP, arvo (flavour only)* |
| Institutional/cultural detail | **Yes, fully** | Australian place names, semesters, ATAR-era schooling, state-level services, bushfire/drought/reef content, AUD |
| Spelling conventions | **Yes** | `-ise`, `-our`, `-re`; `program` (AU) vs `programme` |
| Date/number conventions | **Yes** | day-first dates, "double" for repeated digits, "zed" |
| Discourse style | **Yes** | more informal address, tag questions, *no worries*, *how are you going* |

**Recommendation.** Redefine our `au` accent set as an **Australian *variety* set** — voice cast
plus a lexical/cultural authoring contract — and be explicit in the UI: *"Australian variety:
Australian vocabulary, conventions and situations; voice approximated with a British-accent
synthesiser."* That is a defensible, teachable product, and it beats both (a) silently pretending
and (b) shipping no Australian content at all.

**Two options for real AU phonology, if we ever want it:**
- Add a second local TTS with an AU voice and route `au` to it. Cost: another model download, another
  engine to package — directly against the "942 MB adopted, no download needed" constraint recorded
  for this machine.
- Keep Kokoro and accept the approximation. Cheaper, and honest if labelled.

Either way, **the accent *drill* remains fully valuable**: re-rendering the same script under a
different accent set with a different voice cast still trains voice-timbre adaptation and, with the
lexical layer, trains the vocabulary that actually blocks comprehension.

### 10.3 Making TTS scripts sound spoken

Because the audio is synthesised, "sounds like real speech" has to be achieved in the text.
Techniques that survive Kokoro:

- **Contractions everywhere** (*I'll, we've, that's, don't, there's, it'd*) — the single biggest
  naturalness lever.
- **Filled pauses sparingly** — *um, er, well, right, OK, so* — at turn starts, not mid-phrase.
- **Sentence-final particles and tags** — *isn't it, then, actually, you know*.
- **Short turns and backchannels** in dialogue (*Mm-hm. / Right. / OK. / Sure.*) rendered as their
  own lines with small `pause_after_ms`, which is what creates conversational rhythm.
- **Punctuation as prosody**: commas for micro-pauses, em-dashes for self-interruption, ellipses for
  trailing off, `?` for genuine rises. Kokoro respects punctuation more reliably than it respects
  any markup.
- **Numbers and spellings written as they are said**: "O-K-A-F-O-R" (as our `ls_t1_p1` already
  does), "oh-seven-nine-one", "twenty twenty-six", "half past nine" — never bare digits where a
  human would speak them out.
- **Pause budget for realism**: 150–350 ms between turns in fast dialogue, 400–700 ms at topic
  shifts, 800–1200 ms before a correction lands (the beat before "sorry, no—" is what makes the
  correction audible).

### 10.4 What we cannot render, and the workarounds

| Exam feature | Renderable? | Workaround |
|---|---|---|
| Genuine overlapping speech | **No** — lines are concatenated sequentially | lexical latching, abandoned clauses, very short `pause_after_ms` (≤120 ms) between the latched turns |
| True prosodic emphasis on a contrast word | Weak | rely on lexical marking (*it's not X, it's **Y***) and comma placement |
| Australian/NZ phonology | **No** (§10.2) | variety-level authoring |
| Room acoustics / phone-line quality for Part 1 calls | **No** | state it in the orienting sentence ("a telephone conversation") |
| Laughter, coughs, audience noise | **No** | omit; the real exam is studio-clean anyway |

### 10.5 Matcher implications

`sidecar/bandready/scoring/answers.py` is shared with reading and uses **exact** spelling for
listening — correct per §8.1. Two things the research says the *answer key* must therefore carry
(no matcher change needed, just authoring discipline):

- **Both spelling variants as accepted answers** wherever a keyed word differs between British and
  American convention (`["programme","program"]`, `["organisation","organization"]`,
  `["centre","center"]`, `["colour","color"]`, `["metre","meter"]`, `["travelled","traveled"]`,
  `["theatre","theater"]`, `["licence","license"]` where the sense allows). Our existing keys are
  lowercase arrays-of-arrays, so this is a pure content change.
- **Number/word and date-format variants** where the instruction permits (`["15","fifteen"]`,
  `["9.30","9:30","half past nine"]`, `["3 April","April 3","3rd April"]`).

Failing to do this would make BandReady *stricter than the real exam* — which is the one direction a
practice test must never err in, because it teaches learners to distrust correct answers.

### 10.6 Timing defaults for the mock

Following §6.4, the listening mock should default to **computer-delivered timing**:

- audio plays once, no pause/seek/replay (server-enforced);
- answers typed into fields during the audio (no separate transfer step);
- **2-minute check phase** after Part 4, with a review-flag list of flagged/blank items;
- 1–40 navigator showing answered state; countdown timer;
- **paper mode** as an explicit alternative: answers into a scratch layer during the audio, then a
  **10-minute transfer** phase into the answer sheet — which is worth keeping precisely because it
  drills a different skill (deferred decision-making) and because paper is still the format in many
  countries.

Our UI already has a transfer/check step; what this research changes is **which one is the default**
and what each is *for*.

## 12. Open questions for the design agent

1. **Do we ship an `nz` accent set?** §5.2 says NZ is ~5% of the exam. Kokoro cannot render it any
   more than it can render AU. A fifth accent set that is also "approximated" may cost more in
   credibility than it returns. My inclination: **no separate `nz` set**; instead cover NZ in the
   *teaching* layer (the short-front-vowel-shift lesson in §5.3 is genuinely useful and takes three
   minutes) and note NZ lexis inside the `au` variety contract.
2. **Does the mock default to computer timing?** §6.4 argues yes, strongly. Needs a product call
   because it changes the transfer/check step's default semantics.
3. **Do we relabel `au` as an Australian *variety* set** (§10.2) in `ACCENT_LABELS`, with an
   authoring contract for lexis? This is a one-line label change plus a content rule — but it is a
   change to an owned-by-someone-else file, so it needs routing (see problems).
4. **Do answer keys gain British/American spelling variants** (§10.5) as a blanket authoring rule?
   I believe they must, or we are stricter than the exam.
5. **Ten scripts per accent, or accent mixed within scripts?** The exam mixes *within* a
   conversation (a British caller, an Australian officer). Our schema already supports per-speaker
   `accent`, and `resolve_voices()` honours it when `accent_set=None`. That capability is currently
   unused by our content and is the cheapest realism win available.
