# L-R3 — Strategy and Bands: how IELTS-style Listening is actually scored, and what actually raises the band

Research briefing for BandReady **listening**-content authors.
Compiled 2026-07-28. Consumers: the listening design agent (schema owner), the Part 1–4 script
authoring agents, the drill-content agent, the verify/merge agent, and the coaching copy in
`docs/plan/07-listening-module.md` §§4–10.

Companions: `content/core-en/staging/research/03-bands-and-errors.md` (speaking),
`content/core-en/staging-writing/research/03-bands-and-errors.md` (writing) and
`content/core-en/staging-reading/research/03-strategy-and-bands.md` (reading).
**Do not port any of their shapes.**

- Speaking and Writing are *productive*: the learner makes language, four descriptor criteria judge
  it, so the teaching payload is band-graded model answers.
- Reading is *receptive but static*: the text stays on the page, so the teaching payload is a worked
  solution — locate, verify, name the paraphrase device, name the trap.
- **Listening is receptive and irreversible.** The audio plays once and is gone. There is no
  re-reading, no going back, no verification pass. A worked solution that says "the answer was in
  line 42" teaches nothing, because on test day there is no line 42 to look at.

Everything below is therefore organised around the four things that actually decide a listening raw
score — **prediction before the audio**, **signpost tracking during it**, **distraction resistance
at the moment of decision**, and **answer-form discipline at the point of writing** — plus the
**recovery** protocol that stops one miss becoming three, and the decoding floor underneath all of
it.

## 0. TL;DR for authors — the sixteen things worth teaching

Ordered by band movement bought per hour of learner effort. If a per-question teaching note has room
for one point, it should come from this list.

1. **The audio is linear and so are the answers.** Answers appear in question order, always. That
   single fact powers prediction, powers recovery, and is the only navigational aid the candidate
   has. Teach it as the spine of the whole module (§3.3).
2. **Prediction is the highest-yield listening technique there is.** In the preview pause, decide
   what *kind* of word each gap needs — number, name, plural count noun, uncountable, adjective,
   -ing verb — from the printed grammar around it. A candidate who knows they are waiting for a
   plural noun hears it; a candidate waiting for "an answer" hears prose (§5).
3. **The printed question is a paraphrase of the audio.** As in Reading, the words rarely survive.
   Unlike Reading, you cannot go back and check. So predict the *synonym*, not the word (§5.6).
4. **The last value stated for a slot is the answer, not the first.** Speaker self-correction is the
   defining IELTS listening trap and has no equivalent in Reading. It is worth 3–5 raw marks per
   test to a candidate who has never been taught it (§6.3, §7).
5. **Answer-form discipline is worth 2–5 raw marks to almost everyone.** Misspelt = zero. Over the
   word limit = zero. Wrong plural = zero. Two answers written = zero. These are marks lost by
   candidates who *heard correctly* — pure, avoidable loss, and a much bigger factor here than
   anywhere else in the exam because every completion answer is transcribed under time pressure
   (§4).
6. **Both British and American spellings are accepted; capitalisation is ignored.** Most candidates
   do not know either fact and waste attention on both (§4.2, §4.3).
7. **Missing one answer costs three.** The candidate keeps listening for something already spoken
   while the next two answers go past. The recovery protocol is a *jump forward*, never a search
   backward (§8).
8. **Never leave a blank.** No negative marking. An empty box is a guaranteed zero; a guess of the
   predicted slot type is free (§1, §8.4).
9. **Signposts are the listening equivalent of paraphrase recognition.** A closed inventory of maybe
   150 discourse markers announces an answer, announces a correction, or announces a topic boundary.
   They are learnable in a fortnight and they are the only handholds in Part 4 (§6).
10. **Part 3 and Part 4 are where the band is decided.** Parts 1–2 are near-ceiling for anyone at
    band 6; the difference between 6.5 and 7.5 lives almost entirely in the second half of the test
    (§10.5, §11).
11. **Part 4 has no dialogue, no repair, no redundancy and no mid-part pause on most sets.** Nothing
    is repeated, nobody asks for clarification, and the candidate is 25 minutes tired. It needs its
    own protocol — structure markers, not keywords (§6.4, §8.5).
12. **The preview pause is a work period, not a rest.** 30 seconds, and there is a fixed thing to do
    in it. Teach the protocol as a drill, not as advice (§5.5).
13. **The check step is for form, not for content.** In the 2 minutes at the end you cannot recover
    meaning — the audio is gone. You can recover word limits, plurals, blanks and doubled answers.
    Sweep for those (§3.2).
14. **The band table is unforgiving at the top and generous in the middle.** 18–22 is all band 5.5;
    but band 8 tolerates 5 wrong and band 9 tolerates 1. Show raw score as the primary metric (§2.5).
15. **One conversion table for Academic and General Training.** Listening is literally the same test
    for both. Reading is *not*, and the contrast is worth teaching explicitly (§1.2).
16. **Below the strategy layer there is a decoding layer.** If the learner cannot segment connected
    speech, no amount of prediction helps. That is what the dictation mini-mode is for, and it should
    be prescribed on evidence (near-miss spelling tags), not offered as a menu item (§9).

## 1. Marking mechanics — facts, freely usable

All of this is published operational fact about the exam and is safe to state, implement and teach.

- **40 questions**, 4 parts, 10 questions per part. Identical for Academic and General Training.
- **Each correct answer receives exactly 1 mark.** A three-option MCQ and a one-word gap fill are
  worth the same. ([ielts.org — scoring in
  detail](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail))
- **No half marks at item level.** Raw score is an integer 0–40. Half-*bands* come out of the
  conversion table, never out of an item.
- **No negative marking.** A wrong answer costs nothing beyond the mark not gained, so a blank is
  strictly dominated by a guess.
- **No partial credit.** A two-word answer with one word right scores 0.
- **The recording is played once only.** ([ielts.org — Academic Listening test
  format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening))
  This is the module's defining constraint and everything in this briefing follows from it.
- **Approximately 30 minutes of audio, plus 10 minutes to transfer answers** on the paper test.
  On the computer-delivered test there is **no transfer time — 2 minutes to check** instead, because
  the typed answer already *is* the answer sheet.
- **Marks are lost for incorrect spelling and grammar.** ielts.org states this plainly on the
  Listening format page. There is no edit-distance tolerance and no examiner discretion.
- **Word limits are hard**, and ielts.org states the two rules that matter: *"You will lose the mark
  for writing more than the word limit"* and *"Hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single
  words."*
- **Different accents are used** — ielts.org names British, Australian, New Zealand and North
  American. (See §12.3: our pack currently ships UK and US voices only. This is a content gap, not
  an error, but it must be disclosed in the UI.)
- **The exact thresholds vary slightly between test versions.** ielts.org says so explicitly. Our
  table is therefore *indicative*, and every band we display must carry that caveat.

### 1.1 Part-by-part facts we author against

| Part | Interaction | Register | Speakers | Questions | What makes it hard |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | dialogue | everyday transactional (booking, enquiry, form-filling) | 2 | 1–10 | dense factual detail — names, numbers, addresses; spelling and form errors, not comprehension |
| 2 | monologue | everyday informational (tour, announcement, facility briefing) | 1 | 11–20 | no interlocutor to trigger repair; often spatial (map/plan) or sequential |
| 3 | discussion | academic / training (students + tutor) | 2–4 | 21–30 | attribution — *who* held which view; opinion and evaluation rather than fact; overlapping turns |
| 4 | lecture | academic monologue | 1 | 31–40 | no dialogue, no redundancy, unfamiliar topic, candidate fatigue, frequently no mid-part pause |

**Authoring consequence.** The four parts fail for four *different* reasons, so the teaching payload
must differ by part. Part 1 payload is overwhelmingly answer-form. Part 3 payload is overwhelmingly
attribution and stance. Part 4 payload is overwhelmingly structure-marker navigation. A pack that
writes the same style of note for all forty questions has misunderstood the test.

### 1.2 One table for Academic and GT — say this out loud

**Confirmed: Academic and General Training take the identical Listening test and share the identical
raw-score-to-band conversion.** ielts.org publishes a single set of Listening anchor thresholds with
no Academic/GT split, and both partners' guidance treats Listening as format-independent.

This is worth teaching *because Reading is the opposite*: Academic and GT Reading use different
conversions, and a GT candidate needs more correct answers for the same Reading band. The contrast
is a genuinely useful thing for a learner to hold:

> Your Listening band means the same thing whichever test you sat. Your Reading band does not —
> a GT 7.0 in Reading took more correct answers than an Academic 7.0.

**Product hook:** the format toggle (Academic / General Training) must visibly *not* change anything
in the listening module, and a one-line note should say why. Learners who see the toggle everywhere
else assume it applies here too and then distrust their score.

### 2.4 Check against our implementation

Our table lives in `sidecar/bandready/server/routes/listening.py` as `RAW_TO_BAND`, consumed by
`raw_to_band(raw)` and `_band_for(raw, total, attempt)`. **Verified 2026-07-28: `RAW_TO_BAND` matches
§2.1 exactly at every threshold from 2.5 upward.** Three observations for whoever owns that file
(this is a report, not an edit — the file is outside listening-content ownership):

1. **Sub-2.5 collapse.** `raw_to_band` returns `2.0` for every raw below 4, so a learner who gets one
   question right is told "band 2.0" where the published scale gives 1.0. Identical to the reading
   module's issue; identical low priority; identical trivial fix.
2. **`_band_for` scales short attempts onto 40.** It requires `attempt.test_id` and `total >= 20`,
   then computes `round(raw * 40 / total)`. That correctly refuses to band a single-part practice
   attempt — good, and better than the reading module's behaviour. But on a 20-question two-part
   attempt each question is worth **2 projected raw marks**, so one lucky answer can move the
   displayed band by half a band. The projected band must be labelled as an estimate from a short
   sample, with raw + percentage shown as primary.
3. **Band display and the check step.** Because practice-mode partial attempts report raw only, the
   progress engine sees bands from full tests only. That is the right call and should be preserved —
   listening bands from 10-question samples are noise.

### 2.5 What the table means pedagogically — teach this

- **The middle is a swamp and the top is a cliff.** 18–22 is a *five-mark-wide* band 5.5; a learner
  can gain four questions and see no movement at all. Above 30, bands are two marks wide. So:
  **raw score is the primary progress metric and band is secondary**, precisely because raw score
  moves when the band does not. A learner who goes 19 → 22 has improved by 15% and been told
  nothing.
- **Useful conversions for goal-setting:**
  - 5.5 → 6.0 = **1 more question** if you are at 22. **5 more** if you are at 18. Same band, wildly
    different distance — which is why we must show raw.
  - 6.0 → 6.5 = **3 more questions** (23 → 26). One recovered cascade in Part 3 (§8.1) does it.
  - 6.5 → 7.0 = **4 more questions** (26 → 30). Typically: two spelling/plural fixes and two
    correction traps.
  - 7.0 → 8.0 = **5 more questions** (30 → 35), and past 7.5 they must come out of Parts 3–4.
  - Band 8 tolerates **5 wrong out of 40**. Band 9 tolerates **1**.
- **The answer-form arithmetic is the most motivating number in the module.** A typical band-6.5
  candidate loses 2–4 marks to spelling, plurals and word limits alone. That is the entire 6.5 → 7.0
  gap, available without hearing a single extra word of English. Say it in exactly those terms.

## 3. The clock, the once-only rule, and the preview pauses

### 3.1 The hard facts

- ~30 minutes of audio, played **once**, auto-advancing. No pause, no rewind, no seek. There is no
  "budget your time across the parts" decision to make — the audio budgets it for you. **Listening
  is the only paper in IELTS with no time-management problem and, because of that, the only one
  where attention management is the whole game.**
- The candidate writes *while* listening. There is no "answer later" mode. This is why answer-form
  errors are so much commoner here than in Reading: every completion answer is transcribed under
  cognitive load, mid-utterance, while the next question is already being spoken.
- Preview pauses are built into the recording: a set-opening pause (commonly ~30 s) and sometimes a
  mid-part break. Our scripts author these as narrator lines with `pause_after_ms` 20000–45000
  (`docs/plan/07-listening-module.md` §2), which makes the preview pause a *first-class authored
  object* in BandReady — a thing we can teach into, drill, and vary. Exploit that.

### 3.2 Paper vs computer — teach both, simulate one

| | Paper | Computer-delivered (what BandReady simulates) |
|---|---|---|
| During audio | write in the question booklet | type into the answer field |
| After audio | **10 minutes** to transfer to the answer sheet | **2 minutes** to check |
| Dominant end-stage risk | transcription offset — one row wrong destroys a group | none; the typed answer is the answer |
| What the end stage is *for* | transfer + tidy handwriting + fill blanks | form sweep only |

**The 2-minute check protocol — this is a fixed, executable list, not advice:**

1. **Blanks first.** Every empty box gets the most plausible item of its predicted slot type
   (§5.2). Never leave a blank; there is no negative marking.
2. **Word limits second.** Any answer longer than the instruction allows is a certain zero. Cut it
   to the shortest span that still answers. Articles are words.
3. **Plurals third.** Re-read the printed frame around each gap: does `some ___` / `a ___` /
   `two ___` force a number on the noun you wrote? (§4.6)
4. **Doubled answers fourth.** Any box containing two candidates ("Tuesday/Thursday", "gap(s)") is
   marked wrong. Pick one.
5. **Spelling last, and only on words you copied from a spelled-out name.** You cannot re-hear
   anything, so re-checking a word you are unsure of is a coin flip; re-checking a word you *spelled
   out letter by letter* is a real recovery.

Note what is *not* on the list: rethinking a question you got lost on. The audio is gone. Content
recovery is impossible; only form recovery is possible. This distinction is the single most useful
thing to say about the check step and almost nobody says it.

### 3.3 The ordering ratchet — the spine of the module

**Answers appear in the audio in question order.** Always, within a part, across all question types
our schema supports. Consequences, all of which should appear somewhere in the teaching payload:

- At any moment there is exactly **one live question**. If you know which, you know where you are.
- If you hear the answer to Q19, then Q17 and Q18 are **gone**. Stop hunting (§8).
- The gap between two answers bounds your attention: after answering Q13 you can stop straining
  until the Q14 anchor appears. Listening at 100% for 6 minutes is impossible; listening at 100% for
  eight 20-second windows is not.
- Because the ratchet exists, **overshooting your eyes by one question is always safe and never
  costly** — the two-question eye span in §8.3.

### 3.4 The attention arithmetic that justifies the preview protocol

Teach this as a calculation the learner does once.

A part runs 4.5–6.5 minutes and carries 10 marks. The answers occupy perhaps 25–35 seconds of that
audio in total — ten short bursts of 2–4 seconds each. **Roughly 90% of the audio contains no
answer.** Sustained maximal attention across the whole part is both impossible and unnecessary; what
is necessary is being at maximal attention during those ten bursts.

That is the entire argument for prediction and signposting. Prediction tells you *what the burst will
sound like*; signposting tells you *that it is about to start*. Together they convert an
unsustainable 6-minute vigilance task into ten sustainable 20-second ones.

## 4. Spelling and form — the biggest listening-specific scoring factor

A correctly heard answer spelled wrongly scores zero. In Reading the learner copies from a visible
text, so a spelling error is a transcription slip. **In Listening there is nothing to copy from** —
the learner must produce the orthography from the acoustics. That makes this the largest avoidable
loss in the module, and it is why the payload for Part 1 in particular should weight form over
comprehension.

### 4.1 The rules, stated as rules

| # | Rule | Status |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Spelling must be exactly correct. Marking is binary — no half marks, no edit-distance tolerance, no "close enough". | Official (ielts.org Listening format; IDP spelling guidance) |
| F2 | **Standard British and standard American spellings are both accepted.** `colour`/`color`, `organise`/`organize`, `centre`/`center`. Neither is preferred. | Well-corroborated (British Council; IDP; partner guidance) |
| F3 | **Capitalisation is ignored.** `paris`, `Paris` and `PARIS` all score. On paper, block capitals are *advised* — for handwriting legibility, not for marking. On computer it is irrelevant. | Well-corroborated |
| F4 | Word limits are hard and inclusive of articles. Over the limit = 0 even when the content is right. | Official |
| F5 | **A hyphenated compound counts as ONE word** (`check-in`, `part-time`). So does a contraction. | Official (ielts.org names `check-in` explicitly) |
| F6 | Under `AND/OR A NUMBER`, one number is allowed *in addition to* the word allowance. | Official phrasing; our implementation encodes it |
| F7 | Numbers may be written as digits **or** as words; both are accepted. Digits are strictly safer — they cannot be misspelt. | Official-adjacent (IDP) |
| F8 | Dates may be written in any standard order and abbreviation: `5 March`, `March 5`, `5 Mar`, `5/3`, `03/05`. Ordinal suffixes must be well formed — `2nd`, never `2th`. | Official-adjacent (IDP) |
| F9 | Currency and units must match what the gap needs. `$4.50`, `£40`, `40 pounds`, `60 km`, `60 kilometres`, `sixty kilometres` are all legitimate forms — but if the printed frame already supplies the symbol or unit, do not repeat it. | Official-adjacent (IDP) + our own authoring rule |
| F10 | **Singular and plural are different answers.** If the key is plural and you wrote singular, it is wrong. Some keys legitimately accept both; you cannot rely on it. | Teacher consensus, universal |
| F11 | **Writing two candidate answers scores zero.** `Tuesday/Thursday`, `gap(s)`, `fee (fees)` — all wrong. Brackets and slashes belong in answer keys, never in answers. | Teacher consensus, universal |
| F12 | Spoken digit conventions: zero is often said as "oh"; repeated digits as "double four", "triple six". Write the digits. | Official-adjacent (IDP) |

### 4.2 British vs American — what to actually tell the learner

Both are accepted, and this matters more in Listening than in Reading for a specific reason: in
Reading the correct spelling is printed in front of you, so the question never arises. In Listening
the learner hears `/ˈsentə/` and must choose an orthography. Knowing that **both `centre` and
`center` score** removes a real, recurring hesitation at exactly the moment when hesitation costs the
next answer.

Three caveats to teach with it:

- **"Both accepted" does not mean "anything accepted".** `centere`, `colur`, `organice` are wrong.
  The rule licenses two standard forms, not a spectrum.
- **Hybrid forms are risky.** `analyzed` (US verb) with `centre` (UK noun) in the same test is fine
  for marking, but a learner who mixes systems is usually mixing them because they are guessing,
  which is the actual problem.
- **A few "pairs" are not spelling pairs at all.** In British English `practise`/`practice` and
  `licence`/`license` are verb/noun distinctions, not orthographic variants. Do not teach them as
  free variation, and do not auto-generate them as variants in an answer key (§4.5).

### 4.3 Capitalisation — the anxiety tax

Learners spend real attention on this and it earns nothing. Say it flatly:

> Case is not marked. Write however is fastest. On a computer that means normal typing; on paper it
> means whatever you can read back, and block capitals are only recommended so the marker can read
> your handwriting.

Our matcher lowercases both sides (`_fold` in `sidecar/bandready/scoring/answers.py`), so BandReady
already behaves correctly. **Product hook:** the answer field should never show a capitalisation
warning, and the review screen should never highlight a case difference as an error — doing so would
teach a false rule.

### 4.4 Word limits and the over-length lure

The characteristic listening word-limit failure is different from the reading one. In Reading the
learner copies too much of a visible span. In Listening the learner writes **the natural spoken
phrase**, which is almost always longer than the limit, because speech is verbose and gaps are
terse.

> *Worked pattern W1 (original).* Limit: NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS.
> Printed frame: `Bring a ______ on the second day.`
> The speaker says something like: *"…and on day two you'll want to bring one of those small
> waterproof rucksacks, nothing enormous."*
> The tempting answer — `small waterproof rucksack` — is right in content and **zero** on
> submission. The keyable answer is `waterproof rucksack`.
> *Teaching line: the gap wants the shortest phrase that makes the sentence true, not the phrase the
> speaker used. Trim modifiers from the left.*

The mechanical fix, taught as a habit: **before writing, count on your fingers; if you exceed, drop
the leftmost word and check the sentence still works.** Articles are words. Numbers are not (under
`AND/OR A NUMBER`).

### 4.5 What our matcher does and does not do — read this before writing a key

Verified against `sidecar/bandready/scoring/answers.py` on 2026-07-28. This is the contract every
authored answer key is scored under.

**It does, automatically:**

| Behaviour | Effect on a key |
|---|---|
| lowercases, folds curly quotes/dashes, strips punctuation | case and stray punctuation are free |
| word limit checked on the **raw** answer, before article tolerance | `the coal mine` fails a two-word limit that `coal mine` passes — correct |
| hyphenated compound / contraction = 1 word; `twenty two` = 1 number | matches F5/F6 |
| hyphen ≡ space **at match time** | `well being` matches a `well-being` key; **`wellbeing` does not** |
| number words → digits, including year reading (`eighteen ninety-two` → `1892`) | you need not author both digit and word forms of a number |
| `%` ≡ `percent`; `$40` ≡ `40 dollars`, `£`/`€` likewise | currency and percent forms are equated |
| leading `a`/`an`/`the` stripped **only if no stored variant has one** | authoring an article into a variant makes the comparison literal — usually not what you want |
| `(sea) turtles` expands to `sea turtles` **and** `turtles` | parenthesised optionals are free |
| `gap/gaps` expands to `gap/gaps`, `gap`, `gaps` | slash alternatives are free |
| wrong answers 1–2 edits from a variant are tagged `near_miss` | powers the spelling-leak panel and the dictation feed; never awards a mark |

**It does NOT, and this is where keys go wrong:**

1. **US/UK spellings are not auto-equated at match time.** `spelling_variants()` exists and knows ~50
   pairs, but it is *deliberately not called by the matcher* — it is an authoring helper. **A key
   authored as `["centre"]` will mark a learner's `center` WRONG**, in direct contradiction of rule
   F2. Every key containing a word from `US_UK_PAIRS` **must** carry both forms. This is the single
   highest-impact authoring rule in this briefing, and the validator must enforce it.
2. **Singular and plural are not auto-equated.** Correct per F10 — but when both are genuinely
   possible you must author both (`["fee", "fees"]` or `"fee/fees"`).
3. **Closed compounds are not auto-equated.** `wellbeing` must be authored alongside `well-being`.
4. **A currency symbol keyed is a currency symbol required.** `$40` normalises to `40 dollars`, so a
   learner writing bare `40` is marked wrong. Author both (`["$40", "40"]`) whenever the printed
   frame already shows the symbol — otherwise you are testing typography, not listening.
5. **Slash expansion is indiscriminate.** A key of `km/h` expands to `km/h`, `km` **and** `h`, so a
   learner answering `h` scores. Never use `/` inside a unit in a key; write the unit as a
   parenthesised optional or list the variants explicitly. *(Reported upstream — see `problems`.)*

### 4.6 Singular vs plural — a decision procedure, not a rule of thumb

This is worth its own drill. The decision has two independent inputs, and the learner must consult
both:

**Input A — what the printed frame allows.** Read the words around the gap *before* the audio starts:

| Printed frame | Forces |
|---|---|
| `a ___`, `an ___`, `each ___`, `every ___`, `one ___` | singular countable |
| `some ___`, `several ___`, `many ___`, `both ___`, `a range of ___`, `two ___`, `various ___` | plural |
| `much ___`, `the amount of ___`, `a lot of ___` (+ mass) | uncountable — **never** takes `-s` |
| `Number of ___:` | plural noun *or* a bare figure, depending on the column |
| `___ are`, `___ have` | plural subject |
| `___ is`, `___ has` | singular or uncountable subject |
| a table column whose other cells are plural | plural (parallelism) |

**Input B — what the speaker actually said.** The `-s` is acoustically fragile: it assimilates, it
links into the next word, it disappears before another sibilant. Deciding on grammar alone and
ignoring the audio is how a learner writes `forests` when the speaker said `forest`.

**The procedure:** predict the number from the frame during the preview; then *verify* it in the
burst; when the two disagree, trust the frame, because the frame is what the item writer keyed
against. When the frame permits both and the audio was unclear, write what you think you heard — a
guess of the right form beats a hedge, and `forest(s)` scores zero (F11).

> *Worked pattern W2 (original).* Frame: `The centre runs classes in three ______ .`
> `three` forces plural, so the learner is waiting for a plural noun before the audio starts. When
> the speaker says something like *"…there's pottery, printmaking and a bit of bookbinding"*, the
> keyable answer is a category word the learner must supply — and the plural marker on it is a free
> mark for anyone who read the frame and a lost mark for anyone who did not.

### 4.7 The high-risk transcription inventory (authored for BandReady)

Where listening spelling actually breaks, in rough order of frequency. Use these to seed the
dictation pool and the `answer_form_note` field.

1. **Spelled-out surnames and place names.** The whole point of the spell-out is that the word is
   unguessable. Letter pairs that collide acoustically: **A/H/J/K**, **B/P/V/D/E/G/T/C**, **M/N**,
   **I/Y**, **S/F/X**, **U/W**, **L/O**. Doubling ("double L") and hyphens are stated aloud.
2. **Days, months and ordinals.** `Wednesday`, `February`, `Tuesday`/`Thursday` (near-homophonous
   onset under speed), `2nd` not `2th`, `9th` not `9nd`.
3. **-ance/-ence, -ant/-ent.** `attendance`, `maintenance`, `independent`, `insurance`.
4. **Doubled consonants.** `accommodation`, `necessary`, `committee`, `beginning`, `professional`,
   `recommend`, `occurred`.
5. **Silent letters.** `receipt`, `island`, `Wednesday`, `government`, `library`, `February`,
   `restaurant`.
6. **-ough / -augh clusters.** `thorough`, `borough`, `draught`, `through`.
7. **Compound decisions.** `car park` (two words), `timetable` (one), `check-in` (hyphen and one word
   for limits), `part-time`, `well-being`.
8. **Numbers that are words.** `forty` (not `fourty`), `ninety`, `eighth`, `twelfth`. *Our matcher
   accepts `fourty` because `_TENS` includes it as a number token — which means a learner writing
   `fourty` where a bare digit is keyed will still score. Realistic-ish; flagged, not fixed.*
9. **Units.** `metres`/`meters`, `kilometres`/`kilometers`, `litres`/`liters` — all F2 pairs and all
   must be double-keyed.
10. **Homophone hazards in transactional contexts.** `fair`/`fare`, `stationary`/`stationery`,
    `course`/`coarse`, `weekly`/`weakly`, `site`/`sight`, `whether`/`weather`. These are the
    strongest candidates for a dedicated drill, because the acoustic signal genuinely does not
    disambiguate them — only the frame does.

## 5. Prediction — the highest-yield technique in the module

### 5.1 The claim, stated flatly

The preview pause is not reading time. It is the only moment in the test when the candidate can act
rather than react. In it, a band-8 listener converts every gap from *"a word goes here"* into *"a
plural count noun, probably a facility type, probably right after they finish talking about
opening hours"*. That conversion is what makes the answer audible when it arrives, because attention
is not a spotlight you can hold on everything — it is a filter you have to configure in advance.

The mechanism is not mystical: it is the same reason you notice your own name in a noisy room. A
primed listener detects a target category at far lower signal quality than an unprimed one. Field's
work on L2 listening breakdowns points the same way from the other direction — most failures are
perceptual, at the level of segmenting the stream into words, and anything that narrows the search
space at that level buys real accuracy (§9).

**Prediction is not guessing the answer.** A learner who predicts `library` and hears `learning
centre` and writes `library` has made prediction actively harmful. Predict the **slot**, not the
filler.

### 5.2 The slot-type taxonomy — P-codes

Proposed canonical codes so `predicted_slot` is machine-groupable and the drill engine can target a
slot type. Every completion-type question we author gets exactly one.

| Code | Slot | What the learner is listening for | Characteristic hazard |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | **Cardinal quantity** — price, count, capacity, distance, age | a bare figure, usually said once, often with a unit | 13/30, 15/50; missing the unit |
| **P2** | **Code / reference string** — phone, postcode, membership, flight, room number | a digit-and-letter string, said slowly, often with "double" / "oh" | mis-hearing "oh" as a letter; losing a repeated digit |
| **P3** | **Date** | day + month, sometimes year; often preceded by "on the" | ordinal suffix errors; day/month order |
| **P4** | **Time or duration** | clock time, or "for N weeks/minutes" | am/pm; "half past" vs "half to" in some varieties; duration confused with start time |
| **P5** | **Proper name** — person, company, street, institution | spelled out letter by letter, usually once, sometimes twice | pure transcription; the whole answer is orthography |
| **P6** | **Address fragment** | number + street name + type (`Road`/`Lane`/`Avenue`) | the street *type* is part of the answer and is often dropped |
| **P7** | **Singular count noun** | a noun after `a`/`an`/`each`/`one` | writing the plural |
| **P8** | **Plural count noun** | a noun after `some`/`two`/`several`/`a range of` | dropping the `-s` |
| **P9** | **Uncountable / mass noun** | `equipment`, `advice`, `access`, `funding`, `transport` | adding an illegal `-s` |
| **P10** | **Adjective** | after `is`/`are`/`very`/`quite`, or before a printed noun | writing the noun instead |
| **P11** | **Verb** — base after `to`, `-ing` after a preposition, past in a narrative frame | the printed frame fixes the form | right verb, wrong inflection |
| **P12** | **Two-word noun phrase** — modifier + head | the collocation, spoken as one prosodic chunk | writing three words (§4.4) |
| **P13** | **Letter decision** — MCQ / matching | not a gap; a choice between competing candidates all of which get mentioned | choosing the first mentioned (§7) |
| **P14** | **Category label** — the superordinate the speaker never says | the speaker gives instances; the gap wants the class, or vice versa | the paraphrase gap (§5.6) |

### 5.3 The cue table — how the printed frame tells you the slot

This is the concrete, teachable core. A learner who internalises twenty rows of this table can slot-
type an entire question set in fifteen seconds.

| Printed cue around the gap | Slot | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `a ___` | P7 | and `an ___` additionally tells you the answer **begins with a vowel sound** — a genuinely free constraint that eliminates half your candidates |
| `some / several / many / two / a range of / different ___` | P8 | |
| `much / amount of / level of / access to ___` | P9 | never `-s` |
| `Cost / Fee / Price / Charge / Deposit: ___` | P1 | currency form; check whether the symbol is printed |
| `___ per person / per night / per session` | P1 | |
| `Tel / Phone / Ref / Membership no.: ___` | P2 | expect "double" and "oh" |
| `on ___` | P3 or P7 | a day, a date, or a surface/street |
| `at ___` | P4 or a place | clock time if a time column, place otherwise |
| `in ___` | P3 (month/year), place, or P11 (`-ing`) | |
| `by ___` | P11 (`-ing`) or an agent noun | |
| `for ___` (+ duration column) | P4 | |
| `to ___` | P11 base form | |
| `is / are / was / were ___` | P10 or P7 | adjective if the frame reads evaluatively |
| `very / quite / fairly / rather ___` | P10 | |
| `___ + printed noun` (e.g. `___ classes`) | P10 or a modifier noun | P12 territory |
| `Number of ___:` | P8 | the noun is plural; the *value* is P1 |
| a printed unit after the gap (`___ km`, `___ %`) | P1 | write the bare figure only |
| a printed currency symbol before the gap (`$___`) | P1 | do **not** repeat the symbol |
| all other cells in the column are `-ing` forms | P11 | parallelism is a hard constraint |
| the stem asks `Why…` / `What was the reason…` | P13 | expect several reasons mentioned, one endorsed |
| the stem names two people (Part 3) | P13 | attribution trap incoming (§7 L12) |

### 5.4 Predicting magnitude and register, not just class

Two refinements that separate a good predictor from a mechanical one:

- **Plausible magnitude.** If the gap is a seminar-room capacity, `18` and `25` are plausible; `250`
  is not, and `2.5` is nonsense. Setting a plausible range before listening catches mis-heard
  numbers *at the moment of writing*, which is the only moment you will ever have.
- **Register and collocation.** A university handbook does not say `cheap`; it says `subsidised`. A
  tour guide does not say `place where you buy things`; they say `retail units` or `the shops`.
  Predicting the register narrows the candidate set as hard as predicting the word class does.

### 5.5 The preview protocol — 30 seconds, five steps

Executable, timed, drillable. This is what our authored `pause_after_ms` 30000 narrator lines are
*for*, and the practice player should be able to run a preview-only drill against it.

| Time | Step | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | **Read the instruction line.** How many words? Is a number allowed? | the word limit is a scoring rule, and it changes between sets |
| 3–10 s | **Slot-type every gap.** Write one P-code above each. | configures the filter; this is the step that buys the marks |
| 10–20 s | **Underline one anchor per stem** — the word most likely to be spoken verbatim, or the one whose synonym you should expect | gives you a trigger for each burst |
| 20–26 s | **Read the last question of the set.** | you now know where the set ends, which is what stops you following the audio past your own questions |
| 26–30 s | **Look at the first two questions again.** | the first answer often comes within seconds of the audio restarting; Part 1 in particular gives almost no runway |

If the pause is shorter (mid-part breaks are often ~20 s), drop step 3 and 5, never step 2.

**Between-part reset.** The preview pause of the next part is also the moment to abandon the previous
part psychologically. Ten marks just closed; ten untouched marks are opening. A candidate still
grieving Q19 during the Part 3 preview will lose Q21 as well.

### 5.6 Worked prediction examples — original, and the shape the payload should follow

All frames and utterances below are invented for this document.

> **X1 — P8 from a determiner, plus a paraphrase gap.**
> Frame (NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS): `Members may borrow up to six ______ at a time.`
> Prediction: `six` forces **P8**, a plural count noun; and it must be a thing a member borrows.
> Audio (paraphrased, not verbatim): *"…you can take out six items — well, six of the smaller
> tools, anyway. The big machines are booked separately."*
> Key: `hand tools` (or whatever the script establishes). The learner listening for the printed word
> `borrow` hears nothing, because the speaker says `take out`. **The prediction that saves the mark
> is not the word — it is "plural, borrowable object".**

> **X2 — P7 with the `an` constraint.**
> Frame: `Each application must include an ______ .`
> Prediction: **P7**, singular countable, **beginning with a vowel sound**. That eliminates most of
> the candidate set before a syllable is spoken, and it also tells the learner not to write a plural
> even if the speaker uses one in passing.

> **X3 — P1 with a printed unit.**
> Frame (`Write ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER`): `Maximum group size: ______ people`
> Prediction: **P1**, a bare figure, plausible range roughly 6–40. Writing `12 people` re-states a
> word the frame already prints, and against a **`WRITE A NUMBER` / `ONE WORD ONLY`** instruction it
> breaks the limit outright. *(Verified against our matcher: under `{words: 1, numbers: 1}` the
> redundant `12 people` still passes the counter, so the mark is lost only when the key is `12` —
> which it will be. Either way the learner should write the bare figure.)* This is a one-line
> teaching note that recurs across every table-completion set we will ever author.

> **X4 — P5, where the answer is pure orthography.**
> Frame: `Contact name: ______`
> Prediction: **P5**. The learner should expect a spell-out and should be poised to write letters,
> not a word. The correct behaviour is unusual and must be taught explicitly: **stop trying to
> understand and start transcribing.** A name is the one answer type where comprehension is
> irrelevant and the whole mark is in the transcription.

> **X5 — P11 fixed by parallelism.**
> Frame, a notes column whose other rows read `checking the timetable`, `booking a slot`:
> `Before the session: ______ the equipment`
> Prediction: **P11**, `-ing` form. A learner who writes `check` has heard perfectly and scored zero.
> *Teaching line: when the other rows agree on a form, the gap has no freedom.*

> **X6 — P9, the uncountable trap.**
> Frame: `The grant covers travel and ______ .`
> Prediction: **P9** is likely (`accommodation`, `equipment`, `insurance`) because it is coordinated
> with the uncountable `travel`. A learner who writes `accommodations` has lost the mark to a rule
> they know.

> **X7 — P13, where prediction is about the *competition*, not the answer.**
> Stem: `Why did the group change their meeting day?` with three options printed.
> Prediction: **all three options will be mentioned in the audio.** That is what an IELTS-style MCQ
> does. So the prediction is not "which is right" but "I am listening for the one that is *endorsed*,
> not the ones that are merely *raised*." Preload the endorsement markers from §6.5.

### 5.7 What this means for the schema

Every completion-type question we author should carry:

- `predicted_slot`: a P-code from §5.2.
- `slot_cue`: the exact printed substring that determines it (`"six"`, `"an"`, `"per person"`).
- `plausible_range` (P1/P2/P3/P4 only): the magnitude band a learner should expect.
- `paraphrase_link`: `{audio: "take out", printed: "borrow"}` — the wording change the learner must
  bridge. This is the listening analogue of the reading module's `paraphrase_devices`, and it powers
  a "predict the synonym" drill that needs no new content.

Every P13 question should carry `distractor_mentions`: which options are spoken and where, so the
review screen can show *"all three were mentioned — here is the one that was endorsed."*

## 6. Signposting — the inventories a learner can memorise

### 6.1 Why signposting is the listening analogue of paraphrase recognition

In Reading, the skill that decides the band is recognising that two differently-worded propositions
are the same. In Listening you rarely get to compare propositions at all — you get one pass, in real
time. What you get instead is **metadiscourse**: the speaker constantly announcing what they are
about to do. *"Right, so the other thing you'll need…"* is not content. It is a flag saying an answer
is starting in about 1.5 seconds.

The inventories below are closed sets. There are perhaps 150 items in total, they recur in every
listening text in English, and they are learnable in a fortnight — the same argument the reading
briefing makes for its 200-item quantifier/modal hinge set, and with the same very high
marks-per-item.

Codes are proposed so `signpost_code` is machine-groupable.

### 6.2 Inventory A — answer-imminent markers (`SG-A`)

The answer is arriving within a clause. Teach these as "sit up" triggers.

- **Delivery frames:** *the ___ is…*, *that'll be…*, *it comes to…*, *we're looking at…*, *you'll
  need…*, *what you want is…*, *there's one on…*, *it's held in…*
- **Instruction frames:** *make a note of…*, *do bring…*, *don't forget…*, *you'll have to…*,
  *make sure you…*, *the main thing is to…*
- **Confirmation frames:** *just to confirm…*, *so that's…*, *let me read that back…*, *so we've got
  you down for…* — **the single most reliable answer marker in Part 1**, because it repeats the
  correct value after any correction has already happened (§6.3).
- **Dictation cues:** *that's spelt…*, *shall I spell that?*, *double-…*, *hyphen*, *all one word*,
  *with a K* — these mean the very next thing is a P5/P2 answer and must be transcribed letter by
  letter.
- **Emphasis / evaluation:** *the important thing is…*, *what really matters…*, *crucially…*, *above
  all…*, *the key point here…* — in Parts 3–4 these mark the endorsed option in an MCQ.
- **Repetition of the printed stem's noun.** Not a phrase but a pattern: when the speaker uses the
  question's own anchor noun, the answer is within one clause either side.

### 6.3 Inventory B — correction and retraction markers (`SG-B`)

**The most valuable list in this document.** These announce that a value already stated is being
replaced. The candidate who has written the first value must overwrite it; the candidate who has
not written anything yet must wait.

- **Self-repair:** *sorry*, *no, wait*, *hang on*, *hold on*, *actually*, *in fact*, *I mean*,
  *or rather*, *that is*, *let me rephrase that*, *scratch that*, *did I say…? I meant…*
- **Second thoughts:** *on second thoughts*, *thinking about it*, *actually, do you know what*,
  *no, let's make it…*, *better make that…*
- **Correction by the interlocutor:** *actually it's…*, *it's not X, it's Y*, *are you sure? I
  thought…*, *that's changed*, *not any more*, *that was last year*
- **Temporal update:** *we used to… but now…*, *up until last term…*, *as of September…*, *it's just
  been moved to…*, *originally… but these days…*, *that's been replaced by…*
- **Concessive reversal:** *we do have X, but most people go for Y*, *X is available, although…*,
  *you could do X — mind you, Y is probably better*
- **Rejection of an offer:** *I'd rather…*, *I'd prefer…*, *I'm not keen on…*, *that's a bit…*,
  *I'll give that a miss*, *maybe not that one*
- **Numerical repair:** *forty — sorry, fourteen*, *it was £40, it's £45 now*, *no, that's the
  weekday rate*

**The rule that falls out of the whole inventory, and the single line most worth putting in the
product:**

> **The answer is the last value stated for that slot before the speaker moves on. Never the first.**

**Authoring constraint (falls out of our TTS pipeline, §6.7): every one of these must be marked
lexically in a BandReady script, never by stress alone.**

### 6.4 Inventory C — structure markers (`SG-C`) — the Part 4 map

Part 4 is a lecture with no interlocutor. The only navigation aid is the lecturer's own outline.

- **Framing:** *today I want to look at…*, *I'll be talking about…*, *I'll start by…*, *let me set
  the scene*, *before I get to X, a word about Y*
- **Sequencing:** *first(ly)*, *to begin with*, *next*, *then*, *after that*, *the second/third
  thing*, *moving on*, *turning now to*, *which brings me to*, *finally*, *lastly*, *one last point*
- **Sub-topic close/open:** *so much for X — let's look at Y*, *having covered…*, *that's the
  background; now the interesting part*
- **Exemplification:** *for instance*, *take X, for example*, *a case in point*, *to illustrate*,
  *say you were to…*
- **Cause and result:** *as a result*, *consequently*, *which meant that*, *this led to*, *hence*,
  *the upshot was*
- **Contrast:** *on the other hand*, *by contrast*, *whereas*, *conversely*, *however*, *then again*
- **Digression and return:** *incidentally*, *by the way*, *as an aside*, *but anyway*, *as I was
  saying*, *to get back to*, *where was I* — **teach this pair explicitly**: the digression is nearly
  always answer-free, so it is a legitimate place to breathe; the return marker means resume full
  attention.
- **Summary and close:** *so, to sum up*, *in short*, *essentially*, *the point I'm making is*,
  *that's all I have time for*, *next week we'll look at…*

### 6.5 Inventory D — endorsement and selection markers (`SG-D`) — the MCQ decider

When several candidates are mentioned, these say which one counts.

- **Endorsement:** *the main reason*, *above all*, *primarily*, *chiefly*, *what swung it was*, *in
  the end we went with*, *the deciding factor*, *if I had to pick one*
- **Downgrading a mentioned candidate:** *to some extent*, *partly*, *that was a factor, but*,
  *less because of X than because of Y*, *not so much X as Y*
- **Enumerated sets:** *there are three options*, *either… or*, *both… and*, *neither*, *on the one
  hand… on the other*
- **Agreement / disagreement in Part 3:** *exactly*, *that's what I thought*, *I'm not so sure*,
  *I'd question that*, *fair enough, but*, *I take your point, though* — these carry the
  **attribution** that Part 3 items are built on (§7 L12).

### 6.6 Inventory E — negation and exclusion markers (`SG-E`)

Missing one of these inverts the answer. They are grammatically light, unstressed, and skipped under
load — exactly the reading module's quantifier problem, transposed.

- *apart from*, *except for*, *other than*, *with the exception of*, *rather than*, *instead of*,
  *as opposed to*, *unlike*
- *isn't*, *doesn't*, *won't*, *no longer*, *not until*, *hardly*, *rarely*, *seldom*, *barely*
- Hidden negatives: *fails to*, *lacks*, *far from*, *anything but*, *stops short of*
- Restrictives: *only*, *just*, *merely*, *the only one that*, *nothing but*
- Conditional restriction: *unless*, *provided that*, *as long as*, *subject to*

### 6.7 Prosodic signposts — and the hard constraint our TTS imposes

In real recorded speech, corrections are marked prosodically as well as lexically: a pitch reset, a
pause, and **contrastive stress** on the replacing item (*"not TUESDAY — THURSDAY"*). Competent
listeners use the prosody more than the words.

**Kokoro will not deliver contrastive stress reliably.** Our audio is line-by-line neural TTS
stitched with authored silence (`sidecar/bandready/audio/tts_render.py`, `stitch.py`); prosody is
sentence-internal and flat across a repair. This has three binding consequences for every listening
script we author:

1. **Every correction must be lexically explicit.** If the only signal that Thursday replaces Tuesday
   is stress, our audio does not contain the signal and the item is broken. Use an `SG-B` phrase
   every time.
2. **Substitute silence for the prosodic break.** A `pause_after_ms` of **400–600 ms** before the
   repair, and 250–300 ms after it, reads convincingly as a repair boundary even with flat delivery.
   Author it deliberately; do not let the default 300 ms flatten a correction into a list.
3. **Never build an item on a stress-only minimal pair.** `thirteen` vs `thirty`, `fifteen` vs
   `fifty` are distinguished in real speech mainly by stress placement and vowel length. With TTS
   they may be genuinely ambiguous — which makes the item unfair rather than difficult. If a script
   needs one, disambiguate it in the line: a confirm-back turn (*"one three, or three zero?" "One
   three — thirteen."*) is realistic dialogue and removes the ambiguity. This is a fairness rule, not
   a style preference.

**Corollary for the teaching payload:** we should still *teach* prosodic correction-spotting, because
learners will sit a test with real recordings. Put it in the strategy copy and in the accent drill,
and label it honestly: "our audio marks corrections with words; the real test also marks them with
stress — here is what that sounds like."

## 7. Distraction handling — the trap taxonomy

This is where the marks are lost, and it is the heart of the listening teaching payload. Every
question we author should be tagged with the trap it is built on, and the distractor value should be
recorded so the review screen can say *"you wrote the thing they said first."*

Proposed canonical codes, `L1`–`L14`.

| Code | Trap | What the audio does | The handling rule to teach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L1** | **Self-correction** | speaker states X, then replaces it with Y | wait for the topic to move on before committing; the last value wins (§6.3) |
| **L2** | **Correction by the other speaker** | A says X; B says *"actually it's Y"* | in a dialogue, the person who *owns* the information is authoritative — the agent about the timetable, the caller about their own name |
| **L3** | **Temporal update** | *"it used to be X; from this term it's Y"* | tense is the signal. A past-tense value is almost never the answer to a present-tense frame |
| **L4** | **Rejected option** | an option is offered and declined (*"I'd rather not do the Tuesday one"*) | listen for preference verbs, not just nouns (§6.3) |
| **L5** | **Candidate crowding** | three dates / three prices in one turn, only one answers the question | hold the *question*, not the values; ask "which of these is the one the frame asked for?" |
| **L6** | **Adjacent-slot bleed** | the answer to Q13 is spoken while the learner is still on Q12 | the ordering ratchet: if you hear a Q13-shaped item, Q12 has gone (§3.3, §8) |
| **L7** | **Verbatim decoy** | the printed stem's exact keyword is spoken *next to the wrong value* | the keyword locates the region, never the answer; verify against the frame's grammar |
| **L8** | **Paraphrase gap** | the answer's trigger is a synonym; the printed word is never spoken | predict the synonym in the preview (§5.6 X1); this is the commonest *silent* failure — the learner never even knows the burst happened |
| **L9** | **Number confusion** | 13/30, 15/50, or a figure restated in another form (*"a quarter of a million"*) | confirm-back turns in our scripts; magnitude plausibility in the learner's head (§5.4) |
| **L10** | **Spelling trap** | a name spelled out with a non-obvious letter, a doubled consonant, or a hyphen | stop comprehending, start transcribing (§5.6 X4) |
| **L11** | **Negation / exclusion** | *"everything's included apart from the ferry"* | the `SG-E` inventory (§6.6); mark negation words in the preview |
| **L12** | **Attribution swap** (Part 3) | two students hold different views; the stem asks whose | track speaker identity as hard as content; a voice change is a data point |
| **L13** | **Over-length lure** | the natural phrase is four words; the limit is two | trim from the left; count before writing (§4.4) |
| **L14** | **Plausible-but-unasked** (MCQ) | an option is stated and true, but does not answer the stem's actual question | re-read the stem's question word — *why* vs *what* vs *when* decides it |

**Authoring rules that follow:**

- `docs/plan/07-listening-module.md` §10 already requires "at least 4 questions" per part to carry a
  correction pattern. **Raise it to a distribution requirement:** each part must contain at least one
  `L1`, at least one from `{L2, L3, L4}`, at least one `L8`, and at least one `L11` or `L14`. A part
  built entirely on self-correction teaches one reflex and no discrimination.
- **Never make every question a trap.** A part where all ten items are traps trains paranoia and
  produces a learner who overwrites correct answers. Roughly 4–6 trapped items per part is
  exam-realistic; the rest should be clean.
- **Record the distractor.** Every trapped question needs `distractor_value` (the wrong thing said
  first) and `distractor_line_index`, so the review screen can play both lines back-to-back. *That
  back-to-back replay is the listening equivalent of the reading module's `option_diagnosis` and the
  speaking module's band-graded model — it is the highest-value single feature in the whole review
  experience,* because it shows the learner the exact 3 seconds where their mark was lost.

## 8. Recovery — why one miss costs three, and what to do instead

### 8.1 The cascade, with numbers

The failure mode is mechanical, not emotional. The candidate misses Q17. They keep listening for
Q17's answer. The audio, obeying the ratchet, has already moved to Q18's zone and then Q19's. By the
time the candidate realises, three marks are gone and they are also now anxious, which costs Q20.

**One miss, four marks.** And four marks is:

- 26 → 30, the entire **6.5 → 7.0** gap;
- 30 → 34, most of the **7.0 → 7.5** gap.

Say it in exactly those terms. *"The most expensive thing you can do in this test is care about a
question you have already lost."*

### 8.2 What makes the cascade so hard to break

Three things, all worth naming so the learner recognises them in themselves:

1. **The sunk-cost pull.** The learner has invested attention in Q17 and does not want to write it
   off. But the audio does not care, and there is no scenario in which Q17 comes back.
2. **The absence of a stop signal.** In Reading you can see you are lost. In Listening, being lost
   feels exactly like listening — the audio continues, the voices are still talking, nothing tells
   you that you have drifted out of your own question set.
3. **Reading and listening compete.** Re-reading a stem you missed consumes the same working memory
   that decoding the current utterance needs. Hunting backwards is not free attention; it is
   attention taken directly from the live question.

### 8.3 The recovery protocol — six steps, executable under pressure

1. **Declare the miss out loud (internally). "Gone."** The decisive act is the concession, and it has
   to be deliberate, because nothing external will prompt it.
2. **Mark the box, do not fill it.** A dot, a dash, a single character. It costs half a second, it
   makes the box findable in the check step, and it stops you thinking about it now.
3. **Jump forward, and overshoot.** Move your eyes to the **next** question — and if you are unsure
   how far the audio has travelled, the one after that. The audio is ahead of you by definition; the
   only safe direction is forward. **Overshooting is free** (the ratchet guarantees the answer has
   not passed yet); undershooting costs another mark.
4. **Re-anchor on a signpost, not on a word.** Do not scan the audio for a keyword — keywords are
   paraphrased (`L8`) and you will not hear one. Wait for an `SG-C` structure marker or an `SG-A`
   delivery frame. These are frequent, unmistakable, and topic-independent, which is exactly what you
   need when you no longer know the topic.
5. **Two-question eye span, permanently.** The habit that prevents the cascade rather than curing it:
   always have the live question *and* the next one in view. If you miss one, the next one's frame is
   already loaded and catches you.
6. **Fill the blank at the check step, never during.** In the 2 minutes, put the most plausible item
   of the predicted slot type in every empty box. It costs nothing and it is occasionally right.

### 8.4 Guessing well is a skill

Because there is no negative marking, the only wrong guess is no guess. But guesses can be
better than random:

- **Completion gaps:** write a plausible filler of the predicted slot type. If you know it is a
  plural count noun about facilities, write one. You will not often hit, but you will sometimes, and
  it costs one second.
- **MCQ:** if two options were definitely mentioned and one was definitely rejected, guess between
  the remaining two. If nothing at all was heard, pick the option you have used least in the set —
  a weak but free prior.
- **Matching:** in a no-reuse set, guess a letter you have not used.
- **Never write two answers to hedge.** It converts a possible mark into a certain zero (F11). This
  is the one place where "more effort" is actively negative, and learners do it constantly.

### 8.5 Part-specific re-anchoring

| Part | What to re-anchor on | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the form-field labels being spoken (*"and your postcode?"*) | Part 1 audio narrates its own answer sheet — the structure is the form |
| 2 | sequence and place words (*"as you come out of…"*, *"the next stop"*) | Part 2 is usually a tour or itinerary; the route is the map |
| 3 | **speaker change** | a new voice very often means a new sub-topic and a new question zone; voice identity is free navigation data |
| 4 | `SG-C` structure markers | the lecture outline is the only map; the topic words are unfamiliar by design |

**Part 4 deserves its own paragraph in the product copy.** It is the part where recovery matters
most and is hardest: no dialogue means no repair, no repetition, no clarification request, and often
no mid-part pause. The candidate is also 25 minutes in. Teach three things and nothing else:
(a) preview the whole set, because there may be no second pause; (b) navigate on structure markers,
because you will not know the vocabulary; (c) accept losses fast, because a Part 4 cascade eats the
last marks of the test with nothing after it to recover into.

## 9. The decoding floor — what strategy cannot fix

Everything above is the strategy layer. Underneath it is a perceptual layer, and it is worth being
honest with authors about where the boundary is.

**The finding.** L2 listening breakdowns are substantially *perceptual* rather than
comprehension-level: listeners fail to segment the continuous speech stream into words, and a single
mis-segmentation can derail the interpretation of a whole stretch of discourse. Field's work on
lexical segmentation makes this case and argues for teaching perception directly rather than treating
listening as a comprehension exercise with more practice attached
([Field 2003, *ELT Journal*](https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article-abstract/57/4/325/455304);
[Field 2008, *TESOL Quarterly*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00139.x)).
Classroom work in the same tradition finds that explicit bottom-up activities, measured by dictation,
produce improvements attributable to the intervention
([Siegel & Siegel 2015, *SSLLT* 5(4)](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/4322)).

**What this means for us, concretely.** The connected-speech phenomena that eat listening answers:

- **Weak forms.** Function words reduce to schwa; `for` and `four` become near-identical, and `to`,
  `at`, `of` carry the frame information a P-code prediction depends on.
- **Linking and elision at word boundaries.** The plural `-s` links into a following vowel and stops
  sounding like a plural; a final `-t` or `-d` disappears before a consonant, taking the past tense
  with it.
- **Assimilation.** `ten pounds` surfaces closer to `tem pounds`.
- **Numbers.** `-teen` vs `-ty` is a stress and length contrast, not a segmental one (§6.7).
- **Letters in spell-outs.** The acoustically confusable sets in §4.7 are pure perception; no
  strategy touches them.

**The honest framing for the product:** prediction, signposting and recovery raise the score of a
learner who *can* decode the stream. They do not create decoding ability. A learner whose dictation
accuracy is low needs bottom-up work, and our dictation mini-mode is the right instrument
(`docs/plan/07-listening-module.md` §9). **It should be prescribed on evidence, not offered as a
menu item** — the `near_miss_spelling` tag already gives us the trigger, and a learner with ≥2 near
misses in an attempt should be routed straight into a dictation session seeded with exactly those
cue lines.

**And one uncomfortable finding we should not hide.** Field's IELTS-commissioned study of the
lecture-based Listening question found substantial *test-specific* behaviour: candidates adopting
routines tailored to the test method and exploiting the availability of the questions in written
form, with some participants finding unrestricted lecture listening *less* demanding than the test
([Field, IELTS Research Reports — cognitive validity of the lecture-based
question](https://ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/the-cognitive-validity-of-the-lecture-based-question-in-the-ielts-listening-paper)).

Read plainly: a great deal of what we are about to teach *is* test-wiseness. That is legitimate — the
learner needs the band — but it means two things for the product. First, we should not claim our
strategy module makes anyone a better listener in general; it makes them a better test-taker, and the
dictation and accent drills are what make them a better listener. Second, the honest version is more
motivating anyway: *"these techniques are for the exam; the dictation drill is for your English. Do
both."*

## 10. Why candidates plateau — cause, mechanism, fix, product hook

Ten causes. For each: the mechanism, the fix the learner performs, the hook in our product.

### 10.1 Losing the thread

**Mechanism.** §8.1 — one miss, then continued hunting, then three or four marks. The commonest
single cause of a band-6 score in a candidate who understands the audio fine.
**Fix.** The §8.3 protocol, drilled until the "gone" concession is automatic.
**Product.** A **cascade detector** in the review screen: consecutive wrong answers following a
single miss are a distinct diagnostic pattern, and we can name it — *"you lost Q17, then 18 and 19.
That one miss cost you three marks."* This is far more actionable than "you got 3 wrong", and we
already have the per-question data to compute it. This is, in my judgement, the highest-value single
analytic in the listening module.

### 10.2 Spelling and transcription errors

**Mechanism.** The answer was heard and lost at the keyboard. Highest risk on P5 (names), P2 (codes),
and the §4.7 inventory.
**Fix.** Transcribe letter-by-letter for spell-outs; write digits rather than number words; do not
"tidy" a name you are unsure of.
**Product.** The `near_miss` tag already exists in `answers.py` and must be surfaced as a *category*
in results — "you lost 3 marks to spelling" — never folded into a generic wrong count. Route those
items into dictation automatically (§9).

### 10.3 Exceeding the word limit

**Mechanism.** Writing the natural spoken phrase instead of the minimal one (§4.4). Compounded by
writing under time pressure with no chance to review mid-flow.
**Fix.** Count before writing; trim from the left; articles are words.
**Product.** A live word counter on each input, and — critically — the **check step must list
over-limit answers explicitly**. Those are certain zeros the learner can still fix in the 2 minutes,
and telling them so is not a correctness hint, so it is legitimate even in exam mode.

### 10.4 Writing what was said first

**Mechanism.** `L1`–`L4`. The learner commits at the first plausible value, then stops attending, and
never registers the repair.
**Fix.** The commit rule: *write it, but do not stop listening until the topic moves on.* Physically,
that means being willing to overwrite — which learners resist, because overwriting feels like
undoing work.
**Product.** The back-to-back distractor replay in review (§7). Nothing else teaches this as fast as
hearing the two lines consecutively.

### 10.5 Collapse in Parts 3 and 4

**Mechanism.** Parts 1–2 are transactional and concrete; Parts 3–4 are academic, abstract, opinion-
laden and unfamiliar. A candidate scoring 9–10 in Parts 1–2 and 4–5 in Parts 3–4 lands on 26–29 —
band 6.5 — and can practise whole tests forever without moving, because the practice keeps rehearsing
the half they are already good at.
**Fix.** Weight practice toward Parts 3–4 well beyond their 50% share of the questions. Drill
attribution (`L12`) and structure markers (`SG-C`) specifically.
**Product.** Per-part accuracy must be a first-class number in the results screen, and the
recommended next action should be part-targeted. Our script rows already carry `part`, so a
"Part 4 only" drill is a query, not new content.

### 10.6 Panicking in Part 4

**Mechanism.** No dialogue, no redundancy, unfamiliar topic, 25 minutes of accumulated fatigue, and —
on many sets — no mid-part pause to regroup. Anxiety then consumes the working memory that decoding
needs, which makes comprehension genuinely worse, which raises anxiety.
**Fix.** Reframe the expectation: **you are not supposed to understand a Part 4 lecture.** You are
supposed to catch ten short bursts inside it (§3.4). Navigate on `SG-C`, accept losses fast, and
remember that band 8 tolerates five wrong answers across the whole test.
**Product.** A Part-4-only practice mode with the preview pause extended in practice (never in exam
mode), plus explicit copy setting the expectation before the first mock.

### 10.7 Not using the preview pause

**Mechanism.** The candidate reads the questions *while the audio plays*, which means reading and
decoding compete for the same resources, which means both degrade.
**Fix.** The §5.5 protocol, as a timed drill.
**Product.** A **preview-only drill** is nearly free to build: show a question set, run a 30-second
timer, ask the learner to slot-type each gap, then reveal the P-codes we authored. No audio needed,
30 seconds per rep, directly targets the highest-yield technique. Build this.

### 10.8 Listening for words instead of meaning

**Mechanism.** `L8`. The learner waits for the printed keyword; the speaker paraphrases; the burst
passes silently. The learner does not even know they missed it, which is why this failure is so
persistent — it generates no feeling of difficulty.
**Fix.** Predict the synonym set in the preview, not the word.
**Product.** A **synonym-prediction drill** built from the `paraphrase_link` field (§5.7): show the
printed stem, ask for three ways a speaker might say it, reveal what our script actually says.

### 10.9 Ignoring the printed frame

**Mechanism.** Plurals, articles, printed units and parallel columns all constrain the answer, and
all are visible before the audio starts. Learners treat the frame as scenery.
**Fix.** The §5.3 cue table, drilled to automaticity.
**Product.** In review, when a wrong answer differs from the key only in number or in an extra
printed unit, diagnose it as `form` and not as `comprehension`. That distinction changes what the
learner does next.

### 10.10 Practising by re-taking tests

**Mechanism.** Repeating whole mocks measures the plateau; it does not treat it. And listening mocks
are expensive — 35 minutes for 40 data points, most of them from parts the learner already passes.
**Fix.** After every attempt, one question: *which of the sixteen §0 items lost me the most marks?*
Then drill that.
**Product.** The post-attempt screen must end in a **single recommended action** — dictation, preview
drill, Part 4 drill, correction drill — chosen from the diagnostic categories above, not a table of
percentages.

*(Two circulating statistics — "58% of test-takers score between 5.5 and 6.5 in Listening" and "67%
are stuck at 6.0–6.5" — appear only in commercial preparation blogs with no traceable source. They
are plausible-sounding and unverifiable. **Do not use them in product copy.** The pedagogical point
stands without a number: the middle bands are wide, so plateaus there are partly an artefact of the
conversion table (§2.5).)*

## 11. Band 6 vs band 8 — the behavioural contrast

IELTS publishes no listening-specific band descriptors; the nine-band scale is general. **The table
below is our own clean-room behavioural model**, calibrated to what the raw scores require: band 6 =
23–25/40 (15–17 wrong), band 8 = 35–36/40 (4–5 wrong). Twelve questions, roughly three per part.

The gap is *not* "understands more English". It is nine specific behaviours, every one of them
trainable.

| | Band 6 listener | Band 8 listener |
|---|---|---|
| **The preview pause** | reads the questions in order, gets through about half, treats it as rest | works a fixed 30-second protocol; slot-types every gap; reads the last question of the set |
| **What they are waiting for** | "the answer" | a plural count noun, roughly 6–40, arriving after a delivery frame |
| **The printed frame** | scenery | a constraint list — determiner, printed unit, column parallelism, negation |
| **Triggering** | waits for the stem's keyword; misses paraphrased bursts without noticing | expects the wording to change; triggers on meaning and on `SG-A` frames |
| **Corrections** | writes the first value and stops attending | writes the first value and keeps attending until the topic shifts; overwrites without hesitation |
| **Attention shape** | tries to listen to everything, exhausted by Part 3 | idles between answer zones, spikes on signposts; still sharp in Part 4 |
| **Missing one** | keeps hunting; loses the next two or three | says "gone", marks the box, jumps forward one *or two* questions, re-anchors on a structure marker |
| **Part 3 attribution** | knows the opinion, not whose it was | tracks the speakers as hard as the content; treats a voice change as navigation |
| **Part 4** | tries to understand the lecture; panics when the topic is unfamiliar | tracks the outline via sequencing markers; content-agnostic |
| **Writing** | writes the natural phrase; hedges with two answers when unsure | writes the minimal legal form; counts words; never hedges |
| **Spelling** | tidies up half-heard names | transcribes spell-outs letter by letter; writes digits not number words |
| **The check step** | re-thinks hard questions they cannot possibly recover | sweeps blanks → word limits → plurals → doubled answers, in that order |
| **Self-knowledge** | "I'm bad at listening" | "I lose Part 3 attribution items and I drop plural markers under speed" |

**The line worth putting in the product:** *a band 8 listener is not hearing more of the recording
than you. They are listening hard for about thirty seconds of it, in ten places they identified
before the audio started — and when they miss one, they let it go.*

## 12. Direct instructions to the listening authoring agents

Distilled from everything above. These are the concrete asks.

### 12.1 Per-question teaching payload — minimum viable set

`docs/plan/07-listening-module.md` §2 already carries `cue_line_index`, and §11 mentions an
`explanation` column on `listening_questions`. That is not enough to teach listening. Fill these on
every question:

| Field | Value | Powers |
|---|---|---|
| `cue_line_index` | the line containing the answer (already specced) | transcript highlight, dictation, review |
| `predicted_slot` | a P-code from §5.2 | the preview drill; per-learner slot-type analytics |
| `slot_cue` | the printed substring that determines the slot (`"six"`, `"an"`, `"per person"`) | the prediction teaching note; the cue-table drill |
| `plausible_range` | for P1–P4: the magnitude band a learner should expect | magnitude-plausibility coaching |
| `paraphrase_link` | `{audio: "...", printed: "..."}` | the synonym-prediction drill; `L8` teaching |
| `signpost` | the exact fragment that announces the answer, verbatim from the script | "here is what to listen for"; the signpost drill |
| `signpost_code` | `SG-A` … `SG-E` (§6) | groupable analytics; targeted drills |
| `trap_code` | `L1`–`L14` (§7), or explicitly `none` | the trap drill; the "why was I wrong" prompt |
| `distractor_value` + `distractor_line_index` | the wrong value said first, and where | **the back-to-back replay — the single highest-value review feature** |
| `answer_form_note` | the specific form risk (plural / spelling / limit / unit) | the form-sweep coaching; targeted dictation |
| `recovery_note` | if you miss this one, the next handhold is *X* | the recovery drill; Part 4 payload |

**Rules for the prose fields.** `explanation` for a listening item must not be a location report. It
must follow this shape: **what you should have predicted → what announced it → what the audio
actually said → what the trap was → how to write it.** An explanation that says "the answer is in
line 42" fails review, because on test day there is no line 42.

**Say `none` when there is no trap.** Not every item is a trap; pretending otherwise trains paranoia
and produces learners who overwrite correct answers (§7).

### 12.2 Group-level rules to obey while authoring

- **Distribution of traps per part:** at least one `L1`; at least one of `{L2, L3, L4}`; at least one
  `L8`; at least one of `{L11, L14}`. Roughly 4–6 trapped items per part, never ten.
- **Slot-type diversity per part:** at least one P1 or P2, at least one P5 (spelled-out name — Part 1
  especially), at least one P8 and one P7 in the same part so plural discrimination is actually
  tested, and at least one P9 somewhere in the test.
- **Every key containing a `US_UK_PAIRS` word must carry both spellings.** The matcher does **not**
  auto-equate them (§4.5.1). This is the most likely way for our pack to mark a correct answer wrong.
  The verify agent must check this mechanically.
- **Author both singular and plural** whenever the frame genuinely permits both — `"fee/fees"` works,
  because slash alternatives auto-expand.
- **Never put a `/` inside a unit in a key** (`km/h` expands to `h`, §4.5.5). Use explicit variants.
- **Never key a currency symbol that the printed frame already shows**, or key both forms.
- **Every completion answer must be a span the script actually speaks**, within the stated word limit.
  If the natural utterance has no ≤N-word span that answers the gap, fix the script or the limit —
  never the key.
- **Every correction is lexically marked** with an `SG-B` phrase and preceded by a 400–600 ms
  `pause_after_ms` (§6.7). No stress-only corrections. No stress-only minimal pairs unless
  disambiguated by a confirm-back turn.
- **Part 4 scripts must contain a dense `SG-C` skeleton** — at minimum an opening frame, three
  sequencing markers, one digression-and-return pair, and a summary marker — because those markers
  *are* the recovery handholds we promise in §8.5, and if they are not in the script the teaching is
  a lie.
- **Part 3 scripts must make attribution decidable from the audio alone**: distinct named speakers,
  at least one genuine disagreement, at least one `L12` item.
- **Part 1 scripts must include at least one spell-out with a doubled letter or a hyphen**, because
  that is the `L10`/P5 teaching we owe.
- **Preview pauses are content.** Author them at 30000 ms for a set opening and 20000 ms for a
  mid-part break, and make sure the set they precede is previewable in that time.

### 12.3 Copy we owe the learner, outside the item payload

- A **preview protocol** one-screen briefing (§5.5) before the first mock.
- A **spelling and form** briefing (§4) — the highest marks-per-word copy in the module.
- A **correction-trap** briefing built on the `SG-B` inventory (§6.3).
- A **recovery** briefing with the cascade arithmetic (§8.1) stated in band terms.
- A **Part 4 expectations** note (§10.6): you are not supposed to understand the lecture.
- A **paper vs computer** note (§3.2): 10-minute transfer vs 2-minute check, and what each is for.
- An **accent disclosure.** ielts.org states the real test uses British, Australian, New Zealand and
  North American accents. Our pack ships UK and US Kokoro voices, with `au` falling back to British
  (`sidecar/bandready/audio/tts_render.py`). We must say so plainly rather than let a learner infer
  they have heard the range. Suggested copy: *"BandReady's synthesised voices cover British and North
  American English. The real test also uses Australian and New Zealand speakers — plan extra
  exposure."*
- The **band-table caveat** on every band we display, plus the Academic/GT note from §1.2.

- [IELTS scoring in detail — ielts.org](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail) — 40 questions, 1 mark each, whole and half bands, the four Listening anchor thresholds (band 5 = 16, band 6 = 23, band 7 = 30, band 8 = 35), and the statement that the marks needed vary slightly from version to version. A single Listening table with no Academic/GT split.
- [IELTS Academic: Listening test format — ielts.org](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening) — ~30 minutes plus 10 minutes transfer; recordings heard once only; marks lost for incorrect spelling and grammar; "you will lose the mark for writing more than the word limit"; "hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single words"; British, Australian, New Zealand and North American accents used.
- [How to use numbers in your IELTS test — IDP](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-how-to-write-numbers-in-ielts) — digits preferred over number words in Listening; accepted date formats; currency and measurement forms; well-formed ordinals (`2nd`, not `2th`); "oh" for zero and "double"/"triple" for repeats.
- [IELTS common spelling mistakes — IDP](https://ielts.idp.com/canada/prepare/article-ielts-common-spelling-mistakes) — misspelt answers marked wrong in Listening and Reading.
- [IELTS Listening band scores — IDP India](https://ieltsidpindia.com/information/ielts-band-scores/listening) — full conversion table; one mark per answer; no negative marking.
- [British or American English in the IELTS test — British Council](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/blog/british-or-american-english-ielts) — both varieties accepted; no preference; consistency advised as good practice rather than required. *(Cited from search summary; the page timed out on direct fetch — treat the specific wording as unquoted.)*
- [How computer-delivered IELTS works — IDP](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-how-computer-delivered-ielts-works) — typed answers, no transfer period, 2 minutes to check at the end of Listening.

**Peer-reviewed / research reports (authority for §9, and the honesty note in §9):**

- [Field, *Promoting perception: lexical segmentation in L2 listening*, ELT Journal 57(4) (2003)](https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article-abstract/57/4/325/455304) — perceptual processes and lexical segmentation as the locus of L2 listening breakdown.
- [Field, *Bricks or mortar: which parts of the input does a second language listener rely on?*, TESOL Quarterly 42(3) (2008)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00139.x) — reliance on content vs function words in the speech stream.
- [Field, *The cognitive validity of the lecture-based question in the IELTS Listening paper*, IELTS Research Reports](https://ielts.org/researchers/our-research/research-reports/the-cognitive-validity-of-the-lecture-based-question-in-the-ielts-listening-paper) — verbal-protocol evidence of test-specific and test-wise behaviour, and candidates exploiting the written availability of the questions. The source of §9's honesty note.
- [Siegel & Siegel, *Getting to the bottom of L2 listening instruction: making a case for bottom-up activities*, Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching 5(4) (2015)](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/4322) — six bottom-up activities, dictation-measured gains attributable to the intervention. The evidence base for our dictation mini-mode.
- [Goh, *Metacognitive instruction for second language listening development*, RELC Journal 39(2) (2008)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688208092184) and [Goh & Hu, *Metacognitive awareness and listening performance*, Language Awareness 23 (2014)](https://www.academia.edu/12097975/) — metacognitive knowledge (person, task, strategy) and its relationship to listening performance; the framing behind §10.10 and §11's self-knowledge row.
- [*Validity of the listening module of IELTS: multiple sources of evidence*, Language Testing in Asia (2018)](https://languagetestingasia.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40468-018-0057-4) — construct-validity evidence for the IELTS Listening paper.
