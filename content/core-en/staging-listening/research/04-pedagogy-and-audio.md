## 0. Four theses, stated up front

**Thesis 1 — listening is the one skill where the input is gone before the thinking starts.**
Reading lets the learner re-read; writing and speaking let them revise. Listening gives one pass at
roughly 160–180 words per minute and then removes the evidence. Every teaching device we build has
to respect that the *decision* happens in real time and the *learning* happens afterwards, against
a transcript. A "worked solution" that says "the answer was in line 34" is nearly worthless here,
because the learner already knows they missed line 34. What they do not know is **why their ear
did not stop there**.

**Thesis 2 — most lost marks in listening are decoding failures, not comprehension failures.**
The learner knows the word. They did not *hear* the word, because in connected speech it was not
pronounced the way they store it. `ˈaftə ðə lˈast` is not "after the last" as a dictionary lists
it. The research consensus is that L2 listeners must consciously learn to segment the speech
stream, a skill L1 listeners acquire in infancy and never notice
([bottom-up decoding literature](https://awej.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/18.pdf);
[connected-speech phonology review, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2025.2472474)).
This means our drills must include exercises where the *only* task is transcription, with no
comprehension component at all.

**Thesis 3 — the distinctive trap of this paper is the self-correcting speaker, and it is a
listening-only trap.** In reading, a text that says X then says "actually Y" leaves both X and Y on
the page and the learner can weigh them. In listening the learner has already written X, the pen
has moved on, and Y arrives while their attention is on the next question. Coaching sources
converge on self-correction as the single most frequent distractor family
([Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/common-traps-in-ielts-listening-distractors-self-corrections-and-similar-options);
[ieltsbuddy](https://www.ieltsbuddy.com/ielts-listening-distractors.html);
[BAND9AI](https://band9ai.com/blog/ielts-listening-distractors-right-answer-wrong)). Nothing in the
reading module teaches it. We have to build it from nothing.

**Thesis 4 — our audio is synthesized, so the script is not the deliverable; the render is.**
A script that reads beautifully and renders as mush has taught nobody anything. Part B documents,
with measurements, the exact forms that survive Kokoro and the exact forms that do not. **One of
them is already broken in the four scripts we ship** (§7.1). Authoring agents should treat Part B
§6–§8 as a hard style guide, not as advice.

# PART A — PEDAGOGY

## 1. Intensive versus extensive listening: what each is for

The literature does not crown a winner, and the honest reading is that they do different jobs.

| Finding | Source | What it implies for us |
|---|---|---|
| Comparative study: the **intensive** group improved on both raw scores and Rasch person-ability estimates; the **extensive** group improved on raw scores but *not* on ability estimates | [Comparing the effectiveness of L2 extensive and intensive listening approaches](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353667601_Comparing_the_Effectiveness_of_L2_Extensive_and_Intensive_Listening_Approaches) | Raw-score gains without ability gains is test-wiseness. Intensive work is what moves the underlying construct — which is what a band is meant to measure. Intensive is our core. |
| Other studies find the pre/post gain **larger for extensive** groups | [L2 listening homework: intensive vs extensive](https://www.academia.edu/45388408/L2_Listening_Homework_Intensive_vs_Extensive) | Volume matters too, and it is cheap. But we cannot ship a podcast library. |
| Consensus position across the reviews: **combine them**; listening is a composite competence and neither mode alone develops it | [Developing L2 listening comprehension through extensive and intensive listening](https://benjamins.com/catalog/aila.22015.kar) | Ship intensive in-app; make extensive a *prescription* the app issues and tracks, not content the app hosts. |
| Extensive listening plus **metacognitive scaffolding** (journals, reflection) beats unstructured exposure | [Listening journals for extensive and intensive listening](https://americanenglish.state.gov/files/ae/resource_files/etf_54_2_pg02-11.pdf); [mobile extensive listening + metacognition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1871187124001949) | Bare "go listen to the BBC" is not a feature. "Log 20 minutes, then answer three reflection prompts" is. |

**Design conclusion.** BandReady owns intensive listening: short, high-density segments, replayed,
transcribed, dissected. Extensive listening is a coached habit we schedule and log — a checklist
item with a reflection prompt — not a media library we host. This keeps the pack small and stays
inside the copyright line, because we never rehost anyone's audio.

### 2.1 How to grade dictation usefully

Word Error Rate is the standard metric and is computed as a Levenshtein distance over tokens
([WER intro](https://medium.com/nlplanet/two-minutes-nlp-intro-to-word-error-rate-wer-for-speech-to-text-fc17a98003ea)).
But WER's known pathology is that it weights all errors equally
([Speechmatics on the problem with WER](https://www.speechmatics.com/company/articles-and-news/the-problem-with-word-error-rate-wer)),
and for a *learner* the error classes are pedagogically very different. A raw WER percentage tells
the learner nothing they can act on.

**Grade into four buckets, not one number.** Align learner text to reference text with a
token-level Levenshtein alignment, then classify each mismatch:

1. **Missed function word** — reference token is in a closed-class stopword set (`of, to, a, the,
   have, been, are, was, and, that, for, from, at, in`) and the learner omitted it. *Diagnosis: weak-form
   deafness.* This is the highest-signal bucket and the one learners never notice on their own.
2. **Wrong word, right sounds** — substitution where the pair is phonetically close (share
   ≥60% of characters, or are a known confusable). *Diagnosis: segmentation error.*
   e.g. reference "a spare set", learner "a spirit". Route to a connected-speech drill.
3. **Right word, wrong spelling** — substitution with Levenshtein distance ≤2 against a
   non-dictionary learner token. *Diagnosis: orthography, not hearing.* Crucially: **in the
   exam this scores zero but in the diagnostic it must be praised as a hearing success.** The two
   have to be reported separately or the learner draws the wrong lesson.
4. **Blank / nothing heard** — a run of ≥3 consecutive deletions. *Diagnosis: overload or
   drop-out.* Route to recovery training (§5), not to vocabulary.

We already have the primitive for bucket 3: `near_miss()` in
`sidecar/bandready/scoring/answers.py` computes a capped Levenshtein at distance ≤2. Do not write a
second one — extend that module, as reading already shares it.

**Dose.** Dictation is exhausting. 20–40 seconds of audio per set, replay unlimited, is the right
unit. A whole part is not.

## 3. Bottom-up drills: the connected-speech features that eat marks

The features that destroy comprehension of known vocabulary are well catalogued: weak forms,
elision, assimilation, linking/liaison, and reduced verbal phrases. Even advanced learners are
typically *unconscious* of them, so awareness-raising is a real intervention rather than a
platitude ([Oxford/OUP on bottom-up decoding](https://teachingenglishwithoxford.oup.com/2016/01/20/bottom-up-decoding-listening/);
[BUP for decoding](https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/elt/article/download/0/0/48082/51674)).
Learners do not need to *produce* these features; they need to know they occur
([connected-speech implications for L2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2025.2472474)).

The drill families worth building, each about 90 seconds:

- **Weak-form gap-fill.** Play a 4–8 word chunk; the content words are printed, the function words
  are blanks. `I'll be ___ ___ ___ minutes` → "there in ten". This isolates exactly bucket 1 above.
- **Count-the-words.** Play a chunk, ask "how many words?" before asking what they were. Learners
  who answer 4 to a 7-word chunk have a segmentation problem, and the number makes it visible in a
  way transcription does not.
- **Same-or-different minimal pairs in context.** We already have a `pron_pairs.jsonl` table with
  `a`, `b`, `contrast`, `sentence_a`, `sentence_b`. That asset is currently pointed at speaking;
  it is equally a listening discrimination drill and costs nothing extra to reuse.
- **Number and letter discrimination.** The `-teen` / `-ty` contrast (fifteen/fifty, thirteen/
  thirty) is the most reliable single trap in Part 1 and Part 2 and is purely acoustic — stress on
  the first syllable versus the second. Build it as its own drill; it is high frequency and
  trivially generated.
- **Boundary drill.** Present the same chunk twice with different segmentations and ask which was
  said: "an aim" vs "a name", "ice cream" vs "I scream", "grade A" vs "grey day". Original pairs
  only; these are ours to author.

**Caution about our own pipeline.** Kokoro does produce weak forms and linking — the phonemizer
output in §6 shows `ˈaftə ðə lˈast` with a proper schwa, and `ɒnðə` for "on the" run together as
one unit. So these drills are authorable. But synthesized speech does not elide as aggressively as
a tired human at 4pm, so the drill is a *floor*, not a ceiling. Say so in the UI rather than
pretending otherwise.

## 4. The review step: what a wrong answer must trigger

### 4.1 The frame: diagnostic, not judgemental

Field's central argument is that fixating on *comprehension* — the product — has made teaching
ignore the *process*. His diagnostic approach treats answers as **information about learner
behaviour rather than a verdict on performance**
([Field 2008, process approach ch. 7](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/listening-in-the-language-classroom/process-approach/F9971FB50E0ACB0483864872CACAEF28);
[chapter 6, dividing listening into components](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/listening-in-the-language-classroom/dividing-listening-into-its-components/590F1304C855EB03634F86E71B00AB23);
[review](https://tesl-ej.org/wordpress/issues/volume13/ej51/ej51r3/)).

That is a direct instruction for our review screen: **the first thing shown after a miss must be a
question, not an explanation.** "What happened here?" with four buttons:

> ◦ I didn't hear it at all
> ◦ I heard it but wrote it too late / was still on the last question
> ◦ I wrote the first thing I heard, and they changed it
> ◦ I heard it right but spelled it wrong

Each maps to a different remedy and a different drill. This costs one tap and yields a genuinely
new diagnostic that no score can produce. It also mirrors the reading module's cause-before-reveal
decision, which the reading briefing identified as the difference between a log and a lesson.

### 4.2 Why replay-with-transcript beats another test

Holzknecht et al. (2024) is the cleanest evidence: a second play raised scores by **1.03 points on
a 0–10 scale** across all proficiency levels, improved performance on both multiple-choice and
note-completion, and **reduced listening anxiety**
([Repeating the listening text, TESOL Quarterly](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.3249)).
Self-paced listening specifically gives candidates a chance to recover information they missed the
first time through anxiety or processing load
([self-paced vs single-play listening tests](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15434303.2024.2435811)).

Replaying with the transcript in view converts an unanalysable failure into an observable one. The
learner sees the words *and* hears them, and the gap between the two is the lesson: "that is what
'a set of' sounds like at speed." Simply checking against an answer key cannot do this, because it
never shows the acoustic form ([IELTS Liz on transcripts](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-listening-transcripts/)).

**The rule: never let a learner leave a wrong answer without hearing the moment again.**

### 4.3 Working with transcripts without turning listening into reading

The failure mode is obvious and common: the learner reads along and feels competent, having
practised nothing. Guard against it structurally.

- **Gate the transcript.** It does not exist until the attempt is submitted. (The existing UI
  already gates answers; extend the same gate.)
- **Audio-first replay, text second.** On a missed question, replay the clip with the transcript
  *blurred*. Reveal on tap, or after the second replay. The learner must try to hear it before
  they are allowed to read it.
- **Highlight-only-the-window.** Show ±1 sentence around the answer, not the whole part. A full
  transcript invites reading; a 12-second window invites listening.
- **Transcript-with-blanks as the default view.** The paragraph is shown with the function words
  removed. Now reading the transcript *is* a listening exercise, because the learner has to supply
  what they read past.
- **Shadowing as the terminal step.** Shadowing has good evidence, strongest on pronunciation
  (d≈0.89) and fluency (d≈0.71), with reported comprehension gains too
  ([Hamada 2016, who benefits and how](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168815597504);
  [shadowing with senior high EFL learners](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1479870.pdf)).
  We already have a microphone and faster-whisper in the app for speaking; shadowing a 15-second
  clip and scoring the ASR against the known transcript is a small increment on existing
  infrastructure.

### 4.4 Prediction, before the audio

Vandergrift's metacognitive pedagogical cycle — predict, verify, reflect — has repeatedly beaten
controls on comprehension, with **less skilled listeners gaining most**
([Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari 2010, Language Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2009.00559.x);
[metacognitive sequence with less-proficient listeners](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218754942);
[self-directed online metacognitive practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10666279/)).

For our format, prediction is concrete and gradeable, which is what makes it a feature rather than
a slogan. Before a gap-fill item, the learner can be asked to commit to the **grammatical shape of
the answer**:

> `The tour leaves from the ________ entrance.`
> What are you listening for? ◦ a number ◦ a name ◦ an adjective ◦ a plural noun ◦ a time

This is auto-gradeable against a `predict` field on the question, takes four seconds, and directly
trains the behaviour that separates competent candidates from anxious ones. It also makes the
30-second preview gap — which currently is dead air in our renders — into an active task.

## 5. Recovery: the skill nobody teaches

L2 listeners who miss the opening of a text struggle to build context for everything that follows —
a genuine cascade, not just a lost mark
([listening strategies with varied test tasks](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ806789.pdf)).
Test anxiety compounds it by consuming working memory
([self-paced vs single-play](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15434303.2024.2435811)),
and anxious learners typically *know* the strategies but fail to deploy them under load.

So recovery must be trained as a motor habit, not explained as advice. Two buildable drills:

- **Abandon-and-jump.** A part is played with one question deliberately unanswerable at normal
  attention (the answer is a rapid aside). The learner is scored not on that item but on the
  **two items after it**. Debrief explicitly on the cascade: "you lost Q17. You also lost Q18 and
  Q19, and those were easy." This makes the cost visible, which advice never does.
- **Signpost tracking.** Play a part with the questions hidden; the learner's only job is to tap a
  button each time they hear the speaker move to a new topic. Scored against authored signpost
  timestamps. This trains position-keeping, which is the actual recovery mechanism — you re-enter
  by recognising where the speaker now is, not by remembering what you missed.

The exam habit to teach alongside: write *something* immediately and move on. A blank is worth the
same zero as a guess, and the guess sometimes scores.

## 6. Speed and accent tolerance without subtitles as a crutch

Accent familiarity has a real, measured effect: comprehension is highest with familiar accents,
lower with mismatched, lowest with unfamiliar
([White & Treenate, accent familiarity](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313396083_The_Effects_of_Accent_Familiarity_on_English_as_a_Foreign_Language_Students'_Word_Recognition_and_Comprehension_of_the_English_Language));
unfamiliar accented English measurably depresses EFL listening scores
([Journal of Psycholinguistic Research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-018-9562-y)).
Listeners *do* adapt rapidly to a new accent, though typically not all the way to familiar-accent
levels ([speech familiarity and comprehension](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-017-1297-5)),
and exposure alone does not eliminate the penalty — it needs to be attended to.

Practical programme:

- **Rotate accents within a test, not just between tests.** A test where Part 1 is British and
  Part 3 is American is more useful than four British parts, because the adaptation cost is what
  we are training. Our renderer supports per-speaker `accent` already (see §7.2).
- **Speed laddering, not speed shock.** Kokoro's `speed` parameter is a usable dial (§8.4).
  Ladder 0.9 → 1.0 → 1.1 across a drill set. Note honestly that Kokoro's speed control scales
  duration; it does not re-plan prosody the way a human speaking slowly would enunciate more. So
  slow mode aids *decoding practice*, not *natural slow speech modelling*.
- **Subtitles as a decaying scaffold.** Subtitle research shows matched captions help comprehension
  but the help is condition-dependent
  ([differential effects of subtitles on connected speech](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244020924378)).
  Our design should let the transcript exist but make it *cost* something: first replay is
  audio-only, second replay unlocks blanked text, third unlocks full text. The learner always tries
  the ear first.

# PART B — AUTHORING FOR OUR TEXT-TO-SPEECH PIPELINE

## 7. What the pipeline actually is

Read in full: `sidecar/bandready/audio/tts_render.py` (572 lines) and
`sidecar/bandready/audio/stitch.py` (268 lines).

### 7.1 Pipeline, exactly

Per script, `render_script()`:

1. Resolves every speaker to a concrete voice (`resolve_voices`).
2. Synthesizes **each line independently**, cached at `media/tts-lines/<sha24>.wav` keyed on
   `(voice, text, speed)` — so editing one line re-renders only that line (`line_cache_key`).
3. Concatenates with authored pauses (`stitch.stitch`), recording sample-accurate offsets.
4. Writes `media/listening/<audio_hash>.wav` + `<audio_hash>.timing.json`, registers both in
   `media_files` for LRU eviction.

**Hard facts, all confirmed in source:**

| Property | Value | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Output format | **float32 mono → 16-bit PCM WAV, 24 000 Hz** | `stitch.TARGET_RATE = 24_000`; `write_wav(..., subtype="PCM_16")` |
| Loudness | RMS-normalised to **−16 dBFS**, true-peak clamped to **−1.5 dBFS**, measured over voiced samples only (`|x| > 1e-4`) so long preview gaps don't inflate gain | `stitch.normalize_loudness` |
| Pause range | `pause_after_ms` clamped to **[0, 60 000]** | `stitch.clamp_pause`, `MAX_PAUSE_MS` |
| Pause default | **300 ms** when the key is absent | `tts_render.py:518` |
| Timing JSON | `{schema_version, sample_rate, duration_ms, lines:[{index, start_ms, end_ms, pause_after_ms}]}` plus `audio_hash, accent_set, accent_label, script_id, voices, part` | `StitchResult.timing_document()` + `render_script` update |
| Offset maths | from **sample counts**, never summed float durations — timing.json cannot drift from the audio | `stitch.stitch`, docstring invariant |
| `start_ms` semantics | start of the *spoken* line; the pause belongs to the line *before* it | `stitch.stitch` loop |
| Cache invalidation | `script_audio_hash` = sha256 over `{schema_version, accent_set, lines:[{voice, text, pause}]}`, first 16 hex | `tts_render.script_audio_hash` |
| Re-titling / editing a question | does **not** invalidate audio | same — only voice/text/pause are hashed |
| ffmpeg / pydub | **not used**; numpy + soundfile only | `stitch.py` module docstring |

**Practical consequence for authors:** you can freely edit `title`, `scenario`, `questions`, and
any teaching payload without triggering a re-render. Changing a single character of `text`, a
`pause_after_ms`, or a voice forces a re-render of that line only. Rewrite teaching payloads
fearlessly; rewrite `lines` deliberately.

- **Best UK male pair:** `bm_lewis` (98) + `bm_george` (150) — 52 Hz.
- **⚠ The `au` cast has the worst collision in `VOICE_MAP`:** `male_1 = bm_daniel` (131.5) and
  `male_2 = bm_fable` (124.4) are **7 Hz apart**. A two-male Australian Part 3 is effectively one
  voice. Recommend swapping `au/male_2` to `bm_lewis`.
- **`au` and `uk` share a narrator** (`bm_george`), so the "different accent" drill opens with an
  identical voice. Recommend `bm_daniel` for the `au` narrator.
- **US expansion:** with 15 unused American voices we can build a genuinely distinct second US
  cast without touching the UK ones.

F0 is not the only cue — `af_nicole` is distinguishable mostly by *rate* — but it is the one that
predicts confusability best, and it is cheap to check before shipping a script.

### 7.4 Leading and trailing silence — your authored pause is not the pause

**Measured** (mean over three short utterances, `lang="en-gb"`, `trim` at its default `True`):

| Voice | Lead silence | Trailing silence | Effective gap for `pause_after_ms: 300` |
|---|---|---|---|
| `bm_george` | 36 ms | 114 ms | ~450 ms |
| `bf_emma` | 37 ms | 97 ms | ~434 ms |
| `bm_lewis` | 41 ms | 111 ms | ~452 ms |
| `bf_lily` | 41 ms | 119 ms | ~460 ms |
| `bm_daniel` | 39 ms | 144 ms | ~483 ms |
| `bf_alice` | 50 ms | 188 ms | ~538 ms |
| `bf_isabella` | 35 ms | **286 ms** | ~621 ms |
| `bm_fable` | **103 ms** | **538 ms** (max 754 ms) | **~941 ms** |

Kokoro's `create()` already defaults `trim=True` and `tts_render` does not override it; with
`trim=False` the residual roughly doubles (measured: `bm_fable` 158 ms → 286 ms on one utterance).
So this is the *trimmed* floor, not something we can configure away.

**Consequences:**

1. Authored pauses are **lower bounds**, and the error is voice-dependent by up to ~800 ms.
2. `bm_fable` is unusable for snappy turn-taking. Every one of its turns lands with a near-second
   of dead air. Avoid it for interactive dialogue; it is acceptable for monologue (Part 4).
3. For natural conversational overlap-free turn-taking, author **`pause_after_ms: 150–250`** for
   fast exchanges, not 300–350. The current shipped script uses 250–350 throughout, which after
   trailing silence renders as 350–650 ms gaps — slightly stilted, but acceptable.
4. **Do not** try to compensate per-voice in the content. Fix it once in code by trimming to a
   fixed floor in `stitch`, and let authors write the pause they mean (§C-7).

### 7.5 Length limits and speaking rate

**Measured**, `bm_george`, `lang="en-us"`:

| Input length | Duration | chars/sec |
|---|---|---|
| 113 chars | 7.38 s | 15.3 |
| 341 chars | 20.44 s | 16.7 |
| 569 chars | 32.23 s | 17.7 |
| 911 chars | 49.71 s | 18.3 |
| 1367 chars | 74.56 s | 18.3 |

**No truncation and no error at 1367 characters / 74 seconds.** The 510-token ceiling that older
kokoro-onnx builds warned about did not fire here. Long monologue lines are safe.

But note the **rate is not constant**: short lines run ~15 cps, long ones ~18 cps. And number-heavy
lines are far slower — measured `cps=3.9` for `"Call 0117 496 0384."` and `5.5` for `"at 6.30 pm"`,
against `19.3` for a long clause.

**This breaks `stitch.estimate_speech_ms`,** which assumes a flat 15 cps. It over-estimates prose by
~20% and under-estimates digit-dense lines by up to **4×**. That function feeds mock synthesis and
the lint bounds (07 §10). If any lint gate uses it to check "does this part fit in the exam's
timing", it will pass scripts that are minutes too long. Flagged as a code issue in the summary;
authors should not rely on any predicted duration for a line containing digits.

**Authoring rule:** keep a single `line` under ~350 characters. Not because Kokoro fails, but
because a line is the unit of pausing (§8.3) and the unit of transcript highlighting — a 70-second
line makes the transcript panel useless and gives the learner no seek granularity.

## 8. What renders well, and what does not

### 8.1 ⚠ Spelled-aloud names — the shipped format is broken

This is the most important finding in the document. Spelled names are a Part 1 staple and we
currently render them unintelligibly.

**The shipped script `ls_t1_p1` contains the line:**

> `"Of course. O-K-A-F-O-R. Okafor."`

**Measured** with `bm_lewis`:

| Form | Phonemes (en-gb) | ASR heard |
|---|---|---|
| `O-K-A-F-O-R.` *(shipped)* | `ˈəʊkˈeɪɐˈɛfˈəʊˈɑː` | **"OK FOA, or CAFA"** |
| `O. K. A. F. O. R.` *(fix)* | `ˈəʊ. kˈeɪ. ˈeɪ. ˈɛf. ˈəʊ. ˈɑː.` | **"O-K-A-F-O-R"** ✅ |

The hyphens are stripped and the letter names are concatenated into a single pseudo-word with no
boundaries. A candidate cannot possibly transcribe it. Same result for an invented surname:

| Form | ASR heard |
|---|---|
| `That's B-R-A-D-S-H-A-W.` | "That's BRDSH-W." ❌ (two letters lost) |
| `That's B R A D S H A W.` | "That's B.R.A.D.S.H.W." ❌ (the A is lost) |
| `That's B. R. A. D. S. H. A. W.` | "That's B-R-A-D-S-H-A-W." ✅ |
| `That's B, R, A, D, S, H, A, W.` | "That's B-R-A-D-S-H-A-W." ✅ |

**Why space-separation fails:** the letter **A** standing alone is phonemized as the *article*
`ɐ` ("uh"), not the letter name `ˈeɪ`. Measured across all 26 letters:

| Notation | Result |
|---|---|
| `A A` (space) | `ɐ ˈeɪ` — **first A becomes the article** ❌ |
| `I I` (space) | `aɪ ˈaɪ` — unstressed, weak |
| `R R` (space) | `ˈɑːɹ ˈɑː` — inconsistent |
| `A.` (dot) | `ˈeɪ.` ✅ — **and all 26 letters are correct in dot form** |
| `A-A` (hyphen) | `ˈeɪˈeɪ` — correct phonemes, **zero separation** ❌ |

Real-world confirmation: `"The Y M C A on Lower Bridge Street."` → `wˈaɪ ˈɛm sˈiː ɐ` — the final A
becomes "uh". Must be `Y. M. C. A.`

> ### 🔴 AUTHORING RULE 1 — spell with dots and spaces, never hyphens
> ```
> ✅ "It's Okafor. O. K. A. F. O. R."
> ✅ "Double-barrelled — that's Hyde. H. Y. D. E. Then Ferrand. F. E. R. R. A. N. D."
> ❌ "It's Okafor. O-K-A-F-O-R."
> ❌ "It's Okafor. O K A F O R."
> ```
> Lower case with dots (`o. k. a. f. o. r.`) renders identically and is also acceptable, but use
> upper case for author readability. All-caps *without* dots does **not** trigger spelling —
> `BRADSHAW` renders as the word `bɹˈadʃɔː`.

Note the transcript/UI implication: the *display* transcript should show `O-K-A-F-O-R` (what a
human would write) while the *synthesis* text is `O. K. A. F. O. R.` These want to diverge. See
§C-6 for the `say_as` proposal; until that exists, author the dotted form and accept it in the
transcript, since it reads acceptably.

### 8.2 ⚠ Numbers — digits are read as cardinals, not as digit strings

**Measured:**

| Written | Phonemes (en-gb) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| `Call 0117 496 0384.` | `zˈiəɹəʊwˈɒnwˈɒnsˈɛvən fˈɔːhˈʌndɹɪdən nˈaɪnti sˈɪks zˈiəɹəʊθɹˈiːˈeɪtfˈɔː` | ❌ "zeroonewunseven", then **"four hundred and ninety-six"**, then "zerothreeeightfour" |
| `Call oh one one seven, four nine six, oh three eight four.` | clean, digit by digit | ✅ |
| `oh two oh, double seven, four one` | `ˈəʊ tˈuː ˈəʊ, dˈʌbəl sˈɛvən, fˈɔː wˌɒn` | ✅ ASR recovered "020 7741" |
| `treble two, four five` | `tɹˈɛbəl tˈuː, fˈɔː fˈaɪv` | ✅ |
| `It's 12.5 kilometres.` | `twˈɛlv.fˈaɪv` | ❌ **"point" is silently dropped** → "twelve five" |
| `It's twelve point five kilometres.` | correct | ✅ |
| `It costs £42.50.` | `pˈaʊnd fˈɔːti tˈuː.fˈɪfti` | ❌ "**pound** forty-two fifty" — symbol read as a *prefix* word |
| `It costs forty-two pounds fifty.` | correct | ✅ |
| `Membership is £30 a year` | `pˈaʊnd θˈɜːti` | ❌ "pound thirty" |
| `thirty pounds a year` | correct | ✅ |
| `The postcode is B4 7QT.` | `bˈiː fˈɔː sˈɛvən kjˌuːtˈiː` | ✅ "B four seven Q T" |
| `Room A4, on the second floor.` | `ɹˈuːm ˌeɪ fˈɔː` | ✅ |
| `It's flat 2B.` | `flˈat tˈuː bˈiː` | ✅ |
| `on 14 March 2019` | `fˈɔːtiːn mˈɑːtʃ tˈuː θˈaʊzənd ən nˈaɪntiːn` | ✅ 21st-century years only |
| `in 1994` | `nˈaɪntiːnhˈʌndɹɪdən nˈaɪnti fˈɔː` | ❌ **"nineteen hundred and ninety-four"** — no one says this |
| `extension 4021` | `fˈɔː θˈaʊzənd ən twˈɛnti wˈɒn` | ❌ "four thousand and twenty-one" |
| `room 2019` | `tˈuː θˈaʊzənd ən nˈaɪntiːn` | ❌ a room is not a year |
| `a 1500 word essay` | `wˈɒn θˈaʊzənd fˈaɪvhˈʌndɹɪd` | ✅ a genuine quantity reads correctly |
| `It's the 1st of October.` | `ðə fˈɜːst ɒv ɒktˈəʊbə` | ✅ |
| `at 6.30 pm` | `sˈɪks.θˈɜːti pˌiːˈɛm` | ✅ the `.` is a prosodic break; reads "six thirty" |
| `we pushed it back to 6.45` | `sˈɪks.fˈɔːti fˈaɪv` | ✅ "six forty-five" — `H.MM` times are safe |
| `half past nine` / `a quarter to six` | correct | ✅ |
| `It's number 15, no wait, 50.` | `fˈɪftiːn ... fˈɪfti` | ✅ the -teen/-ty contrast renders correctly |

> ### 🔴 AUTHORING RULE 2 — write numbers the way they are *said*
> - **Phone / reference / account numbers → words, always.** `"oh two oh, double seven, four one"`.
>   Never a digit run.
> - **Decimals → "point".** `"twelve point five"`. Never `12.5`.
> - **Money → words with the unit after.** `"forty-two pounds fifty"`. Never `£42.50`.
> - **Clock times as `H.MM` are safe** — `6.30`, `6.45` read as "six thirty", "six forty-five".
> - **Years: 2000+ only.** `2019` ✅ but `1994` renders "nineteen *hundred and* ninety-four" ❌.
>   For a 20th-century year write `"nineteen ninety-four"`.
> - **Any other 4-digit number is wrong** — extension, room, reference, membership. `extension 4021`
>   becomes "four thousand and twenty-one". Write `"four oh two one"`.
> - **Room / flat / postcode / dates / ordinals are safe as digits** *when short*: `B4 7QT`,
>   `flat 2B`, `14 March 2019`, `the 1st of October`. Genuine quantities are safe at any length
>   (`a 1500 word essay` ✅).
> - **`double` and `treble` work** and are worth using — they are a real listening skill.
> - The `-teen`/`-ty` contrast synthesizes correctly, so it is a legitimate item.
>
> Keep the **answer key** in the learner's written form (`020 7741`, `12.5`, `£42.50`) while the
> **script text** is the spoken form. These are already separate fields; use that.

### 8.3 ⚠ Punctuation controls prosody, not pauses

**Measured** (bf_emma, "I went there / yesterday / it was closed", internal gaps >80 ms):

| Punctuation | Duration | Internal silence |
|---|---|---|
| none | 2.01 s | **none** |
| commas | 2.03 s | **none** |
| full stops | 2.20 s | **none** |
| semicolons | 2.07 s | **none** |
| `-` dash | 2.01 s | **none** — the dash is stripped entirely |
| newline `\n` | 2.01 s | **none** |
| `...` ellipsis | 2.28 s | 108 ms |

Longer sentences *do* sometimes produce a gap — `"It's on the fourth floor. Sorry, no, the fifth."`
gave 282 ms at the full stop — but it is inconsistent and not controllable.

> ### 🔴 AUTHORING RULE 3 — the only reliable pause is `pause_after_ms`
> Punctuation changes phrasing, stress and duration (a full stop lengthened the utterance by 9%),
> which is worth using for *naturalness*. It does **not** give you a dependable silence.
> If a pause matters — a hesitation before an answer, a beat before a correction, the 30-second
> question preview, the 10-second review gap — **split the line** and set `pause_after_ms`.
> The dash `-` is worse than useless: it is dropped from the audio while remaining in the
> transcript, so audio and transcript diverge. Use a comma or an ellipsis instead.

### 8.4 Speed, hesitation, and self-correction

**Speed** (measured, `bf_emma`, same sentence): 0.8 → 4.14 s, 0.9 → 3.48 s, 1.0 → 3.31 s,
1.1 → 3.16 s, 1.25 → 2.82 s. Monotonic and usable; the parameter is threaded through
`tts_config()["speed"]` and is part of the line cache key, so a speed change re-renders everything.
Note it is a global setting, not per-line.

**Hesitation renders well.** Measured: `"Um, well, I mean... it's, er, roughly forty pounds, I
think."` synthesized cleanly and ASR recovered it near-verbatim. Fillers (`um`, `er`, `well`,
`I mean`, `you know`, `sort of`) are safe and should be used liberally — they are most of what
makes a script sound spoken rather than read.

**Self-correction — our most important device — renders well too.** Measured internal gaps:

| Line | Duration | Internal gaps |
|---|---|---|
| `It's on the fourth floor. Sorry, no, the fifth.` | 3.09 s | 282 ms |
| `Tuesdays, mostly. Well, Tuesdays and Thursdays now.` | 3.80 s | 344 ms, 86 ms |
| `We open at nine. Um, actually, from September it's eight thirty.` | 4.50 s | 405 ms, 123 ms |

The `um, actually` construction produced the largest and most natural break. **Author corrections
as `<wrong>. <filler> <correction-marker> <right>`** — full stop, then `um, actually` / `sorry, no`
/ `hang on` / `well, I say X` — and you get both the prosodic reset and a usable gap for free.

### 8.5 Abbreviations — expand everything

**Measured:** `Dr. Patel from St. Mary's Rd.` → `dˈɒktə. pɐtˈɛl fɹɒm sənt. mˈeəɹiz ˌɑːdˈiː.`
`Dr.` → "doctor" ✅. `St.` → "**saint**" (wrong for a street) ❌. `Rd.` → `ˌɑːdˈiː` = spelled
"**R D**" ❌ — ASR confirmed, heard as "R.D."

> ### 🔴 AUTHORING RULE 4 — never abbreviate in `text`
> Write `Doctor`, `Saint`, `Street`, `Road`, `Avenue`, `Mount`, `approximately`, `for example`.
> Acronyms meant to be spelled are fine and correct — `RSPB` → `ˌɑːɹˌɛspˌiːbˈiː`, `NHS` →
> `ˌɛnˌeɪtʃˈɛs` — but any acronym ending in `A` needs the dotted form (`Y. M. C. A.`).

### 8.6 ❌ Overlapping speech is impossible

`Kokoro.create(text, voice, speed, lang, is_phonemes, trim)` takes exactly **one** voice and
returns one buffer. `stitch.stitch()` concatenates strictly sequentially with a monotonic cursor;
there is no mixing path anywhere in either module. The available methods are `create`,
`create_stream`, `get_voice_style`, `from_session` — none blends speakers.

**Say so plainly: we cannot render interruption, overlap, or back-channelling over another
speaker.** This is a genuine fidelity loss versus the real exam, where speakers talk over each
other in Part 3 in particular.

**Workarounds that do preserve the *listening skill* being tested:**

- **Latched turns.** Set `pause_after_ms: 0` and begin the next speaker mid-clause:
  `A: "...so I thought we could start with the questionnaire—"` / `B: "—the questionnaire, yes,
  but only if we've got the ethics clearance."` With a 0 ms authored pause the residual is only the
  voice's trailing silence (97–188 ms for most; see §7.4 and avoid `bm_fable` here). It reads and
  hears as an interruption.
- **Reported overlap.** `"Sorry, you go — no, after you."` A one-line collision without needing
  two simultaneous streams.
- **Recovery after interruption** — the actual skill — is fully testable: speaker A is cut off,
  and the answer comes when A resumes the point 20 seconds later.

Do **not** try to fake overlap by mixing rendered WAVs in content tooling; it defeats the line
cache, breaks `timing.json` monotonicity, and the transcript panel assumes non-overlapping windows.

### 8.7 🔴 The accent bug: British voices are being given American phonemes

`tts_render.py:295` hardcodes `lang="en-us"` on **every** synthesis call, including all eight
British voices.

**Measured**, `bf_emma`, `"I'd rather not, actually. The car park's past the tower, after the last
bath house."`:

| lang | Phonemes |
|---|---|
| `en-us` *(shipped)* | `aɪd ɹˈæðɚ nˈɑːt, ˈæktʃuːəli. ðə kˈɑːɹ pˈɑːɹks pˈæst ðə tˈaʊɚ, ˈæftɚ ðə lˈæst bˈæθ hˈaʊs.` |
| `en-gb` *(correct)* | `aɪd ɹˈɑːðə nˈɒt, ˈaktʃuːəli. ðə kˈɑː pˈɑːks pˈast ðə tˈaʊə, ˈaftə ðə lˈast bˈaθ hˈaʊs.` |

Every diagnostic British feature is wrong: the TRAP–BATH split (`ˈæftɚ`/`bˈæθ` vs `ˈaftə`/`bˈaθ`),
non-rhoticity (`kˈɑːɹ pˈɑːɹks` vs `kˈɑː pˈɑːks`), the LOT vowel (`nˈɑːt` vs `nˈɒt`), and
final-schwa (`tˈaʊɚ` vs `tˈaʊə`).

**And it corrupts spelled letters, which is our Part 1 staple:**

| | en-us | en-gb |
|---|---|---|
| `Z.` | `zˈiː` — "**zee**" | `zˈɛd` — "zed" ✅ |
| `R.` | `ˈɑːɹ` (rhotic) | `ˈɑː` ✅ |

A British enrolments officer currently spells a surname saying "zee". That is an error a real
candidate would notice immediately.

The voices' *timbre* is still British — the acoustic model is doing its job — so the result is a
hybrid accent that does not exist anywhere: British voice quality over American vowels. This
directly undermines the accent-drill feature, which is the module's main differentiator, because
`uk` and `us` renders currently differ in voice but not fully in phonology.

**Fix (code, one line, `_synthesize_kokoro`):** derive lang from the voice prefix —
`"en-gb" if voice.startswith(("bf_","bm_")) else "en-us"`. **This changes no `audio_hash`**
(the hash covers voice, text and pause, not lang), so cached renders must be invalidated
explicitly — bump `schema_version` on affected scripts or clear `media/tts-lines/`. Flag this
in the verify agent's handover; it is not in my owned path.

### 8.8 The escape hatch: `is_phonemes=True`

**Measured and working.** `Kokoro.create()` accepts `is_phonemes=True` and will synthesize a raw
IPA string directly. Verified: `create("ðɪ ɐdɹˈɛs ɪz fˈanʃɔː ɹˈəʊd.", voice="bf_emma",
lang="en-gb", is_phonemes=True)` produced 1.83 s of correct audio.

This is our fix for any proper noun Kokoro mangles. Spot checks show it is mostly good already —
`Leicester` → `lˈɛstə` ✅, `Slough` → `slˈaʊ` ✅, `Cholmondeley` → `kˈəʊməndˌɛli` (wrong; should be
"Chumley"), `Featherstonehaugh` → `fˈɛðəstˌəʊnhɔː` (wrong; "Fanshaw"), `Magdalen` → `mˈaɡdeɪlən`
(wrong; "Maudlin").

**Authoring guidance:** prefer inventing names Kokoro handles. If a script genuinely needs a
counter-intuitive name — and a name whose spelling is not predictable from its sound is a
*legitimate and excellent* Part 1 item — the pipeline can support it, but only after the code
change in §C-6. Until then, **check any invented surname through the phonemizer before shipping**,
and pick a different name if it fails.

### 8.9 What a candidate will notice as synthetic

The research is consistent: TTS and human voices are rated similarly for *comprehensibility* but
unfavourably for *naturalness*; comprehending synthetic speech is more effortful (longer response
times, somewhat lower comprehension) though the penalty **shrinks with exposure**; and learners
perform comparably on comprehension, dictation and aural identification tasks with either
([Synthetic voices in the foreign language context, LLT 2020](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/bitstreams/54f66cfe-2240-41a9-8c61-54a2396388fa/download);
[Using TTS voices to develop listening materials](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229725408_Using_TTS_voices_to_develop_audio_materials_for_listening_comprehension_A_digital_approach)).

Ranked by how much each part suffers, worst first:

1. **Part 3 (2–4 speakers, academic discussion).** Worst hit. Real Part 3 audio is full of
   overlap, latching, and reactive tone — exactly what we cannot render (§8.6). Our version will
   sound like an audiobook of a play. Mitigate with latched turns, short turns (5–15 words), and
   heavy back-channelling on its own line (`"Mm."`, `"Right."`, `"Yeah, exactly."`).
2. **Part 1 (transactional dialogue).** Suffers on turn-taking rhythm — the fixed inter-turn gap
   is the giveaway. Mitigate by *varying* `pause_after_ms` (150 for a quick agreement, 400 before a
   considered answer) instead of the uniform 250–350 currently shipped.
3. **Part 2 (monologue, e.g. a tour or a radio announcement).** Renders well. A single voice
   delivering prepared information is close to what Kokoro is good at, and is genuinely close to
   the real thing.
4. **Part 4 (academic lecture).** Renders best. A lecture *is* a prepared monologue; the register
   matches. Long lines are safe here (§7.5).

Two things a careful candidate will still notice: emotional flatness (no amusement, no irritation —
so never write an item that hinges on tone of voice), and unvarying tempo within a turn.

**Set the expectation in the UI.** One honest line — "voices are synthesized; use the accent drill
and the real-exam recording notes to bridge the gap" — costs nothing and buys trust. It also
converts a limitation into the rationale for extensive listening (§1).

## 9. Reproducing the measurements

```python
from kokoro_onnx import Kokoro
from kokoro_onnx.tokenizer import Tokenizer
import os
M = os.path.expanduser("~/Library/Application Support/BandReady/models/kokoro/kokoro-v1.0.onnx")
V = os.path.expanduser("~/Library/Application Support/BandReady/models/kokoro/voices-v1.0.bin")
k, t = Kokoro(M, V), Tokenizer()

sorted(k.get_voices())                          # §7.2 inventory
t.phonemize("That's B. R. A. D.", lang="en-gb") # §8.1 fastest possible check
samples, rate = k.create(text, voice="bf_emma", speed=1.0, lang="en-gb")
```

Run from `sidecar/` with `uv run python …`. Loading the model takes ~0.3 s, so a lint pass over a
whole pack is cheap. `Tokenizer.phonemize()` alone — no synthesis — is enough to catch every
§8.1–§8.5 defect, which makes an automated authoring gate genuinely practical (§C-1).

| Rule | Regex | Hits in shipped content |
|---|---|---|
| hyphen-spelled name | `\b(?:[A-Za-z]-){2,}[A-Za-z]\b` | **2 — both genuine defects** (`O-K-A-F-O-R`, `B-E-L-L-F-I-E-L-D`) |
| 4-digit non-year | `\b\d{4,}\b` minus `\b20\d\d\b` in a date context | 0 (the two hits, `2019`/`2016`, are correct 21st-century years) |
| 20th-century year | `\b1[89]\d\d\b` | 0 |
| true decimal / price | `\d+\.\d+` **not** matching `\b\d{1,2}\.[0-5]\d\b` (clock time) | 0 (the two `6.45` hits are times and render correctly) |
| currency symbol | `[£$€]\d` | 0 |
| abbreviation | `\b(?:Dr\|St\|Rd\|Ave\|Mt\|Prof)\.` | 0 |
| bare dash | `\s-\s` | 0 |

Note the two exemptions matter: naive `\b\d{4,}\b` and naive `\d+\.\d+` both false-positive on
correct content, so ship the carve-outs or authors will learn to ignore the linter. This is pure
regex, runs inside the existing validator, and costs nothing.
**Do this before authoring 35 more scripts.**

*Impact: high. Cost: one line + cache invalidation.*
§8.7. Without it, the accent drill — our headline listening feature — is only half real, and
British speakers say "zee".

**C-3. Fix the `au` cast collision.**
*Impact: medium. Cost: trivial.*
§7.3. `au/male_2` `bm_fable`(124) → `bm_lewis`(98); `au/narrator` `bm_george` → `bm_daniel` so the

### Tier 1 — highest learner impact per unit of build

**C-4. Prediction gate in the preview gap.**
*Impact: very high. Cost: low.*
Evidence: §4.4, Vandergrift's cycle, with the largest gains among weaker listeners — our core user.
Content: one field per question, `predict: {expect: "plural_noun" | "number" | "name" | "time" |
"adjective" | "place", why: "…"}`. On screen: during the existing 30 s preview, each visible
question shows a five-chip row. Learner taps one. On submit, the review shows
**"You predicted a number. It was a plural noun — that's why 'twenty' looked plausible."**
This turns dead air into the module's signature interaction, and it is the cheapest thing here
that no competitor does well.

**C-5. Cause-first review with per-question replay.**
*Impact: very high. Cost: medium.*
Evidence: §4.1 (Field's diagnostic stance), §4.2 (Holzknecht: +1.03/10 and lower anxiety from a
second play), §2.1 (feedback is the active ingredient).
On screen, for each wrong answer, in this order:
1. **"What happened?"** — the four buttons from §4.1. Nothing else is visible.
2. **Replay just the answer window** — from `timing.json`, `start_ms` of the answer line minus
   3000 ms to `end_ms` plus 1500 ms. Transcript **blurred**.
3. **Reveal** — transcript unblurs, the answer span highlighted, and the *distractor* span
   highlighted in a second colour with the signpost word underlined.
4. **The teaching note**, selected by the cause the learner chose in step 1.

The seek data already exists: `GET /api/v1/media/listening/{hash}.timing.json` gives sample-accurate
`start_ms`/`end_ms` per line, and the docstring guarantees it cannot drift. This is the single
highest-value use of an asset we already ship and currently under-use.

**C-6. The `say_as` field — separate what is spoken from what is written.**
*Impact: high. Cost: low-medium.*
The root cause of §8.1 and §8.2 is that one string serves two purposes. Add an optional per-line
`say_as` (and optional `phonemes`):
```json
{"speaker": "s2", "text": "Of course. O-K-A-F-O-R. Okafor.",
 "say_as": "Of course. O. K. A. F. O. R. Okafor.", "pause_after_ms": 600}
```
`tts_render` synthesizes `say_as or text`; the transcript panel displays `text`. Authors then write
naturally and the render stays correct. **Note this must be added to `script_audio_hash`'s payload**
(`tts_render.py:143`) or edits to `say_as` will not invalidate the cache. `phonemes` unlocks §8.8
and makes counter-intuitive surnames — a first-class Part 1 item — safe to author.

**C-7. Trim to a fixed silence floor in `stitch`.**
*Impact: medium-high (fixes every script at once). Cost: low.*
§7.4: trailing silence varies 97–538 ms by voice, so authored pauses are unpredictable. Trim each
line's leading and trailing silence to a fixed 40 ms in `stitch.stitch()` before concatenating, so
`pause_after_ms` means what it says. Then authors can write real turn-taking rhythm (§8.9 item 2)
and `pause_after_ms: 0` becomes a genuine latched interruption (§8.6). One numpy threshold scan per
line; timing offsets stay sample-accurate because they are computed after trimming.

### Tier 2 — the drills

**C-8. Dictation drill.**
*Impact: very high. Cost: medium.*
Evidence: §2 — a large effect from a cheap exercise, and construct-valid.
20–40 s clip, unlimited replay, free-text box. Grade with the **four buckets** of §2.1, extending
`scoring/answers.py` (`near_miss` already gives the Levenshtein primitive — do not duplicate it).
On screen: a word-level diff, colour-coded by bucket, with a one-line diagnosis per bucket and a
"you heard 34 of 41 words; 5 of the 7 you missed were function words" headline. That headline is
the insight most learners have never had. Source clips from parts already in the pack — zero new
content cost, and it makes each authored script earn its keep several times over.

**C-9. Distractor spotlight in the transcript.**
*Impact: high. Cost: low (content, not code).*
Evidence: §3 of the theses; self-correction is the defining listening trap and coaching sources
converge on it.
Per question, author `trap: {kind: "self_correction" | "similar_option" | "paraphrase" |
"irrelevant_number" | "negation", decoy_line: 14, decoy_text: "the fourth floor",
signpost: "actually", note: "…"}`. On screen, the review transcript shows the decoy struck through
in amber, the signpost word boxed, and the real answer in green — so the learner *sees* the shape
of the trap rather than reading a description of it. Then aggregate: **"Self-correction has cost
you 6 marks across 3 tests"** on the progress screen. That sentence changes behaviour.

**C-10. Connected-speech / weak-form drill.**
*Impact: high. Cost: medium.*
Evidence: §3 — this is where known words are lost, and learners are unaware it happens.
Chunk-level gap-fill on function words, plus count-the-words, plus reuse of the existing
`pron_pairs.jsonl` for discrimination. Clips come from existing scripts via `timing.json`, so the
content cost is authoring a `chunks: [{line: 7, start_word: 3, end_word: 11, blanks: [4,6]}]` list —
not new audio.

**C-11. Number and letter dictation drill.**
*Impact: high. Cost: low.*
Phone numbers, postcodes, prices, dates, `-teen`/`-ty`, spelled names. Purely generative content
(no scripts needed — synthesize on demand), scored exactly, and it targets the mechanically
commonest lost marks. Must follow §8.1 and §8.2 rules to be correct at all, which is another reason
C-1 comes first. High value per line of code.

### Tier 3 — the long game

**C-12. Signpost tracker / position drill.**
*Impact: medium-high. Cost: medium.*
§5. Play a part with questions hidden; learner taps on each topic shift; scored against authored
`signposts: [{line: 22, marker: "Right, moving on to", ms: 184000}]`. Trains position-keeping, which
is the real recovery mechanism, and the content field is trivial to author alongside the script.

**C-13. Recovery / cascade drill.**
*Impact: medium-high. Cost: low (mostly a scoring view).*
§5. Score the *two questions after* a miss and surface the cascade explicitly. Almost free: it is an
analysis over attempt data we already store, plus one debrief card.

**C-14. Accent ladder and speed ladder.**
*Impact: medium. Cost: low — the mechanism exists.*
§6. `render_script(accent_set=...)` already forces the whole cast onto another accent set and
produces a separate `audio_hash`, so the same script in three accents is one API call each. Ladder
speed 0.9 → 1.0 → 1.1 via the `speed` config (note: global, and it re-renders everything, so cache
warming matters). Blocked on **C-2** for the accent half to be worth anything.

**C-15. Shadowing step.**
*Impact: medium. Cost: medium — but the parts exist.*
§4.3, d≈0.89 on pronunciation. Mic + faster-whisper are already in the app for speaking; score the
learner's ASR output against the known transcript with the same alignment code as C-8. Reuse, not
new infrastructure.

**C-16. Extensive-listening prescription and log.**
*Impact: medium (real but diffuse). Cost: low.*
§1. We host nothing. The app prescribes minutes, offers a genre list, and requires three reflection
prompts per entry — because unstructured exposure is the version that does not work. Also the
honest answer to §8.9: our synthetic audio is the intensive half; real human audio has to come
from outside, and we should say so and schedule it rather than pretend.

**C-17. Transcript-with-blanks as the default transcript view.**
*Impact: medium. Cost: low.*
§4.3. Prevents the transcript panel from silently becoming a reading exercise, which is the main
way a transcript feature fails. Reuses C-10's blanking logic.

## 10. Direct instructions for the authoring agents

1. **Spell with dots**: `O. K. A. F. O. R.` Never hyphens, never bare spaced capitals. (§8.1)
2. **Numbers as words** for phone/reference numbers, decimals ("point"), and money
   ("forty pounds fifty"). Digits are fine for rooms, flats, postcodes, dates, ordinals. (§8.2)
3. **No abbreviations** in `text` — write `Doctor`, `Street`, `Road`, `Saint`. (§8.5)
4. **Never use ` - `** for a pause or an interruption; it vanishes from the audio. Use a comma, an
   ellipsis, or split the line. (§8.3)
5. **All pauses come from `pause_after_ms`.** Vary it: 150 for a quick agreement, 250 for a normal
   turn, 400–600 before a considered answer or after a spelled name, 30000 for a question preview.
   Remember 100–540 ms of the voice's own trailing silence is added on top. (§7.4, §8.3)
6. **Self-corrections as `<wrong>. <filler> <marker> <right>`** — `"It's on the fourth floor. Um,
   actually, no — the fifth."` Renders with a natural 300–400 ms break for free. (§8.4)
7. **Fillers liberally**: `um`, `er`, `well`, `I mean`, `sort of`, `you know`. They render
   correctly and they are most of what makes a script sound spoken. (§8.4)
8. **Check speaker F0 separation ≥40 Hz** before finalising a same-sex dialogue. Use the §7.3
   table. Never pair `bf_emma`+`bf_lily`, `af_bella`+`af_sarah`, or `bm_daniel`+`bm_fable`. (§7.3)
9. **Avoid `bm_fable` in dialogue** — ~540 ms of trailing silence per turn. Monologue only. (§7.4)
10. **Lines under ~350 characters**, for transcript granularity, not because Kokoro fails. (§7.5)
11. **No item may depend on tone of voice, overlap, or emotional colour.** We cannot render them.
    (§8.6, §8.9)
12. **Run the phonemizer over every proper noun and every number line before shipping.** One
    `Tokenizer.phonemize()` call, no synthesis needed, catches essentially every defect above. (§9)
