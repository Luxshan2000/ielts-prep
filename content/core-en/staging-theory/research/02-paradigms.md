the practice module's "walking route"). Compiled 2026-07-31. Consumers: the theory design agent
(chapter schema + ordering), the chapter authors, and the verify agent.

## 0. How to read this file

### 0.1 The two model verbs — used everywhere, never varied

Every paradigm in §§2–5 is built from the **same two verbs**, so that a learner can lay any two
tables side by side and see that only one thing changed.

| Role | Verb | Base | Past simple | Past participle | Why this verb |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The regular verb** | **paint** | paint | painted | painted | Regular in every form; transitive, so it also drives the whole passive chapter; natural in the continuous; no awkward spelling. |
| **The irregular verb** | **write** | write | wrote | written | Three distinct forms, so it exposes the base/past/participle distinction that regular verbs hide; transitive; natural in the continuous. |

Standing objects, so the sentences stay comparable and stay in the house world:
*paint the bridge* (the Verdon river bridge) and *write the report* (the Norland survey report).

**Why one regular and one irregular is not optional.** For *paint*, past simple and past participle
are the identical string `painted`. A learner who only ever sees regular verbs cannot tell that
`I have painted` and `I painted` use two *different* grammatical forms that happen to be spelled the
same, and will later produce `*I have wrote`. Showing `wrote` / `written` in the same cell position
makes the distinction visible. **Every tense table below therefore has a regular row and an irregular
row.** [H]

| `V-s` | the **third-person singular present** form: *paints*, *writes* |
| `V-ed` | the **past simple** form: *painted*, *wrote* |
| `V-en` | the **past participle**: *painted*, *written*. (Named `-en` by convention because irregular participles often end that way. It is *not* a spelling instruction.) |
| `V-ing` | the **-ing form**: *painting*, *writing* |
| `Ø` | nothing goes here — the slot is deliberately empty |
| **[H]** | High confidence: a plain fact about English that any reference grammar states |
| **[M]** | Medium: the field agrees but the boundary is fuzzy, or usage is shifting |
| **[JUDGEMENT]** | A pedagogical decision I am making for this product, not a fact |
| **[BrE/AmE]** | **British and American usage differ here.** Every one of these must survive into the learner-facing chapter with both variants shown. |
| **[SPOKEN]** | Common and correct in speech, but **wrong or too informal in IELTS Writing**. Must be flagged in the chapter, not silently taught. |
| **[FORMAL]** | Correct but noticeably formal; safe in Writing, can sound stiff in Speaking. |

### 0.3 Terminology that must be defined the first time it appears

The learner starting this reference has **zero grammar knowledge**. These are the terms §§1–16 cannot
avoid. The chapter author must define each one in plain words at first use, and the definition below
is the approved wording — do not invent a competing one, because a reference book that defines
"auxiliary" two different ways in two chapters has failed.

| Term | Plain-words definition to use |
|---|---|
| **verb** | The word that says what happens or what is: *paint*, *write*, *be*, *seem*. |
| **subject** | The person or thing the sentence is about — the one doing the verb. In *The council painted the bridge*, the subject is *the council*. |
| **object** | The person or thing the verb is done to. In *The council painted the bridge*, the object is *the bridge*. |
| **transitive verb** | A verb that can take an object: you *paint something*. |
| **intransitive verb** | A verb that cannot take an object: you cannot *\*arrive something*. |
| **auxiliary (helping verb)** | A verb put in front of the main verb to build a form. English has three: **be**, **have**, **do**. They carry the tense so the main verb does not have to. |
| **modal** | A special auxiliary that adds an attitude — how sure, how necessary, how allowed: *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must*. |
| **operator** | The first auxiliary or modal in a sentence. It is the word that moves to make a question and the word that `not` attaches to. If a sentence has no operator, English *supplies* one (`do`). This is the single most useful word in the whole reference — §8 is built on it. |
| **infinitive** | The base form of the verb. **To-infinitive** = *to paint*. **Bare infinitive** = *paint*, with no *to*. Which one a word takes is not guessable; it has to be listed (§6, §12). |
| **participle** | A verb form used with an auxiliary or as a describing word. **Past participle** = *painted*, *written*. **Present participle** = the *-ing* form. |
| **gerund** | The *-ing* form used as a noun: *Painting is expensive.* |
| **person** | Who the subject is: **1st** = *I / we*, **2nd** = *you*, **3rd** = *he / she / it / they* and any noun. |
| **number** | Singular (one) or plural (more than one). |
| **tense** | The verb form that locates something in time and says how the speaker is presenting it. |
| **aspect** | The part of the verb form that says *how* the speaker views the event — as finished and connected back (**perfect**), or as in progress (**continuous**). |
| **voice** | Active (*the council painted the bridge*) or passive (*the bridge was painted*). |
| **clause** | A group of words with its own subject and verb. |
| **polarity** | Whether a sentence is positive or negative. Used constantly in §8's tag rules. |
| **inversion** | Putting the operator in front of the subject: *she is* → *is she*. |
| **backshift** | Moving a verb one step further into the past when you report what somebody said (§9). |
| **contraction** | A shortened, run-together form: *I'm*, *don't*, *she'll*. |

### 0.4 Three rules that govern every table in this file

1. **Only the operator carries tense and person.** Once `do`, `have`, `be`, `will` or a modal is
   present, the main verb stops changing. `He does not paint` — not `*He does not paints`. `She did
   not write` — not `*She did not wrote`. This single rule removes about a third of the errors a
   beginner makes, and it should be stated in the chapter *before* the first table, not after. [H]
2. **English marks person only in one place: third person singular, present.** Every other cell of
   every present-tense table is the bare form. The only verb with more distinctions is `be`. [H]
3. **Contractions are speech, and the tables show them as a separate column, never as the headline
   form.** IELTS Writing is academic register and takes the full forms; Speaking takes the
   contracted ones and sounds stilted without them. Every table below therefore has a **Contracted /
   spoken** column that is explicitly labelled. [SPOKEN] [JUDGEMENT]

## 1. What a verb form is made of — the parts every later table draws on

### 1.1 The five forms of an English verb

Every English verb except `be` has exactly **five** forms. That is the whole inventory. Nothing in
§§2–5 uses anything else. [H]

| # | Form name | Regular: *paint* | Irregular: *write* | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Base form** (infinitive) | paint | write | After *to*; after all modals; after *do/does/did*; imperatives; present tense except 3rd sing. |
| 2 | **-s form** (3rd person singular present) | paints | writes | Present simple, only with *he / she / it* or a singular noun |
| 3 | **Past simple** | painted | wrote | Past simple tense only. Never after an auxiliary. |
| 4 | **Past participle** | painted | written | After *have* (perfect tenses); after *be* (passive); as an adjective |
| 5 | **-ing form** | painting | writing | After *be* (continuous tenses); as a gerund; in participle clauses |

**The trap regular verbs hide.** For *paint*, forms 3 and 4 are the same string. For *write* they are
not. Learners who never see the second column produce `*I have wrote the report` and `*The bridge was
wrote about`. Show both columns every time. [H]

### 1.2 `be` — the one verb with eight forms

`be` is the most irregular verb in English and also the most frequent, so it gets its own table
early. Everything in the continuous tenses and the entire passive is built on it. [H]

| Function | Form |
|---|---|
| Base form | be |
| Present, *I* | am |
| Present, *he / she / it* + singular noun | is |
| Present, *you / we / they* + plural noun | are |
| Past, *I / he / she / it* + singular noun | was |
| Past, *you / we / they* + plural noun | were |
| Past participle | been |
| -ing form | being |

**Full present and past of `be`, with negatives, questions and short answers:**

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted) | Question | Short answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I am | I'm | I am not | I'm not | Am I? | Yes, I am. / No, I'm not. |
| you (sing.) | you are | you're | you are not | you aren't / you're not | Are you? | Yes, you are. / No, you aren't. |
| he / she / it | he is | he's | he is not | he isn't / he's not | Is he? | Yes, he is. / No, he isn't. |
| we | we are | we're | we are not | we aren't / we're not | Are we? | Yes, we are. / No, we aren't. |
| you (pl.) | you are | you're | you are not | you aren't / you're not | Are you? | Yes, you are. / No, you aren't. |
| they | they are | they're | they are not | they aren't / they're not | Are they? | Yes, they are. / No, they aren't. |
| I (past) | I was | — | I was not | I wasn't | Was I? | Yes, I was. / No, I wasn't. |
| you (past) | you were | — | you were not | you weren't | Were you? | Yes, you were. / No, you weren't. |
| he / she / it (past) | he was | — | he was not | he wasn't | Was he? | Yes, he was. / No, he wasn't. |
| we / you / they (past) | they were | — | they were not | they weren't | Were they? | Yes, they were. / No, they weren't. |

**Four notes the chapter must carry:**

- **There is no contraction of `am not`.** `I'm not` contracts the *am*, not the *not*. `*amn't` is
  not standard English (it exists in Scottish and Irish English, and the chapter may say so, but it
  must not be offered as a form to produce). [H]
- **Two negative contractions, one meaning.** `he isn't` and `he's not` are both correct and mean the
  same thing. `he's not` slightly emphasises the *not*. Same for `you aren't` / `you're not`. [H]
- **Past `be` has no positive contraction.** `*I's`, `*we're` for *we were* do not exist. Only
  `wasn't` and `weren't`. [H]
- **Never contract a positive short answer.** `Yes, I am.` — `*Yes, I'm.` is wrong. The rule is
  general: a contraction cannot end a clause. Same for `*Yes, I've`, `*Yes, he's`, `*Yes, they're`.
  Negative short answers *do* contract: `No, I'm not.` `No, he isn't.` This is one of the highest-value
  half-lines in the whole reference and beginners are almost never told it. [H]

### 1.3 `have` and `do` — auxiliary and main verb are different jobs

Both `have` and `do` lead double lives: they are auxiliaries (grammar words that build a form) and
they are ordinary main verbs (with their own meaning). The chapter must separate the two or the
learner will produce `*Have you a car?` and `*I haven't done my homework yesterday`.

| Verb | Forms | As **auxiliary** | As **main verb** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **have** | have / has / had / had / having | Builds the perfect: *have painted*, *has written*, *had been writing* | Means *own, possess, eat, experience*: *We have a survey team.* |
| **do** | do / does / did / done / doing | Builds questions and negatives when there is no other operator: *Do you paint?*, *She didn't write.* Also emphatic: *I **do** agree.* | Means *perform, carry out*: *She did the calculations.* |

**The critical consequence:** when `have` or `do` is a *main* verb, it needs `do`-support like any
other verb.

| | Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| main-verb *have* | Do you have a permit? | `*Have you a permit?` [FORMAL/dated in BrE; not for learners] |
| main-verb *have*, negative | She doesn't have a permit. | `*She hasn't a permit.` [dated BrE] |
| main-verb *do* | Did they do the survey? | `*Did they the survey?` |
| main-verb *do*, negative | They didn't do the survey. | `*They didn't the survey.` |

**[BrE/AmE]** For possession, British English also has **`have got`**: *I've got a permit.* / *Have you
got a permit?* / *I haven't got a permit.* American English uses it too but leans harder on
*do you have*. `have got` is informal-to-neutral; it is fine in Speaking and **should be avoided in
IELTS Writing**, where `have` is the register-appropriate choice. [SPOKEN] Note also that `have got`
has no past: the past of *I've got a permit* is *I had a permit*, never `*I had got a permit`
(that means something else). [H]

### 1.4 The whole contraction inventory, in one place

Contractions are the single biggest difference between how the tables look and how English sounds.
Here is every one a learner needs, with what it is short for. The ambiguous ones — `'s` and `'d` —
are the ones worth teaching properly, because they each stand for two different words and the learner
resolves them from what comes next.

| Contraction | Stands for | How to tell which | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `'m` | am | only ever *am* | I'm painting |
| `'re` | are | only ever *are* | they're painting |
| `'s` | **is** | followed by `-ing` or an adjective/noun | she's painting; she's ready |
| `'s` | **has** | followed by a **past participle** | she's painted it; she's written it |
| `'ve` | have | only ever *have* | we've painted |
| `'d` | **had** | followed by a **past participle** | we'd painted |
| `'d` | **would** | followed by a **bare infinitive** | we'd paint |
| `'ll` | will | only ever *will* | we'll paint |
| `n't` | not | attaches to the operator | doesn't, hasn't, wouldn't |

**Negative contractions, including the irregular ones:**

| Full | Contracted | Note |
|---|---|---|
| is not | isn't | also *'s not* |
| are not | aren't | also *'re not* |
| am not | — | no *n't* form; use *I'm not*; question form *aren't I?* (§8.5) |
| was not | wasn't | |
| were not | weren't | |
| do not | don't | |
| does not | doesn't | spelled with *oe*, pronounced /dʌz(ə)nt/ |
| did not | didn't | |
| have not | haven't | also *'ve not*, more BrE than AmE [BrE/AmE] |
| has not | hasn't | also *'s not* |
| had not | hadn't | also *'d not* |
| will not | **won't** | irregular spelling — not `*willn't` |
| would not | wouldn't | |
| shall not | **shan't** | [BrE], now dated; rare in AmE |
| should not | shouldn't | |
| can not | **can't** | written as one word `cannot` when full; `can not` as two words only when *not* belongs to what follows |
| could not | couldn't | |
| may not | — | no standard contraction; `*mayn't` is obsolete |
| might not | mightn't | [BrE], rare |
| must not | mustn't | the first *t* is silent |
| need not | needn't | modal *need* only (§5.5) |
| dare not | daren't | modal *dare* only (§5.5) |
| ought not to | oughtn't to | rare |
| used to not | didn't use to | see §5.5 for the spelling question |

**Reduced forms that are speech only and must never be written**, in any BandReady content, in any
IELTS answer, or in any quotation that is not explicitly marked as speech: `gonna` (going to),
`wanna` (want to), `gotta` (got to), `gimme`, `lemme`, `dunno`, `'cos`/`cause`, `shoulda`/`coulda`/
`woulda`, `ain't`. [SPOKEN]

**One error that comes directly from a contraction.** `would've` sounds identical to `would of`, so
learners write `*would of painted`, `*should of written`, `*could of gone`. There is no verb `of`.
The word is always **have**. Flag it in the modal chapter (§5.3) as well as here. [H]

## 2. The twelve tenses — complete paradigms

### 2.0 How the twelve are built

Two switches and three times, as `01-syllabus.md` §3 sets out. The chapter should show this
*before* the tables, so that twelve tables read as twelve consequences rather than twelve facts to
memorise.

| Switch | Off | On | What "on" adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect?** | nothing | `have` + past participle | Connects back to an earlier point |
| **Continuous?** | nothing | `be` + -ing form | Views it as in progress, not as a whole |

| | **Simple** | **Continuous** | **Perfect** | **Perfect continuous** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | paints / writes | is painting / is writing | has painted / has written | has been painting / has been writing |
| **Past** | painted / wrote | was painting / was writing | had painted / had written | had been painting / had been writing |
| **Future** | will paint / will write | will be painting / will be writing | will have painted / will have written | will have been painting / will have been writing |

**The stacking order never varies:** `modal` → `have` → `been` → `being` → `past participle`. Reading
`will have been being painted` left to right, that is: future, perfect, continuous, passive. Nothing
in English reorders those. [H]

### 2.1 Present simple

**Meaning in one line:** things that are always or generally true — habits, permanent situations,
facts, states. → TH-R3 for the full decision against the present continuous.

**Form:** base form; add **-s** for *he / she / it* and singular nouns (spelling in §14.1).

| Person | Positive (regular) | Positive (irregular) | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I paint | I write | I do not paint | I don't paint | Do I paint? |
| you | you paint | you write | you do not paint | you don't paint | Do you paint? |
| he / she / it | he **paints** | he **writes** | he **does** not paint | he **doesn't** paint | **Does** he paint? |
| we | we paint | we write | we do not paint | we don't paint | Do we paint? |
| you (pl.) | you paint | you write | you do not paint | you don't paint | Do you paint? |
| they | they paint | they write | they do not paint | they don't paint | Do they paint? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Do you paint? | Yes, I do. | No, I don't. |
| Does she write? | Yes, she does. | No, she doesn't. |
| Do they paint? | Yes, they do. | No, they don't. |

**Notes.**

- **The `-s` moves onto `do`.** In the negative and the question, `does` carries the third-person
  `-s` and the main verb goes back to the base form: `he doesn't paint`, not `*he doesn't paints`;
  `Does he write?`, not `*Does he writes?` This is rule 1 of §0.4 in action. [H]
- **There is no positive contraction** in the present simple. `*I'm paint` and `*he's paint` are
  not forms. Beginners produce them by analogy with the continuous. [H]
- **Emphatic `do`.** `I do agree`, `She does understand` — the auxiliary is added to a positive
  sentence for emphasis or contradiction. Correct in both speech and writing, though in writing it is
  a stylistic device rather than a default. [H]
- **Stative verbs** (*know, believe, own, contain, seem, prefer*) live almost entirely in the present
  simple, which is why the present simple is not just "the habit tense". → TH-R3.
- Example: *The Verdon depot paints the bridge railings every third summer.* / *The surveyor writes a
  short note after each visit.*

### 2.2 Present continuous (present progressive)

**Meaning in one line:** in progress now, or temporary, or a fixed arrangement in the future.

**Form:** `am / is / are` + **-ing** form (spelling in §14.3).

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted, two ways) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I am painting | I'm painting | I am not painting | I'm not painting *(only one)* | Am I painting? |
| you | you are painting | you're painting | you are not painting | you aren't / you're not painting | Are you painting? |
| he / she / it | he is writing | he's writing | he is not writing | he isn't / he's not writing | Is he writing? |
| we | we are painting | we're painting | we are not painting | we aren't / we're not painting | Are we painting? |
| you (pl.) | you are writing | you're writing | you are not writing | you aren't / you're not writing | Are you writing? |
| they | they are painting | they're painting | they are not painting | they aren't / they're not painting | Are they painting? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Are you painting? | Yes, I am. *(never `*Yes, I'm.`)* | No, I'm not. |
| Is she writing? | Yes, she is. | No, she isn't. / No, she's not. |
| Are they painting? | Yes, they are. | No, they aren't. / No, they're not. |

**Notes.**

- **Negative questions.** `Aren't you painting?` (contracted, normal) vs `Are you not painting?`
  (uncontracted, [FORMAL] and slightly indignant). For *I*, the negative question is **`Aren't I
  painting?`** — see §8.5. [H]
- **Stative verbs normally have no continuous:** `*I am knowing`, `*She is owning`, `*It is
  containing` are not English. Some verbs switch between a stative and a dynamic meaning and then the
  continuous is fine with a *different* sense: *I think the design works* (opinion, stative) vs *I'm
  thinking about the design* (mental activity, dynamic). → TH-R3. [H]
- Example: *They're painting the Verdon bridge this week.* / *She's writing the Norland report at the
  moment.*

### 2.3 Present perfect simple

**Meaning in one line:** a past event tied to now — experience, present result, or an unfinished
period. → TH-R3 for the present perfect vs past simple decision, which is the highest-traffic choice
in the whole reference.

**Form:** `have / has` + **past participle**.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I have painted | I've painted | I have not painted | I haven't painted / I've not painted | Have I painted? |
| you | you have written | you've written | you have not written | you haven't written / you've not written | Have you written? |
| he / she / it | he **has** painted | he's painted | he has not painted | he hasn't painted / he's not painted | **Has** he painted? |
| we | we have written | we've written | we have not written | we haven't written | Have we written? |
| you (pl.) | you have painted | you've painted | you have not painted | you haven't painted | Have you painted? |
| they | they have written | they've written | they have not written | they haven't written | Have they written? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Have you painted it? | Yes, I have. *(never `*Yes, I've.`)* | No, I haven't. |
| Has she written it? | Yes, she has. | No, she hasn't. |

**Notes.**

- **`he's painted` = `he has painted`.** The `'s` here is *has*, not *is*, and the past participle
  after it is the clue. Contrast `he's painting` (= is). Teach the two side by side. [H]
- **[BrE/AmE]** The `'ve not` / `'s not` / `'d not` negatives are more frequent in British English;
  American English strongly prefers `haven't` / `hasn't` / `hadn't`. Both are correct everywhere. [M]
- **Never with a finished time expression.** `*I have painted it last year` is wrong in both
  varieties; `last year` closes the time, so the past simple is required. This is a *usage* rule, not
  a form rule, and it belongs to TH-R3 — but the form chapter should carry the one-line warning
  because it is the error learners make immediately after learning the paradigm. [H]
- **[BrE/AmE] A real difference in the choice, not the form.** With *just*, *already* and *yet*,
  British English strongly prefers the present perfect (*I've just finished*, *Have you finished
  yet?*); American English commonly uses the past simple (*I just finished*, *Did you finish yet?*).
  Both are accepted in IELTS; the British pattern is the safer default for a learner because it is
  never wrong in either variety. [M] [JUDGEMENT]
- **Past participles are the whole difficulty.** Regular verbs hide it (`painted` twice); irregular
  verbs expose it (`wrote` vs `written`). §10 is the list. [H]

### 2.4 Present perfect continuous

**Meaning in one line:** how long something has been going on, up to now — often explaining a present
state.

**Form:** `have / has` + `been` + **-ing** form.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I have been painting | I've been painting | I have not been painting | I haven't been painting | Have I been painting? |
| you | you have been writing | you've been writing | you have not been writing | you haven't been writing | Have you been writing? |
| he / she / it | he **has** been painting | he's been painting | he has not been painting | he hasn't been painting | **Has** he been painting? |
| we | we have been writing | we've been writing | we have not been writing | we haven't been writing | Have we been writing? |
| they | they have been painting | they've been painting | they have not been painting | they haven't been painting | Have they been painting? |

**Short answers:** exactly the same as the present perfect simple — `Yes, I have.` / `No, I haven't.`
The short answer only ever repeats the **operator**, so the `been` and the `-ing` drop away. This is
worth saying out loud once, because it explains every short answer in the file. [H]

**Notes.**

- Stative verbs are again excluded: `*I have been knowing her for five years` → `I have known her for
  five years`. [H]
- `How long have you been writing it?` is the natural question shape; `How long` + present perfect
  continuous is a fixed partnership worth showing. [M]
- Example: *The contractors have been painting the bridge since April.* / *She's been writing the
  Ashfield chapter all morning.*

### 2.5 Past simple

**Meaning in one line:** a finished event or state in a finished time.

**Form:** past simple form of the verb (regular `-ed`, spelling in §14.2; irregular listed in §10).

| Person | Positive (regular) | Positive (irregular) | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I painted | I wrote | I did not paint | I didn't paint | Did I paint? |
| you | you painted | you wrote | you did not paint | you didn't paint | Did you paint? |
| he / she / it | he painted | he wrote | he did not paint | he didn't paint | Did he paint? |
| we | we painted | we wrote | we did not paint | we didn't paint | Did we paint? |
| you (pl.) | you painted | you wrote | you did not paint | you didn't paint | Did you paint? |
| they | they painted | they wrote | they did not paint | they didn't paint | Did they paint? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Did you paint it? | Yes, I did. | No, I didn't. |
| Did she write it? | Yes, she did. | No, she didn't. |

**Notes.**

- **The past simple does not change for person at all.** One form for all six persons, in every verb
  in English except `be` (§1.2). Learners coming from languages with six past endings find this hard
  to believe; say it explicitly. [H]
- **After `did`, the verb goes back to the base form.** `He didn't write` — never `*He didn't wrote`.
  `Did she paint?` — never `*Did she painted?` The past is already marked on `did`, and English
  marks it once. This is the single most common formation error in the past tense. [H]
- **There is no positive contraction** of the past simple. [H]
- **Emphatic `did`:** *The council **did** approve the funding* — used to contradict. [H]
- Example: *The depot painted the railings in 2019.* / *She wrote the Marlow chapter last winter.*

### 2.6 Past continuous (past progressive)

**Meaning in one line:** already in progress at a past moment — the background against which
something else happened.

**Form:** `was / were` + **-ing** form.

| Person | Positive | Negative (full) | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I **was** painting | I was not painting | I wasn't painting | Was I painting? |
| you | you **were** writing | you were not writing | you weren't writing | Were you writing? |
| he / she / it | he **was** painting | he was not painting | he wasn't painting | Was he painting? |
| we | we **were** writing | we were not writing | we weren't writing | Were we writing? |
| you (pl.) | you **were** painting | you were not painting | you weren't painting | Were you painting? |
| they | they **were** writing | they were not writing | they weren't writing | Were they writing? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Were you painting? | Yes, I was. | No, I wasn't. |
| Was she writing? | Yes, she was. | No, she wasn't. |

**Notes.**

- **No positive contraction** — `*I's painting`, `*they're painting` (for *were*) do not exist. The
  past of `be` simply has no contracted positive. [H]
- The `was` / `were` split is the same one as in §1.2: `was` with *I / he / she / it*, `were` with
  *you / we / they*. [H]
- Example: *They were painting the north span when the storm arrived.* / *She was writing the summary
  while the survey team packed up.*

### 2.7 Past perfect simple

**Meaning in one line:** earlier than the past moment you are already talking about ("past before
past").

**Form:** `had` + **past participle**. One form for every person.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I had painted | I'd painted | I had not painted | I hadn't painted / I'd not painted | Had I painted? |
| you | you had written | you'd written | you had not written | you hadn't written | Had you written? |
| he / she / it | he had painted | he'd painted | he had not painted | he hadn't painted | Had he painted? |
| we | we had written | we'd written | we had not written | we hadn't written | Had we written? |
| you (pl.) | you had painted | you'd painted | you had not painted | you hadn't painted | Had you painted? |
| they | they had written | they'd written | they had not written | they hadn't written | Had they written? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Had they painted it? | Yes, they had. | No, they hadn't. |

**Notes.**

- **`'d` is ambiguous and the resolution rule is mechanical.** `'d` + past participle = **had**
  (`they'd painted` = they had painted). `'d` + bare infinitive = **would** (`they'd paint` = they
  would paint). Show the pair in the same line; learners who have not been given this rule mis-hear
  the third conditional for years. [H]
- **The past perfect has no person changes at all** — `had` for all six. [H]
- Example: *By the time the inspector arrived, the crew had painted the whole span.* / *He had
  already written the recommendations before the data came back.*

### 2.8 Past perfect continuous

**Meaning in one line:** how long something had been going on up to a point in the past.

**Form:** `had` + `been` + **-ing** form. One form for every person.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I had been painting | I'd been painting | I hadn't been painting | Had I been painting? |
| he / she / it | he had been writing | he'd been writing | he hadn't been writing | Had he been writing? |
| they | they had been painting | they'd been painting | they hadn't been painting | Had they been painting? |

**Short answers:** `Yes, they had.` / `No, they hadn't.`

**Note.** This is a low-frequency form in learner writing and it is easy to overuse. `01-syllabus.md`
§3.1 marks it low-priority for production. In a *reference*, it must be present and complete — but
the chapter should say plainly that a learner who never produces it is not losing anything. [M]
[JUDGEMENT]

Example: *The team had been painting for six weeks when the funding was withdrawn.*

### 2.9 Future simple (`will`)

**Meaning in one line:** a prediction, or a decision made at the moment of speaking, or an offer,
promise or refusal. → TH-R3 for `will` vs `going to` vs the present continuous.

**Form:** `will` + **bare infinitive**. One form for every person.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Negative (contracted) | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I will paint | I'll paint | I will not paint | I won't paint | Will I paint? |
| you | you will write | you'll write | you will not write | you won't write | Will you write? |
| he / she / it | he will paint | he'll paint | he will not paint | he won't paint | Will he paint? |
| we | we will write | we'll write | we will not write | we won't write | Will we write? |
| you (pl.) | you will paint | you'll paint | you will not paint | you won't paint | Will you paint? |
| they | they will write | they'll write | they will not write | they won't write | Will they write? |

**Short answers**

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Will you paint it? | Yes, I will. | No, I won't. |
| Will she write it? | Yes, she will. | No, she won't. |

**Notes.**

- **`won't` is an irregular contraction** — there is no `*willn't`. Worth one line on its own. [H]
- **`'ll not`** (*I'll not go*) exists and is more British than American; `won't` is the everyday form
  in both. [BrE/AmE] [M]
- **`shall`.** The old prescriptive rule — `shall` for plain future with *I / we*, `will` elsewhere —
  is no longer how English is used. In current usage `will` covers all persons, and `shall` survives
  in three places: (a) **offers and suggestions in questions**, `Shall I open the window?`, `Shall we
  begin?`; (b) formal, legal and regulatory writing, where `shall` means *is required to*; (c) the
  contraction `shan't` [BrE, dated]. **[BrE/AmE]** `Shall I…?` is normal British English; American
  English usually says `Should I…?` or `Do you want me to…?` Both are understood everywhere. [M]
- **Never `will` in a time clause or an `if`-clause.** `When the survey finishes, we'll publish it` —
  not `*When the survey will finish`. The subordinate clause takes a present form even though it
  refers to the future. Applies after *when, before, after, until, as soon as, once, by the time, if,
  unless, provided*. This is a formation rule and it must sit in the paradigm chapter, not only in
  the conditionals chapter. [H]
- Example: *The contractor will paint the handrails next spring.* / *I'll write the covering note
  this evening.*

### 2.10 Future continuous

**Meaning in one line:** in progress at a moment in the future; also a neutral, pressure-free way of
asking about someone's arrangements.

**Form:** `will be` + **-ing** form. One form for every person.

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I will be painting | I'll be painting | I won't be painting | Will I be painting? |
| he / she / it | he will be writing | he'll be writing | he won't be writing | Will he be writing? |
| they | they will be painting | they'll be painting | they won't be painting | Will they be painting? |

**Short answers:** `Yes, I will.` / `No, I won't.`

**Note.** The "neutral enquiry" use is genuinely useful and rarely taught: *Will you be using the
meeting room this afternoon?* asks about arrangements without sounding like a request, whereas *Will
you use the meeting room?* sounds like one. [M]

## 3. The future is not one tense — the other future forms

English has no future tense in the way it has a past tense (there is no future *ending*). It has a
set of **forms used for future time**, and `will` is only one of them. A reference that shows only
`will` leaves the learner unable to read or write half of what is said about the future. [H]

| Form | Structure | What it is for | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `will` + bare infinitive | will + V | prediction; instant decision; offer; promise; refusal | The trust will fund the repairs. |
| `be going to` + bare infinitive | am/is/are going to + V | intention already formed; prediction from present evidence | They're going to repaint the whole span. |
| Present continuous | am/is/are + V-ing | a fixed arrangement, usually with another person and a time | We're meeting the contractor on Thursday. |
| Present simple | V / V-s | a timetable or schedule set by an institution | The last train leaves at 23:40. |
| `be to` + to-infinitive | am/is/are to + to-V | formal instruction or official arrangement [FORMAL] | Delegates are to assemble in the north hall. |
| `be about to` + bare infinitive | am/is/are about to + V | on the point of happening | The council is about to publish the findings. |
| `be due to` + to-infinitive | am/is/are due to + to-V | scheduled, expected at a stated time | The report is due to appear in June. |
| Future continuous | will be + V-ing | in progress at a future point; neutral enquiry | I'll be working late on Tuesday. |
| Future perfect | will have + V-en | finished by a future point | They'll have finished by autumn. |

**Full paradigm for `be going to`**, because it is the second most frequent future form and learners
get its negative and question wrong:

| Person | Positive | Contracted | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I am going to paint | I'm going to paint | I'm not going to paint | Am I going to paint? |
| you | you are going to write | you're going to write | you aren't / you're not going to write | Are you going to write? |
| he / she / it | he is going to paint | he's going to paint | he isn't / he's not going to paint | Is he going to paint? |
| we | we are going to write | we're going to write | we aren't going to write | Are we going to write? |
| they | they are going to paint | they're going to paint | they aren't going to paint | Are they going to paint? |

- Past: **`was / were going to`** = a plan that did not happen. *We were going to repaint it, but the
  funding fell through.* [H]
- The `to` in `going to` belongs to `going to`; a **bare infinitive** follows it: `going to paint`,
  not `*going to to paint`. [H]
- `going to go` is correct but clumsy; `We're going to Sandmouth` (present continuous) is the natural
  form. [M]
- **[SPOKEN]** `gonna` is the spoken reduction. It must never appear in writing. [SPOKEN]

**One-line contrast for the reference (full treatment in TH-R3):** `will` = decided now or predicted
from belief; `going to` = decided before now or predicted from what you can see; present continuous =
already arranged with someone; present simple = a published timetable.

## 4. The passive — every tense that has one, and the ones that do not

### 4.1 The rule, in one line

**The passive is `be` + past participle.** `be` takes whatever tense the sentence needs; the main
verb is frozen in its past-participle form and never changes again. That is the entire system. [H]

Turning an active sentence into a passive one, step by step:

| Step | Action | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Start with an active sentence that has an **object** | The council **painted** *the bridge*. |
| 1 | Move the **object** to the front — it becomes the new subject | *The bridge* … |
| 2 | Put **`be`** in the same tense as the original verb, agreeing with the new subject | The bridge **was** … |
| 3 | Put the main verb in its **past participle** form | The bridge was **painted**. |
| 4 | *Optionally* add the old subject after **`by`** | The bridge was painted **by the council**. |

**Only transitive verbs can do this**, because step 1 requires an object. That is the whole
explanation of §4.4. [H]

### 4.2 The passive in every tense

Using *paint* (regular) and *write* (irregular), with singular and plural subjects so the `be`
agreement is visible.

| Tense | Passive structure | Singular example | Plural example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present simple | am / is / are + V-en | The bridge **is painted** every third year. | The reports **are written** in March. |
| Present continuous | am / is / are **being** + V-en | The bridge **is being painted** this week. | The reports **are being written** now. |
| Present perfect | has / have **been** + V-en | The bridge **has been painted**. | The reports **have been written**. |
| Present perfect continuous | has / have **been being** + V-en | *(avoided — see §4.5)* | *(avoided)* |
| Past simple | was / were + V-en | The bridge **was painted** in 2019. | The reports **were written** last winter. |
| Past continuous | was / were **being** + V-en | The bridge **was being painted** when the storm hit. | The reports **were being written** at the time. |
| Past perfect | had **been** + V-en | The bridge **had been painted** before the survey. | The reports **had been written** by then. |
| Past perfect continuous | had **been being** + V-en | *(avoided — see §4.5)* | *(avoided)* |
| Future simple | will **be** + V-en | The bridge **will be painted** next spring. | The reports **will be written** in June. |
| Future continuous | will **be being** + V-en | *(avoided — see §4.5)* | *(avoided)* |
| Future perfect | will **have been** + V-en | The bridge **will have been painted** by August. | The reports **will have been written** by then. |
| Future perfect continuous | — | **no passive** (see §4.5) | **no passive** |
| `be going to` | am / is / are going to **be** + V-en | The bridge **is going to be painted**. | The reports **are going to be written**. |

**The `be`-chain in the passive**, laid out so the learner can see that nothing is arbitrary:

| Aspect | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| simple | paints | is painted |
| continuous | is painting | is **being** painted |
| perfect | has painted | has **been** painted |
| perfect continuous | has been painting | has been **being** painted *(theoretically formed, practically avoided)* |

### 4.3 Passives with modals, infinitives and -ing forms

| Structure | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| modal + bare infinitive | The crew **must repaint** the span. | The span **must be repainted**. |
| modal perfect | They **should have repainted** it. | It **should have been repainted**. |
| `have to` | They **have to repaint** it. | It **has to be repainted**. |
| `be going to` | They **are going to repaint** it. | It **is going to be repainted**. |
| to-infinitive | They want **to repaint** it. | It needs **to be repainted**. |
| perfect infinitive | It seems **to have repainted**… | It seems **to have been repainted**. |
| -ing form / gerund | I remember **repainting** it. | I remember **being repainted** *(only where sense allows)*; *The span resented being ignored* is the shape. |
| perfect -ing | **Having repainted** the span, they left. | **Having been repainted**, the span was reopened. |
| imperative | **Repaint** the span. | *(no passive imperative; use* Let the span be repainted *[FORMAL, rare] or rewrite)* |

### 4.4 Verbs that have no passive, and what is used instead

Three groups. Every one of them produces a recognisable learner error, and the chapter must name the
error, not just the rule.

**(a) Intransitive verbs — no object, so no passive.** This is the largest and most damaging group.

| Verb | The error learners produce | The correct form |
|---|---|---|
| happen | `*The accident was happened.` | The accident happened. |
| occur | `*A fault was occurred.` | A fault occurred. |
| arrive | `*We were arrived at noon.` | We arrived at noon. |
| appear / disappear | `*The funding was disappeared.` | The funding disappeared. |
| rise | `*Prices were risen.` | Prices rose. |
| fall | `*The figure was fallen.` | The figure fell. |
| die | `*He was died.` | He died. |
| exist | `*A rule was existed.` | A rule existed. |
| come / go | `*She was gone to Marlow.` | She went to Marlow. |
| remain / stay | `*It was remained open.` | It remained open. |
| seem / look / sound | `*It was seemed difficult.` | It seemed difficult. |
| belong | `*The land was belonged to the trust.` | The land belonged to the trust. |
| result | `*A delay was resulted.` | A delay resulted. / This resulted in a delay. |

**Why the error is so persistent:** the learner has correctly learned "the passive makes the sentence
sound formal and academic", and *rise*, *fall*, *occur* and *result* are exactly the verbs that fill
an academic Task 1 answer. The rule to give them is a *test*, not a prohibition: **if you cannot ask
"…something?" after the verb, it has no passive.** You can *paint something*; you cannot
*\*happen something*. [H]

**(b) Stative and measure verbs — they have an object grammatically but no passive.**

| Verb | No passive | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| have (= own) | `*A permit is had by the trust.` | The trust has a permit. |
| lack | `*Funding is lacked.` | It lacks funding. / Funding is lacking. |
| resemble | `*He is resembled by his brother.` | He resembles his brother. |
| suit / fit | `*The role is suited by her.` | The role suits her. *(but* she is suited to the role *is fine — different structure)* |
| cost | `*£40,000 was cost by the works.` | The works cost £40,000. |
| weigh / measure (= be of a weight) | `*Two tonnes is weighed by the beam.` | The beam weighs two tonnes. |
| equal / mean | `*The same thing is meant by it.` | It means the same thing. *(this exact passive is idiomatic and fine; the general point stands)* |

**(c) The tenses whose passive is grammatically formable but not used.** This is the honest answer to
"does every tense have a passive?" — and it is more useful than a flat "yes" or "no".

| Tense | Passive form | Status | What is used instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present perfect continuous | has been being painted | Formable, essentially never used — three auxiliaries in a row | The active: *They have been painting the bridge since April.* Or drop the aspect: *The bridge has been under repair since April.* |
| Past perfect continuous | had been being painted | Same | The active, or *had been under repair* |
| Future continuous | will be being painted | Same | *will be painted*, or *work will be going on* |
| Future perfect continuous | — | Not formed at all | The active |
| Imperative | — | Not formed | Rewrite: *The form must be completed* rather than a passive command |

**The safe statement for the chapter:** *eight of the twelve tenses have a passive you should use;
the four continuous-perfect combinations technically form one, but English speakers avoid stacking
`been being`, and so should you.* [H] [JUDGEMENT]

### 4.5 `get`-passive, double objects, causative, and the reporting passive

**The `get`-passive.** `get` + past participle replaces `be` in informal English, typically for
something that happens *to* the subject, unplanned, and often unwelcome.

| `be`-passive | `get`-passive | Register |
|---|---|---|
| The window was broken. | The window got broken. | [SPOKEN] |
| She was promoted. | She got promoted. | [SPOKEN] |
| The application was rejected. | The application got rejected. | [SPOKEN] |

**Do not use `get`-passives in IELTS Writing.** They are correct English and completely normal in
Speaking; in academic writing they read as informal. [SPOKEN]

Fixed exceptions that are neutral even in writing: *get married*, *get dressed*, *get lost*, *get
started*, *get involved*. [M]

**Verbs with two objects give two possible passives.** With *give, send, offer, show, tell, teach,
lend, pay, promise, award, hand, owe*, either object can become the subject. English strongly prefers
the **person** as the new subject.

| Active | Passive 1 (preferred) | Passive 2 |
|---|---|---|
| The trust gave the school a grant. | The school **was given** a grant. | A grant **was given to** the school. |
| They offered Verdon the contract. | Verdon **was offered** the contract. | The contract **was offered to** Verdon. |
| The tutor showed the class the data. | The class **was shown** the data. | The data **was shown to** the class. |

**Causative `have` / `get` something done** — you arrange for someone else to do it. Structure:
`have / get` + **object** + **past participle**.

| Structure | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| have + object + V-en | We **had the roof repaired**. | Neutral; safe in writing |
| get + object + V-en | We **got the roof repaired**. | Slightly more informal [SPOKEN-leaning] |
| have + object + V-en (unwanted event) | She **had her bicycle stolen**. | Something happened *to* her |
| get + object + to-infinitive | We **got the contractor to repair** it. | *Persuaded* — different structure, note the to-infinitive |
| have + object + bare infinitive | We **had the contractor repair** it. | *Instructed*; bare infinitive [BrE/AmE: more common in AmE] |

**The reporting passive — the highest-value passive for academic writing.** Two shapes, both from the
same active *People believe that the scheme is effective.*

| Shape | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Impersonal | It + is/was + V-en + that-clause | **It is believed that** the scheme is effective. |
| Personal | Subject + is/was + V-en + to-infinitive | The scheme **is believed to be** effective. |
| Personal, past reference | Subject + is/was + V-en + **to have** + V-en | The scheme **is believed to have been** effective. |

Verbs that take this pattern: *say, believe, think, know, report, claim, expect, understand, consider,
allege, estimate, assume, suggest, rumour, presume, find*. This is the structure that lets an academic
writer state a widely-held view without naming who holds it, and it is worth its own section in the
chapter. [H]

### 4.6 Two passive facts learners are never told

- **The `by`-phrase is usually left out.** The passive exists largely *so that* the agent can be
  omitted — because it is unknown, obvious, unimportant, or deliberately unnamed. Adding *by the
  workers* to *The bridge was painted* usually makes the sentence worse. Include the `by`-phrase only
  when the agent is the new information. → TH-R3 for the full decision. [H]
- **The past participle after `be` can also just be an adjective.** *The gate was closed* is ambiguous
  in English: it can describe an **action** (someone closed it) or a **state** (it was not open). The
  continuous disambiguates: *The gate was **being** closed* can only be the action. Learners who have
  not been told this find the passive slippery for no reason they can name. [H]

## 5. Modals and semi-modals — the complete inventory

### 5.1 What makes a modal a modal

The nine **central modals** — `can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must` — share five
properties, and the five properties are what the learner actually needs. [H]

| Property | Consequence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Followed by the **bare infinitive** | never `to`, never `-ing`, never a past form | `she must go`, not `*she must to go` |
| 2. **No `-s`** in the third person | one form for every person | `he can`, not `*he cans` |
| 3. **No `-ing`, no participle, no infinitive** | you cannot say `*to can`, `*musting` | use a semi-modal instead: `to be able to` |
| 4. They **are their own operator** | negatives and questions need no `do` | `She can't go.` `Can she go?` — not `*Does she can go?` |
| 5. **No true past tense** | past meaning is built with `have` + past participle, or with a semi-modal | `must` → `had to`; `must` (deduction) → `must have been` |

Property 5 is where every learner error lives, and it is why the table in §5.2 has a "past form or
past equivalent" column rather than a "past tense" column.

### 5.2 The nine central modals — full table

| Modal | Main meanings | Negative (full / contracted) | Question | Past form or past equivalent | Followed by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability; permission; possibility in general; informal request | cannot / **can't** | Can I…? | **could** (general past ability); **was/were able to** or **managed to** (one specific past success) | bare infinitive |
| **could** | past ability; polite request; present/future possibility; hypothetical | could not / **couldn't** | Could I…? | **could have** + past participle (unrealised past possibility) | bare infinitive |
| **may** | permission [FORMAL]; possibility | may not *(no contraction)* | May I…? *(permission only)* | **may have** + past participle (past possibility); **was/were allowed to** (past permission) | bare infinitive |
| **might** | possibility, slightly more remote than *may* | might not / **mightn't** [BrE, rare] | Might I…? [FORMAL, rare] | **might have** + past participle | bare infinitive |
| **will** | future; prediction; instant decision; offer; promise; willingness; habit | will not / **won't** | Will you…? | **would** (in reported speech and for past habit) | bare infinitive |
| **would** | hypothetical results; polite request/offer; past habit; reported *will* | would not / **wouldn't** | Would you…? | **would have** + past participle | bare infinitive |
| **shall** | offers and suggestions in questions [BrE]; obligation in legal text [FORMAL] | shall not / **shan't** [BrE, dated] | **Shall I…? Shall we…?** | **should** (in reported speech) | bare infinitive |
| **should** | advice; expectation; mild obligation | should not / **shouldn't** | Should I…? | **should have** + past participle (criticism / unfulfilled expectation) | bare infinitive |
| **must** | strong obligation from the speaker; confident deduction | must not / **mustn't** | Must I…? | obligation → **had to**; deduction → **must have** + past participle | bare infinitive |

**The three highest-cost facts in that table**, which the chapter must set out separately:

1. **`mustn't` and `don't have to` are opposites, not synonyms.** `mustn't` = it is forbidden;
   `don't have to` = it is not necessary, you may if you want. *Visitors mustn't enter the compound*
   (forbidden) vs *Visitors don't have to sign in* (optional). Learners routinely swap them and
   reverse the meaning of the sentence. [H]
2. **`must` has no past.** For past obligation, the only option is `had to`. `*I musted` and
   `*I must went` do not exist. `must have` + participle exists but means something entirely
   different — a deduction about the past. [H]
3. **`can` has no future and no infinitive.** `*I will can`, `*to can`, `*canning` do not exist. The
   gap is filled by `be able to`: `I will be able to`, `to be able to`, `being able to`. [H]

### 5.3 The modal perfect forms — `modal + have + past participle`

Every central modal builds its past meaning the same way, and the meanings are not guessable from the
parts, so they have to be listed.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **must have** + V-en | I am confident this was true | The tide **must have turned** early — the causeway is already clear. |
| **can't have** + V-en | I am confident this was not true | They **can't have finished** the survey; the equipment is still on site. |
| **couldn't have** + V-en | same as *can't have*, slightly softer; also "it was impossible" | She **couldn't have known** about the closure. |
| **could have** + V-en | it was possible but did not happen; or a past possibility, unresolved | The council **could have funded** it, but chose not to. |
| **might have / may have** + V-en | it is possible that this happened | The file **may have been archived** in 2016. |
| **might not have / may not have** + V-en | it is possible that this did not happen | They **might not have received** the notice. |
| **should have** + V-en | it was the right thing and it did not happen (criticism, regret) | We **should have checked** the tide table. |
| **shouldn't have** + V-en | it was the wrong thing and it did happen | They **shouldn't have removed** the barrier. |
| **ought to have** + V-en | as *should have*, slightly more formal | The report **ought to have included** the raw figures. |
| **would have** + V-en | the unreal past result (third conditional, §7) | With a second crew they **would have finished** on time. |
| **needn't have** + V-en | it was done, and it was unnecessary | We **needn't have booked** — the hall was empty. |
| **didn't need to** + V | it was unnecessary; usually implies it was not done | We **didn't need to book**, so we just turned up. |

**[BrE/AmE]** For a *negative deduction* about the past, British English uses **`can't have`**
(*He can't have seen the notice*). American English also uses **`must not have`** (*He must not have
seen the notice*), which sounds unusual to British ears in that meaning. Both are correct in their own
variety; **`can't have` is the safer default for a learner** because it is accepted everywhere. [M]

**`needn't have` vs `didn't need to`** is a genuine and rarely-taught meaning difference, not a
stylistic one, and the table above states it correctly. Verify it against a second source before
authoring — it is subtle. [M]

### 5.4 The certainty and obligation scales

Modals are far easier to hold in the head as two ordered scales than as nine separate words. This
ordering is a teaching device, not a fact about English, and the boundaries between neighbours are
genuinely fuzzy. [M] [JUDGEMENT]

**How sure am I? (deduction, present)**

| Strength | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| certain, from evidence | **must** be | **can't** be |
| probable | **should** be, **ought to** be, **will** be | shouldn't be, won't be |
| possible | **may** be, **might** be, **could** be | may not be, might not be |

**How necessary is it? (obligation)**

| Strength | Present | Past | Future |
|---|---|---|---|
| forbidden | **mustn't**, **can't**, **may not** | wasn't allowed to, couldn't | won't be allowed to |
| required | **must**, **have to**, **have got to** [BrE, SPOKEN], **need to**, **be required to** | **had to** | will have to |
| advised | **should**, **ought to**, **had better** | should have + V-en | should |
| optional | **don't have to**, **needn't**, **don't need to** | didn't have to, didn't need to | won't have to |

**The `must` / `have to` difference [M]:** `must` traditionally expresses an obligation the *speaker*
imposes or accepts (*I must finish this today*); `have to` expresses one imposed from outside (*I have
to renew the permit every year*). The distinction is real but weakening, and `have to` is taking over
in speech, especially in American English. Present it as a tendency, not a rule. [BrE/AmE] [M]

### 5.5 The semi-modals — the forms that fill the gaps

A **semi-modal** is a multi-word verb that behaves like a modal in meaning but has ordinary verb
forms, which is exactly why English needs them: they supply the past tenses, infinitives and
participles the central modals lack.

| Semi-modal | Meaning | Positive | Negative | Question | Past | Followed by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **have to** | external obligation | I have to / he has to | don't have to / doesn't have to | Do you have to…? | had to / didn't have to | **to-infinitive** |
| **have got to** [BrE, SPOKEN] | same, informal | I've got to | haven't got to | Have you got to…? | *(none — use* had to *)* | **to-infinitive** |
| **need to** (lexical) | necessity | I need to / he needs to | don't need to / doesn't need to | Do you need to…? | needed to / didn't need to | **to-infinitive** |
| **need** (modal) | necessity, non-affirmative only [BrE, FORMAL] | *(rare in positives)* | **needn't** | **Need I…?** | **needn't have** + V-en | **bare infinitive** |
| **dare** (lexical) | have the courage | I dare to / he dares to | don't dare (to) | Do you dare to…? | didn't dare (to) | to-infinitive, *to* often dropped after a negative |
| **dare** (modal) [BrE, fixed] | same | *(rare)* | **daren't** | **Dare I…?** | *(none)* | **bare infinitive** |
| **ought to** | advice, expectation | I ought to | ought not to / **oughtn't to** [rare] | Ought I to…? [FORMAL, rare] | **ought to have** + V-en | **to-infinitive** |
| **used to** | a past habit or state that is now over | I used to | **didn't use to** | **Did you use to…?** | *(is itself past — no present form)* | **to-infinitive** |
| **had better** | strong advice, with an implied bad consequence | I'd better | **I'd better not** | Had I better…? [rare] | *(none)* | **bare infinitive** |
| **would rather** | preference | I'd rather | **I'd rather not** | Would you rather…? | **would rather have** + V-en | **bare infinitive** |
| **be able to** | ability — supplies *can*'s missing forms | I am able to | am not able to / am unable to | Are you able to…? | was/were able to | **to-infinitive** |
| **be allowed to** | permission — supplies *may*'s missing forms | I am allowed to | am not allowed to | Am I allowed to…? | was/were allowed to | **to-infinitive** |
| **be supposed to** | expected, by arrangement or rule | I am supposed to | am not supposed to | Am I supposed to…? | was/were supposed to | **to-infinitive** |
| **be going to** | intention, prediction from evidence | I am going to | am not going to | Am I going to…? | was/were going to | **bare infinitive** |
| **be to** [FORMAL] | official instruction or arrangement | I am to | am not to | Am I to…? | was to; **was to have** + V-en (unfulfilled) | **to-infinitive** |
| **be about to** | on the point of | I am about to | am not about to | Am I about to…? | was about to | **bare infinitive** |
| **be due to** | scheduled | It is due to | is not due to | Is it due to…? | was due to | **to-infinitive** |
| **be bound to** | certain to happen | It is bound to | *(rare)* | Is it bound to…? | was bound to | **bare infinitive** |
| **be likely to** | probable | It is likely to | is unlikely to | Is it likely to…? | was likely to | **to-infinitive** |
| **may as well / might as well** | there is no reason not to | We may as well | *(rare)* | *(rare)* | might as well have + V-en | **bare infinitive** |

**Three form traps in that table that must be flagged in the chapter:**

- **`had better` and `would rather` take the BARE infinitive**, despite both containing a past-looking
  word. `You'd better leave now`, not `*You'd better to leave`. `I'd rather stay`, not `*I'd rather to
  stay`. And the negative goes **after** the whole phrase: `You'd better **not** leave`, not `*You
  hadn't better leave`. [H]
- **`ought to` and `used to` take the TO-infinitive**, unlike every central modal. `You ought to
  check`, `She used to work here`. [H]
- **`be going to` takes the BARE infinitive** because the `to` is already part of `going to`. [H]

**The `used to` spelling question, stated honestly.** In the negative and the question, `used` loses
its `-d` because `did` already carries the past: **`didn't use to`**, **`Did you use to…?`** That is
the form recommended by most style guides and the one BandReady should teach. However,
**`didn't used to`** is extremely common in real written English and many reference works now list it
as an accepted variant. The older forms `used not to` and `usedn't to` are [FORMAL] and dated, mainly
British. **Teach `didn't use to`; do not mark `didn't used to` as an error in learner writing.**
[M] [BrE/AmE] [JUDGEMENT]

**`used to` vs `be used to` vs `get used to` — three different structures that look alike.** This
catches almost every learner and it belongs in both this chapter and TH-R3.

| Structure | Meaning | Followed by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **used to** + V | a past habit that has stopped | **bare infinitive** | The trust **used to publish** an annual survey. |
| **be used to** + V-ing / noun | accustomed to something now | **-ing form or noun** | She **is used to working** in the field. |
| **get used to** + V-ing / noun | becoming accustomed | **-ing form or noun** | He is **getting used to the commute**. |

`*I am used to work here` means something different from what the learner intends, and `*I used to
working` is not English at all. [H]

### 5.6 `would` for past habit, and how it differs from `used to`

| Form | Works with actions? | Works with states? | Needs a stated past time frame? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **used to** + V | yes | **yes** — *There used to be a ferry.* | no |
| **would** + V | yes | **no** — `*There would be a ferry` does not have that meaning | usually yes |
| past simple | yes | yes | no |

The consequence: `used to` is the safe default, and `would` is only available for repeated *actions*
once a past setting has been established. → TH-R3. [H]

## 6. Non-finite forms — the shapes an infinitive, a gerund and a participle can take

Before the conditionals and the verb-pattern chapters can be written, the learner needs the
*inventory* of non-finite forms. "Non-finite" means the form carries no tense and no person of its
own. There are more of them than a beginner expects, and each one has a passive and a perfect
version. Using *write*:

| Form | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Bare infinitive | write | be written |
| To-infinitive | to write | to be written |
| Continuous infinitive | to be writing | — |
| Perfect infinitive | to have written | to have been written |
| Perfect continuous infinitive | to have been writing | — |
| Gerund / -ing form | writing | being written |
| Perfect gerund | having written | having been written |
| Past participle | *(not applicable)* | written |

**Negatives** go in front of the whole form, and there is no `do`: `not to write`, `not writing`,
`not having written`. `*to not write` occurs and is increasingly accepted, but `not to write` is the
safe form for exam writing. [M]

### 6.1 Where the BARE infinitive is required

This list is closed and short, which makes it worth memorising. Everywhere else that a verb follows a
verb, it is either a to-infinitive or an -ing form (§12).

| Trigger | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| after all nine central modals | She **must check** the readings. | §5.1 property 1 |
| after `do / does / did` | They **didn't check** the readings. | §2.5 |
| after `had better` | You'd **better check** it. | §5.5 |
| after `would rather` | I'd **rather check** it myself. | §5.5 |
| after `be going to` | We're going to **check** it. | the *to* is part of *going to* |
| after **make** + object | The rules **make everyone sign** in. | but the passive takes *to*: *everyone is made **to sign*** |
| after **let** + object | They **let us use** the hall. | *let* has no passive; use *were allowed to use* |
| after causative **have** + object | We **had the surveyor recheck** it. | [BrE/AmE] commoner in AmE |
| after perception verbs + object — `see, hear, watch, feel, notice, observe` | I **saw the crew finish**. | bare infinitive = you saw the **whole** action |
| after `help` | She **helped us carry** it. | `help us **to** carry` is equally correct — *to* is optional |
| after `why (not)` | **Why not check** the archive? | fixed question shape |
| after `rather than` | They rebuilt it **rather than repair** it. | *rather than repairing* is also correct |

**The perception-verb pair is a real meaning difference, not a free choice:**

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| see / hear + object + **bare infinitive** | you perceived the **complete** action from start to finish | I heard the pump **stop**. |
| see / hear + object + **-ing form** | you perceived it **in progress**, possibly only part of it | I heard the pump **running**. |

### 6.2 The split infinitive — the honest answer

Putting an adverb between `to` and the verb (*to carefully record*) was declared an error by
nineteenth-century prescriptive grammarians, and that judgement has been abandoned by essentially
every modern reference work; it is not a rule of English. However, **some readers still react to it**,
and in a timed exam the safest placement is before or after the whole infinitive (*carefully to
record* is stiff; *to record it carefully* is natural). **Teach it as a style preference with the
history stated, never as an error.** [M] [JUDGEMENT]

## 7. Conditionals — every type, with the tense in each half stated exactly

### 7.1 The two halves and the two systems

Every conditional sentence has two halves:

- the **`if`-clause** (the condition) — *If the grant is approved…*
- the **main clause** (the result) — *…the work will start in April.*

Either half can come first. **When the `if`-clause comes first, it is followed by a comma; when it
comes second, no comma is used.** [H]

> If the grant is approved, the work will start in April.
> The work will start in April if the grant is approved.

The numbered "types" are a teaching label, not a fact about English. Underneath, there are two
systems, and `01-syllabus.md` §5 teaches them that way:

| System | What it says | Verb machinery |
|---|---|---|
| **Real** (zero, first) | This might actually happen, or it always happens | Ordinary present and future forms |
| **Unreal** (second, third, mixed) | This is imaginary, or contrary to fact | The **unreal past**: a past-tense form used to signal distance from reality, not past time |

The unreal past is the key that unlocks the second conditional, the third conditional, mixed
conditionals, `wish`, `if only`, `would rather` + clause, `it's time` and `as if` — all one mechanism.
It is worth naming it once and reusing the name. [H]

### 7.2 The full table

| Type | `if`-clause — exact form | Main clause — exact form | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zero** | **present simple** | **present simple** | Always true; an automatic consequence | If the tide covers the causeway, the crossing closes. |
| **First** | **present simple** | **`will` + bare infinitive** | A real future possibility and its likely result | If the survey confirms the fault, the trust will close the span. |
| **Second** | **past simple** (or `were` for all persons) | **`would` + bare infinitive** | Imaginary present or future; unlikely or contrary to fact | If the trust owned the land, it would restore the wharf. |
| **Third** | **past perfect** (`had` + past participle) | **`would have` + past participle** | Imaginary past; it did not happen | If the funding had arrived in time, the crew would have finished the span. |
| **Mixed 1** — past condition, present result | **past perfect** | **`would` + bare infinitive** | A different past would have produced a different present | If the bypass had been built in 2011, Verdon would be a much quieter town today. |
| **Mixed 2** — present condition, past result | **past simple** | **`would have` + past participle** | A permanent present fact explains a past outcome | If Norland were nearer the coast, the harbour scheme would have gone ahead. |

**Read the table as a single pattern and it stops being six things:** the `if`-clause takes one step
back in time from where the meaning sits, and the result clause uses `will` for real and `would` for
unreal, plus `have + past participle` whenever the result is in the past. [JUDGEMENT]

### 7.3 What can be substituted into each half

The six rows above are the *core* patterns. Real English varies both halves freely, and a reference
that shows only the core patterns leaves the learner unable to read authentic text.

**Zero conditional — variants**

| Variant | Example |
|---|---|
| `when` instead of `if` (because it always happens) | When the temperature drops below zero, the sensors log a fault. |
| imperative in the result | If the alarm sounds, evacuate the north wing. |
| modal in the result | If a beam shows corrosion, it must be replaced. |

**First conditional — variants**

| Position | What can go there | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `if`-clause | present simple | If the council approves it, … |
| `if`-clause | present continuous | If you're travelling on Friday, … |
| `if`-clause | present perfect | If the team has finished the survey, … |
| `if`-clause | `should` (= if it happens to) [FORMAL] | If you should need the archive key, … |
| main clause | `will` | … the work will begin. |
| main clause | `may / might / could` (less certain) | … the work may begin in May. |
| main clause | `must / should / ought to` (obligation, advice) | … you must inform the office. |
| main clause | imperative | … send the file to Ashfield. |
| main clause | `be going to` | … they're going to appeal. |
| main clause | present simple (= a promise or a rule) | … the fee is waived. |

**Second conditional — variants**

| Position | What can go there | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `if`-clause | past simple | If the trust owned the land, … |
| `if`-clause | `were` for all persons [FORMAL] | If the trust **were** the owner, … |
| `if`-clause | past continuous | If the crew were working nights, … |
| `if`-clause | `were to` + infinitive (a remote hypothesis) [FORMAL] | If the council **were to sell** the site, … |
| main clause | `would` | … it would restore the wharf. |
| main clause | `could` (would be able to) | … it could restore the wharf. |
| main clause | `might` (would perhaps) | … it might restore the wharf. |

**Third conditional — variants**

| Position | What can go there | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `if`-clause | past perfect | If the funding had arrived, … |
| `if`-clause | past perfect continuous | If the crew had been working nights, … |
| main clause | `would have` + V-en | … they would have finished. |
| main clause | `could have` + V-en | … they could have finished. |
| main clause | `might have` + V-en | … they might have finished. |
| main clause | `would have been` + V-ing | … they would have been finishing by June. |

### 7.4 `were` or `was` in the second conditional — stated honestly

In the second conditional, formal English uses **`were` for every person**, including *I*, *he*, *she*
and *it*. This is the last surviving fragment of the English subjunctive.

| Formal / written | Informal / spoken |
|---|---|
| If I **were** in charge, … | If I **was** in charge, … |
| If the site **were** larger, … | If the site **was** larger, … |

- **`If I were you…` is fixed.** Nobody says `*If I was you` in the advice formula, in either variety.
- **For IELTS Writing, use `were`.** It is never wrong, in either British or American English.
- **For Speaking, either is fine**, and `was` is extremely common in both varieties.
- **This is a formal/informal split, not a British/American one.** Both varieties do both. [H]

### 7.5 `will` and `would` in the `if`-clause — the exceptions to the exception

The rule learners are taught is "never put `will` or `would` in the `if`-clause". The rule is right
about 95% of the time, and the exceptions are real English that learners will meet.

| Not English | Correct | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `*If it will rain, we'll postpone.` | If it rains, we'll postpone. | Plain future condition — present form only |
| `*If I would have known, I'd have called.` | If I had known, I'd have called. | The `if`-clause of a third conditional takes the past perfect. `*would have` here is a very common error and it is an error in every standard variety. [H] |

| Genuine exception | Example | Meaning of `will` / `would` there |
|---|---|---|
| Willingness | If you **will** sign at the bottom, I'll process it today. [FORMAL, polite] | *are willing to* — not future |
| Insistence (stressed) | If you **will** leave the gate open, you can't be surprised when the sheep get out. | *insist on* — mild reproach |
| Polite request | If you **would** wait here, someone will come through. [FORMAL] | politeness marker |
| Result-in-the-if-clause | If it **will** help, I'll bring the older maps. | the *if*-clause refers to the result, not the condition |

**Authoring instruction:** show the exceptions, but label them clearly as advanced reading knowledge.
A learner who half-remembers this list produces `*If it will rain`. [JUDGEMENT]

### 7.6 Inverted conditionals — `if` removed, operator moved to the front

Formal written English can drop `if` and invert instead. Only three forms do this, and none of them
uses `did`. [H]

| Type | Inverted form | Equivalent | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second (with `be`) | **Were** the site any larger, a second access road would be required. | If the site were any larger… | [FORMAL] |
| Second (with `were to`) | **Were** the council **to sell** the site, the covenant would lapse. | If the council were to sell… | [FORMAL] |
| Third | **Had** the survey been completed earlier, the closure would have been avoided. | If the survey had been completed… | [FORMAL] |
| First / remote possibility | **Should** you require the raw data, it is held at Brackenfield. | If you should require… | [FORMAL] |

**Negatives do not contract in inverted conditionals:** `Had the survey **not** been completed…`, not
`*Hadn't the survey been completed…`. [H]

`*Did the council own the site…` is **not** an inverted conditional; inversion with `do` is a
question, not a condition. [H]

### 7.7 Other ways to express a condition

| Word / phrase | Meaning | Verb form after it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **unless** | except if | present form for future reference — never `will` | The gate stays locked unless the warden is on site. |
| **provided (that) / providing (that)** | only if this condition holds [FORMAL] | present form | Access is granted provided that a permit is displayed. |
| **as long as / so long as** | only if, for the whole time that | present form | The offer stands as long as the survey is valid. |
| **on condition that** | only if [FORMAL] | present form or `should` | Funding was released on condition that the trust reported quarterly. |
| **in the event that** [FORMAL] | if this happens | present form or `should` | In the event that the ferry is cancelled, use the coast road. |
| **assuming (that)** | taking it as given | present form | Assuming the figures are correct, the trend is downward. |
| **suppose / supposing / what if** | invites a hypothesis | present (real) or past (unreal) | Supposing the grant fell through, what then? |
| **in case** | as a precaution, *before* anything happens | **present** form for the future — never `will` | Take a spare battery in case the meter fails. |
| **but for** + noun / -ing | if it were not for | *(no clause)* | But for the delay, the scheme would have finished on time. |
| **otherwise / or else** | if not | ordinary clause | Book by Friday; otherwise the rate goes up. |
| **even if** | this makes no difference | as for the matching conditional type | Even if the appeal succeeded, the site would stay closed. |
| **whether … or not** | in both cases | present form | The survey goes ahead whether the weather improves or not. |
| **only if** | this and nothing less | inverts the **main** clause when `only if` is first | **Only if** the council agrees **will** the work start. |

**`unless` is not always interchangeable with `if not`.** `unless` means *except if*. It cannot be
used when the `if not` clause states something the speaker imagines *would cause* the main clause to
be different in a hypothetical judgement:

> I'd be surprised **if** the trust **didn't** appeal.
> `*I'd be surprised unless the trust appealed.` — not English.

Also, `unless` is not used in unreal (second/third) conditionals in most contexts; use `if … not`.
**[M]** — this boundary is genuinely fuzzy and the chapter should show the safe cases and say the
edge is disputed rather than inventing a crisp rule.

**`in case` is not `if`.** `in case` = do it *in advance*, because the thing *might* happen. `if` = do
it *only when* the thing happens.

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Take a spare battery **in case** the meter fails. | Take it now, before anything happens. |
| Take a spare battery **if** the meter fails. | Only go and get one after it fails. |

**[BrE/AmE]** American English also uses `in case` where British English says `if`, in sentences like
*In case you need help, call the office* — the "precaution" reading is the British default. Show both
readings. [M]

### 7.8 The rest of the unreal-past family — same machinery, different word

Once the learner has the unreal past, these come free. Every one of them uses a past form to mean
*not real*, not *earlier*.

| Structure | Form after it | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **wish / if only** + past simple | past simple | regret about **now** | I wish the archive **were** open at weekends. |
| **wish / if only** + past perfect | past perfect | regret about the **past** | If only we **had booked** the survey earlier. |
| **wish** + `would` | `would` + bare infinitive | annoyance at somebody else's repeated behaviour | I wish the contractor **would answer** his messages. |
| **would rather** + subject + past simple | past simple | I prefer that somebody else does / doesn't do something now | I'd rather you **didn't** move the boxes. |
| **would rather** + subject + past perfect | past perfect | preference about the past | I'd rather they **hadn't published** the draft. |
| **it's (high / about) time** + subject + past simple | past simple | it should have happened already | It's time the council **replaced** the footbridge. |
| **as if / as though** + past simple | past simple | comparison to something untrue | He talks about the wharf as if he **owned** it. |
| **suppose / imagine** + past simple | past simple | a hypothesis | Suppose the grant **fell** through — what then? |

**Two restrictions on `wish + would` that are almost never taught:**

- You cannot wish about **yourself** with `would`: `*I wish I would stop worrying` — say *I wish I
  could stop worrying*.
- You cannot use `would` for a **state**: `*I wish the hall would be bigger` — say *I wish the hall
  were bigger*. `wish + would` is for another person's changeable behaviour. [H]

## 8. Question formation

### 8.1 The auxiliary-inversion rule, step by step

This is the single most mechanical procedure in English grammar, and once a learner has it, every
question in the language falls out of it. Present it as five numbered steps, with a worked example
running down the side.

| Step | Instruction | Worked: *The council approves the plan.* | Worked: *She has written the report.* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Find the **operator** — the first auxiliary or modal in the sentence (`be`, `have`, `will`, `can`, `must`…). | There is none. | The operator is **has**. |
| **2** | **If there is no operator, supply `do`.** `do` takes the tense and the person; the main verb goes back to its **base form**. | approves → **does** + **approve** | — (skip) |
| **3** | **Invert:** put the operator in front of the subject. | Does the council approve…? | Has she written…? |
| **4** | Leave everything else in its original order. | Does the council approve the plan? | Has she written the report? |
| **5** | If there is a question word, put it **first** — unless it is the subject (§8.3). | When does the council approve the plan? | Why has she written the report? |

**Two special cases of step 1:**

- **Main-verb `be` is its own operator.** `She is ready.` → `Is she ready?` No `do`. [H]
- **Main-verb `have` needs `do`** in modern standard English. `She has a permit.` → `Does she have a
  permit?` The older `Has she a permit?` survives in [BrE] as a dated formal register and should not
  be taught for production. [BrE/AmE] [M]

**The errors this rule prevents**, all of which come from omitting step 2:

| Wrong | Right | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|
| `*Does the council approves the plan?` | Does the council approve the plan? | The `-s` moved to `do` and was left on the verb as well |
| `*Did she wrote the report?` | Did she write the report? | The past moved to `did` and was left on the verb as well |
| `*Where you did go?` | Where did you go? | Step 3 skipped after step 5 |
| `*What means this word?` | What does this word mean? | Step 2 skipped |
| `*You are coming?` | Are you coming? | Step 3 skipped. (This form exists in speech as a rising-intonation question, but it is [SPOKEN] and must not appear in writing.) |
| `*Is the report was written?` | Was the report written? | Two operators supplied |

### 8.2 The question words

| Word | Asks about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | a person (subject or object) | Who signed the release? |
| **whom** | a person, object position [FORMAL] | To whom should the form be sent? |
| **whose** | possession | Whose signature is on the form? |
| **what** | a thing; a choice from an open set | What caused the delay? |
| **which** | a choice from a **limited, known** set | Which of the two routes is shorter? |
| **when** | time | When did the survey close? |
| **where** | place | Where is the archive held? |
| **why** | reason | Why was the span closed? |
| **how** | manner or method | How did they measure the drift? |
| **how much / how many** | quantity | How many spans were repainted? |
| **how long** | duration | How long has the ferry been running? |
| **how often** | frequency | How often is the bridge inspected? |
| **how far / high / deep / wide / old** | measurement | How far is Sandmouth from Marlow? |
| **what … like** | description | What is the coast road like in winter? |
| **what kind / sort / type of** | category | What kind of survey was it? |
| **what … for** | purpose | What is the second valve for? |

**`what` vs `which`:** `which` implies the choice is from a set both speakers can see or have
already named; `what` leaves the set open. Both are often possible. [M]

**`whom`.** Correct after a preposition (*to whom*, *for whom*, *with whom*) and as an object. In
everyday English, including everyday writing, `who` has almost entirely replaced it and the
preposition moves to the end. Teach both, and teach that **`whom` is never wrong in object position
but can sound very formal**, whereas using `whom` in *subject* position (`*Whom wrote it?`) is a
straightforward error. [M]

**Prepositions in questions.** English normally leaves the preposition at the end:

| Everyday | Formal |
|---|---|
| Who did she write **to**? | **To whom** did she write? |
| What is the grant intended **for**? | **For what** is the grant intended? *(stiff — rewrite)* |
| Which department does he report **to**? | **To which department** does he report? |

Both are correct. The "never end a sentence with a preposition" rule is a prescriptive invention with
no basis in how English works, and the reference should say so plainly while noting that the fronted
version reads as more formal. [M] [JUDGEMENT]

### 8.3 Subject questions vs object questions — the contrast

This is where step 2 of §8.1 stops applying, and it is one of the highest-value half-pages in the
whole reference because the two look almost identical and mean completely different things.

**The rule:** if the question word **is the subject** — if it stands where the subject would stand —
there is **no inversion and no `do`**. The question word simply takes the subject's place and the
sentence keeps statement order. [H]

| | **Subject question** | **Object question** |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | wh-word + verb (statement order) | wh-word + operator + subject + verb |
| `do` needed? | **No** | **Yes**, if there is no other operator |
| Example | **Who** wrote the report? | **Who** did the trust appoint? |
| Answer | *The site engineer* wrote it. | They appointed *the site engineer*. |
| Example | **What** caused the delay? | **What** did the delay cause? |
| Example | **Which team** won the contract? | **Which team** did they beat? |
| Example | **How many people** attended? | **How many people** did they invite? |
| Example | **Whose signature** appears on it? | **Whose signature** did they check? |
| Wrong | `*Who did write the report?` *(unless heavily emphatic)* | `*Who the trust appointed?` |

**A test the learner can apply in one second:** answer the question in a full sentence. If the answer
goes **before** the verb, it was a subject question and you need no `do`. If it goes **after**, it was
an object question and you do.

> Q: Who signed it? → A: **The warden** signed it. → answer is before the verb → subject question.
> Q: Who did she call? → A: She called **the warden**. → answer is after the verb → object question.

**Agreement in subject questions:** `who` and `what` as subjects take a **singular** verb by default,
even when the expected answer is plural. *Who is coming to the meeting?* — the answer may be six
people. `Who are coming?` is not used. [H]

**Subject questions in the past look deceptively simple:** `Who wrote it?` uses the past simple
directly, with no `did` anywhere. Learners who have just been drilled on `did` insert one and produce
`*Who did wrote it?` or `*Who did write it?` (the latter is grammatical only as an emphatic
contradiction: *Who **did** write it, then?*). [H]

### 8.4 Negative questions

| Form | Structure | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contracted | `n't` attaches to the operator, in front of the subject | normal | **Didn't** the council approve it? |
| Uncontracted | `not` goes **after** the subject | [FORMAL] | **Did** the council **not** approve it? |

**What they mean.** A negative question is rarely a neutral request for information. It usually
carries either **surprise** (*Haven't they finished yet?*) or an **expectation of agreement**
(*Isn't the coast road quicker?*). [M]

**Answering a negative question is a genuine trap.** English answers the *fact*, not the question's
polarity: to *Didn't they finish?*, `Yes` means *they finished* and `No` means *they didn't*. Many
languages do the opposite, and the chapter should say so, because it produces real
misunderstandings. [H]

### 8.5 Tag questions — the complete rules, including all the irregular tags

A tag question is a short question added to the end of a statement: *The ferry runs on Sundays,
**doesn't it**?* Tags belong to **speech**. They are natural and useful in IELTS Speaking and they
have essentially no place in IELTS Writing. [SPOKEN]

**The four-step rule:**

| Step | Instruction |
|---|---|
| 1 | Take the **operator** from the statement. If there is none, supply `do / does / did`. |
| 2 | **Reverse the polarity.** Positive statement → negative tag. Negative statement → positive tag. |
| 3 | Replace the subject with a **pronoun**. |
| 4 | Contract the negative tag (`isn't it`, not `*is not it`). |

| Statement | Tag | Note |
|---|---|---|
| The survey is finished, | **isn't it?** | positive → negative |
| The survey isn't finished, | **is it?** | negative → positive |
| The council approved it, | **didn't they?** | no operator → `did` |
| She has written it, | **hasn't she?** | operator `has` |
| They'll be there, | **won't they?** | operator `will` |
| You can drive, | **can't you?** | operator `can` |
| We should ask, | **shouldn't we?** | operator `should` |

**The irregular and non-obvious tags — every one of them:**

| Statement | Tag | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **I am** late, | **aren't I?** | English has no `*amn't I`. `aren't I?` is standard in both British and American English in speech. The formal alternative is **`am I not?`** [H] |
| I am not late, | **am I?** | the positive tag is regular |
| **Let's** start, | **shall we?** | fixed; never `*don't we` |
| Let me try, / Let him wait, | **will you?** | this `let` = *allow*, so the tag addresses the listener |
| **Close the gate,** *(imperative)* | **will you? / would you? / can you? / could you?** | all four are used; `would/could` are politer |
| **Don't close the gate,** *(negative imperative)* | **will you?** | the tag stays **positive** — this is the one imperative tag that does not reverse |
| Come in, | **won't you?** | a warm invitation, not a command [FORMAL/polite] |
| **There's** a fault in the wiring, | **isn't there?** | `there` repeats as the subject |
| There aren't any spare keys, | **are there?** | |
| **Nobody / no one** objected, | **did they?** | *nobody* is negative → positive tag; the pronoun is **they** |
| **Nothing** was damaged, | **was it?** | *nothing* → `it` |
| **Everybody / someone** signed, | **didn't they?** | indefinite people → **they** |
| **Everything** is ready, | **isn't it?** | indefinite things → **it** |
| **This / that** is the right file, | **isn't it?** | *this/that* → **it** |
| **These / those** are the originals, | **aren't they?** | *these/those* → **they** |
| I **used to** work there, | **didn't I?** | `used to` takes `did` |
| You'd **better** check, | **hadn't you?** | the operator is `had` |
| You'd **rather** wait, | **wouldn't you?** | the operator is `would` |
| He **has to** sign it, | **doesn't he?** | `have to` is a main verb → `do` |
| He's **got to** sign it, | **hasn't he?** | [BrE] `have got to` keeps `have` |
| She **has** a permit, | **doesn't she?** | main-verb `have` → `do` in modern usage |
| They **ought to** be told, | **shouldn't they? / oughtn't they?** | `shouldn't they?` is far commoner [M] |
| **I think** she's coming, | **isn't she?** | with *I think/suppose/believe*, the tag matches the **inner** clause, not *I think* |
| **I don't think** she's coming, | **is she?** | the negative in *I don't think* transfers to the inner clause |

**Words that count as negative even without `not`**, and therefore take a **positive** tag: *never,
hardly, scarcely, barely, seldom, rarely, few, little, no, none, nobody, nothing, nowhere, neither*.

> The ferry rarely runs in January, **does it?**
> There were few objections, **were there?**

**Intonation changes the meaning**, and this belongs in a Speaking-linked chapter:

| Intonation on the tag | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Falling** | I am fairly sure; I want you to agree | *The gate's locked, isn't it? ↘* |
| **Rising** | I genuinely don't know; this is a real question | *The gate's locked, isn't it? ↗* |

**Same-polarity tags** — *So you've finished, **have you**?* — exist and carry interest, surprise or
sarcasm. Correct in speech; do not teach them for production and never for writing. [SPOKEN]

### 8.6 Embedded (indirect) questions — the word order goes back to normal

When a question is put **inside** another sentence, it stops being a question grammatically: the
inversion is undone and no `do` is supplied. This is the mirror image of §8.1 and it must be shown
next to it. [H]

| Direct question | Embedded |
|---|---|
| Where **is** the archive? | Could you tell me where the archive **is**? |
| When **did** the survey **start**? | Do you know when the survey **started**? |
| What **does** this symbol **mean**? | I'm not sure what this symbol **means**. |
| Why **has** the span **been** closed? | Nobody has explained why the span **has been** closed. |
| **Does** the library **open** on Sundays? | Could you tell me **whether/if** the library **opens** on Sundays? |
| **Is** there a later ferry? | I wondered **whether/if** there **was** a later ferry. |

**The three rules:**

1. **Statement word order** — subject before verb. `*Could you tell me where is the archive?` is the
   single most common error in this area and it survives to a high level. [H]
2. **No `do / does / did`** — the tense goes back onto the main verb. `when the survey **started**`,
   not `*when did the survey start`. [H]
3. **Yes/no questions need `whether` or `if`** to join on, because there is no question word to do
   the joining. In formal writing prefer **`whether`**; before a to-infinitive and before `or not`
   immediately, `whether` is required: *whether to appeal*, *whether or not to appeal*. [M]

**Punctuation.** A question mark is used only if the **whole sentence** is a question.

> Could you tell me where the archive is**?** ← the outer sentence is a question
> I wonder where the archive is**.** ← the outer sentence is a statement

**Common introductions that trigger the embedded pattern:**
*Could you tell me…, Do you know…, Do you have any idea…, I'd like to know…, I wonder…, Can you
explain…, I'm not sure…, It isn't clear…, The report examines…, The question is…, Nobody knows…*

**This is not only a politeness structure.** The same word order is required in academic writing
whenever a question becomes a noun clause: *The study investigates **why emissions rose** after 2015*
— not `*why did emissions rise`. Learners who file this under "polite questions" never transfer it to
Writing, so the chapter must show the academic use in the same table. [JUDGEMENT]

### 8.7 Short questions and echo questions [SPOKEN]

| Form | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Reply questions | "The ferry's been cancelled." "**Has it?**" | shows interest — operator + pronoun, same polarity |
| Echo questions | "They've closed the causeway." "**They've closed the what?**" | asks for a repeated word |
| Elliptical questions | "**Coming?**" "**Any luck?**" | words omitted where recoverable |
| Question tags on their own | "**Right?**" "**Yeah?**" | seeks confirmation |

All four are [SPOKEN]. They are worth *recognising* for IELTS Listening and worth a light touch in
Speaking; none of them belongs in Writing.

## 9. Reported speech

### 9.1 Direct and reported — what changes

**Direct speech** repeats the exact words, inside quotation marks. **Reported (indirect) speech**
puts them into your own sentence, with no quotation marks.

> Direct: The warden said, "I am closing the gate at six."
> Reported: The warden said that he **was** closing the gate at six.

Four things can change, and they change independently:

| # | What changes | Because |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The verb tense** — usually one step further back ("backshift") | The reporting is happening later than the speaking |
| 2 | **Pronouns and possessives** | The speaker is now somebody else |
| 3 | **Time and place words** | *here* and *tomorrow* meant something different at the time |
| 4 | **Word order, in questions** | A reported question is not a question (§9.5) |

`that` is optional after most reporting verbs and is usually kept in formal writing: *She said (that)
the survey was complete.* [H]

### 9.2 The backshift table — from → to

This is the table the chapter renders. The left column is what the person actually said; the right
column is what you write when you report it **with a past-tense reporting verb** (*said, told,
explained, claimed*).

| # | Direct speech (what was said) | → | Reported speech |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **present simple** — *"The survey is complete."* | → | **past simple** — said the survey **was** complete |
| 2 | **present continuous** — *"I am writing the summary."* | → | **past continuous** — said she **was writing** the summary |
| 3 | **present perfect** — *"We have finished the span."* | → | **past perfect** — said they **had finished** the span |
| 4 | **present perfect continuous** — *"I have been checking the figures."* | → | **past perfect continuous** — said he **had been checking** the figures |
| 5 | **past simple** — *"The council approved it."* | → | **past perfect** — said the council **had approved** it *(often left unchanged — see §9.3)* |
| 6 | **past continuous** — *"I was working late."* | → | **past perfect continuous** — said she **had been working** late *(often left unchanged)* |
| 7 | **past perfect** — *"They had already left."* | → | **past perfect** — **no change possible**; there is nowhere further back to go |
| 8 | **past perfect continuous** — *"It had been raining."* | → | **past perfect continuous** — **no change** |
| 9 | **`will`** — *"I will send it."* | → | **`would`** — said he **would** send it |
| 10 | **`will be` + -ing** — *"I'll be working."* | → | **`would be` + -ing** — said she **would be** working |
| 11 | **`will have` + V-en** — *"I'll have finished."* | → | **`would have` + V-en** — said he **would have** finished |
| 12 | **`be going to`** — *"We are going to appeal."* | → | **`was / were going to`** — said they **were going to** appeal |
| 13 | **`can`** — *"I can drive."* | → | **`could`** — said she **could** drive |
| 14 | **`may`** (possibility) — *"It may rain."* | → | **`might`** — said it **might** rain |
| 15 | **`may`** (permission) — *"You may leave."* | → | **`could`** or **`might`** — said they **could** leave |
| 16 | **`must`** (obligation) — *"You must sign it."* | → | **`had to`** — said we **had to** sign it |
| 17 | **`must`** (deduction) — *"It must be the wrong file."* | → | **`must`** — **no change**; said it **must** be the wrong file |
| 18 | **`shall`** (future) — *"I shall write."* | → | **`would`** — said he **would** write |
| 19 | **`shall`** (offer/suggestion) — *"Shall I open it?"* | → | **`should`** — asked whether he **should** open it |
| 20 | **`have to`** — *"I have to leave."* | → | **`had to`** — said she **had to** leave |
| 21 | **imperative** — *"Sign the form."* | → | **to-infinitive** — told us **to sign** the form (§9.6) |
| 22 | **negative imperative** — *"Don't sign it."* | → | **`not to`** + infinitive — told us **not to sign** it |

**The modals and semi-modals that never backshift** — they are already past-marked or have no past
form, so they stay exactly as they are: **would, could, might, should, ought to, used to, had better,
must (deduction), needn't**, and any modal perfect (**would have, could have, should have, might
have**). [H]

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| "I **would** check the archive first." | said she **would** check the archive first |
| "It **could** be a printing error." | said it **could** be a printing error |
| "You **should** have told me." | said I **should have** told her |
| "We **used to** publish it annually." | said they **used to** publish it annually |
| "You'd **better** confirm it." | said we'd **better** confirm it |

### 9.3 When backshift is optional, and when it does not happen at all

This is the part most references compress into a footnote, and it is where learners over-apply the
rule and produce stilted or wrong English. Backshift is a **consequence of the time gap**, not a
mechanical obligation. [H]

| Situation | Backshift? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The reporting verb is present, present perfect or future** — *she says, she has said, she will say* | **No backshift at all** | She says the survey **is** complete. |
| **You are reporting something moments later, still in the same situation** | Optional; no backshift is natural | He just told me the gate **is** locked. |
| **The statement is still true now** | **Optional** — both correct | She said the archive **opens** / **opened** at nine. |
| **A permanent fact or a general truth** | **Optional**, and no-backshift is common | The tutor explained that saltwater **corrodes** the fittings. |
| **The original was already past perfect** | **Impossible** — nowhere further back | He said they **had** already **left**. |
| **The original used an unreal past** — second/third conditional, `wish`, `would rather`, `it's time`, `as if` | **No backshift** — the past form is already doing a non-past job | She said that if the trust **owned** the site, it **would** restore the wharf. |
| **The original used `would / could / might / should / ought to / used to`** | **No backshift** — no further-back form exists | He said he **might** come. |
| **A past simple with a stated finished time** | **Optional**, and no-backshift is very common | She said the council **approved** it in 2019. |
| **The time reference is still valid** | Time words do **not** shift | She said this morning that she **is** flying **tomorrow**. *(if it is still today)* |
| **Backshift would create a false meaning** | Do not backshift | *He said he lives in Marlow* — he still does. *He said he lived in Marlow* can suggest he no longer does. |

**The honest summary the chapter should print:** *backshift is normal, but it is not compulsory when
the words are still true at the moment you report them. If in doubt in an exam, backshift — it is
never grammatically wrong, only sometimes slightly less natural.* [JUDGEMENT]

### 9.4 Pronoun, time and place changes

**Pronouns and possessives** shift to fit the new speaker and listener. There is no table that can do
this mechanically; the learner has to think about who is now speaking to whom.

> Direct: "**I** left **my** copy on **your** desk."
> Reported (I am reporting what she said to me): She said she had left **her** copy on **my** desk.

**Time and place**, when the reporting happens later or somewhere else:

| Direct | → | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| now | → | then / at that time |
| today | → | that day |
| tonight | → | that night |
| this morning | → | that morning |
| yesterday | → | the day before / the previous day |
| the day before yesterday | → | two days before |
| tomorrow | → | the next day / the following day |
| the day after tomorrow | → | two days later |
| last week / month / year | → | the week / month / year before, or the previous week |
| next week / month / year | → | the following week, or the week after |
| a week ago | → | a week before / the previous week |
| this (+ noun) | → | that |
| these | → | those |
| here | → | there |
| this place | → | that place |
| come | → | go |
| bring | → | take |

**These shifts are not automatic either.** If you are still in the same place, `here` stays `here`.
If you are reporting on the same day, `today` stays `today`. The shift happens **only when the
original word would now point at the wrong thing**. Learners taught the table as a rule produce
sentences that are grammatically fine and factually wrong. [H]

### 9.5 Reported questions

Reported questions use the **embedded question** word order from §8.6 — which is why §8 comes first.

| Rule | Direct | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| **No inversion** | "Where **is** the archive?" | She asked where the archive **was**. |
| **No `do / does / did`** | "When **did** the survey **start**?" | He asked when the survey **had started**. |
| **Yes/no questions need `if` or `whether`** | "**Is** there a later ferry?" | I asked **whether** there **was** a later ferry. |
| **No question mark** | "Who signed it?" | She asked who **had signed** it. |
| **Subject questions keep their order** | "Who signed it?" | She asked who **had signed** it. *(already statement order)* |
| **`whether` before a to-infinitive** | "Should I appeal?" | She wondered **whether to appeal**. |

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `*She asked where was the archive.` | She asked where the archive was. |
| `*He asked when did the survey start.` | He asked when the survey had started. |
| `*I asked that there was a later ferry.` | I asked whether there was a later ferry. |
| `*She asked me where the archive was?` | She asked me where the archive was. |

**`ask` takes an optional person object**: *She asked **me** where the archive was.* **`wonder`,
`enquire` and `want to know` do not**: `*I wondered him whether…` is not English. [H]

### 9.6 Reported commands, requests and advice

The imperative does not survive into reported speech. It becomes a **to-infinitive**.

| Function | Direct | Reported |
|---|---|---|
| Order | "Sign the form." | The clerk **told us to sign** the form. |
| Negative order | "Don't move the barrier." | The warden **told them not to move** the barrier. |
| Request | "Could you send the file?" | She **asked me to send** the file. |
| Advice | "You should check the tide table." | He **advised us to check** the tide table. |
| Warning | "Don't use the coast road." | She **warned us not to use** the coast road. |
| Reminder | "Remember to sign it." | He **reminded me to sign** it. |
| Invitation | "Would you like to come?" | They **invited me to come**. |
| Offer | "I'll carry that." | He **offered to carry** it. |
| Refusal | "I won't sign it." | She **refused to sign** it. |
| Promise | "I'll send it today." | He **promised to send** it that day. |
| Suggestion | "Let's postpone it." | She **suggested postponing** it. / She **suggested that we postpone** it. |

**Note the odd one out:** `suggest` does **not** take a to-infinitive. `*She suggested me to
postpone it` is one of the most persistent errors in learner English. The three correct patterns are
**`suggest + -ing`**, **`suggest that + subject + base form`** [FORMAL] and **`suggest that +
subject + should + base form`** [BrE]. [H]

### 9.7 Reporting verbs and the patterns they take

Choosing a precise reporting verb instead of `say` is one of the cheapest upgrades available in IELTS
Writing — but each verb has a fixed pattern and using the wrong pattern costs more than the upgrade
gains. This table is the pattern list.

| Pattern | Verbs that take it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **verb + that-clause** | say, admit, agree, announce, argue, claim, complain, confirm, deny, explain, insist, maintain, mention, note, observe, point out, promise, recommend, report, reply, state, suggest, warn | The engineer **explained that** the fault was structural. |
| **verb + object + that-clause** | tell, inform, remind, warn, assure, notify, persuade, convince | The clerk **informed us that** the office had moved. |
| **verb + to-infinitive** | agree, claim, decide, demand, offer, promise, refuse, threaten, volunteer, swear | The contractor **refused to extend** the guarantee. |
| **verb + object + to-infinitive** | tell, ask, advise, remind, warn, order, instruct, invite, encourage, persuade, urge, forbid, beg, request, expect, want | The surveyor **advised the trust to postpone** the work. |
| **verb + -ing** | admit, deny, mention, recommend, suggest, propose, report, regret | She **denied altering** the figures. |
| **verb + preposition + -ing** | apologise **for**, insist **on**, object **to**, confess **to**, boast **about**, complain **about** | He **apologised for missing** the meeting. |
| **verb + object + preposition + -ing** | accuse sb **of**, congratulate sb **on**, blame sb **for**, thank sb **for**, warn sb **against**, praise sb **for**, criticise sb **for**, discourage sb **from**, prevent sb **from** | They **accused the contractor of overcharging**. |
| **verb + (that) + subject + should** | suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose, request, advise | The board **recommended that the trust should appoint** an auditor. |
| **verb + (that) + subject + base form** [FORMAL, the subjunctive] | suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose, request, advise, urge | The board **recommended that the trust appoint** an auditor. |

**`say` vs `tell` — the difference is the object, not the meaning.** [H]

| Verb | Takes a person object? | Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **say** | **No** (unless with `to`) | She **said** the gate was locked. / She **said to me** that… | `*She said me that…` |
| **tell** | **Yes, obligatory** | She **told me** the gate was locked. | `*She told that the gate was locked.` |

Fixed exceptions where `tell` takes no person: *tell the truth, tell a lie, tell the time, tell the
difference*. [H]

**[BrE/AmE] The `should` subjunctive.** After *suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose, request*,
British English commonly inserts `should` (*They insisted that the file **should be** reviewed*);
American English commonly uses the bare base form, called the mandative subjunctive (*They insisted
that the file **be** reviewed*). **Both are correct in both varieties** and both are safe in IELTS
Writing. What is not standard is the plain present (`*They insisted that the file is reviewed`). [M]

## 11. Irregular verbs — the traps

These are the ones that cost marks even at a high level, because they are pairs of *different verbs*
that look like forms of each other.

### 11.1 `lie` / `lay` — three verbs, not two

| Verb | Meaning | Base | Past | Past participle | Takes an object? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **lie** | be in a horizontal position; be situated | lie | **lay** | **lain** | **No** |
| **lay** | put something down | lay | **laid** | **laid** | **Yes** |
| **lie** | say something untrue | lie | **lied** | **lied** | No |

> The village **lies** below the ridge. — *lie* (situated), no object
> The village **lay** below the ridge. — past of *lie*
> The crew **laid** the cable along the verge. — *lay*, with an object
> He **lied** about the readings. — a completely different verb

**Why this one is uniquely confusing:** the past simple of *lie* is `lay`, which is also the base form
of a different verb. There is no trick that makes it easy; the only reliable test is **does it take an
object?** You *lay* something. You just *lie*. [H]

### 11.2 Pairs where one member is regular and one is not

| Irregular verb | Regular look-alike | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| hang / **hung** / hung | hang / **hanged** / hanged | *hung* = suspended. *hanged* = executed by hanging, and only that. |
| shine / **shone** / shone | shine / **shined** / shined | *shone* = gave out light. *shined* = polished. |
| find / **found** / found | found / **founded** / founded | *found* = establish (a company, a town) is regular. |
| wind / **wound** /waʊnd/ | wound / **wounded** /ˈwuːndɪd/ | *wind* = turn, coil. *wound* = injure, is regular and sounds different. |
| fall / **fell** / fallen | fell / **felled** / felled | *fell* = cut down a tree is regular. |
| lie / lay / lain | lay / laid / laid | see §11.1 |
| rise / **rose** / risen | raise / **raised** / raised | *rise* takes no object; *raise* takes one. Prices **rise**; a company **raises** prices. |
| sit / **sat** / sat | set / **set** / set | *sit* takes no object; *set* takes one. |

**`rise` vs `raise` is IELTS Task 1's most expensive pair**, because both appear constantly in trend
descriptions. `Sales rose sharply` (no object) vs `The company raised prices` (object). `*Sales raised
sharply` is wrong. [H]

|---|---|---|
| past participle of *get* | **got** | **gotten** |
| Example | She has **got** a reply. | She has **gotten** a reply. |
| Meaning of *have got* = possess | I've **got** a permit. | I've **got** a permit. — **`gotten` is not used here in either variety** |
| Meaning of *have got to* = must | I've **got to** leave. | I've **got to** leave. — again never *gotten* |

**The rule to give the learner:** `gotten` means *obtained* or *became*; `got` in `have got` means
*possess* or *must*, and it never becomes `gotten` even in American English. Both varieties are
accepted in IELTS. [M]

### 11.5 `born` and `borne`

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **born** | only in *be born* — coming into life | She was **born** in Brackenfield. |
| **borne** | every other sense of *bear*: carry, endure, produce | The costs were **borne** by the trust. / a water-**borne** infection |

### 11.6 `been` and `gone`

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **has been to** | went there and has come back | She **has been to** Sandmouth twice. |
| **has gone to** | went there and is still there | She **has gone to** Sandmouth — she'll be back on Friday. |

This is a genuine meaning difference, not a stylistic one, and it is one of the few places where
`been` is used as a past participle of `go`. [H]

## 12. Verb patterns — what follows a verb

### 12.1 The problem, stated honestly

When one verb follows another, the second verb takes one of four shapes, and **there is no rule that
predicts which**. It is a property of the first verb and it has to be listed. This is not a failure of
the reference; it is a fact about English, and saying so plainly stops the learner hunting for a
pattern that does not exist. [H]

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| **-ing form (gerund)** | They **avoided answering** the question. |
| **to-infinitive** | They **agreed to answer** the question. |
| **object + to-infinitive** | They **asked us to answer** the question. |
| **object + bare infinitive** | They **made us answer** the question. |

**One reliable sub-rule, and it is worth its weight:** after a **preposition**, the verb is always the
**-ing form**. Always, with no exceptions. *interested in **working***, *instead of **waiting***,
*good at **explaining***, *before **leaving***, *without **checking***. [H]

### 12.2 Verbs followed by the -ing form

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| admit | avoid | consider | delay |
| deny | detest | discuss | dislike |
| endure | enjoy | escape | excuse |
| face | fancy [BrE] | feel like | finish |
| forgive | imagine | involve | justify |
| keep (on) | mention | mind | miss |
| postpone | practise [BrE] / practice [AmE] | prevent | quit |
| recall | recollect | report | resent |
| resist | risk | suggest | tolerate |
| understand | anticipate | appreciate | dread |
| entail | contemplate | defer | resume |

**Phrasal and multi-word verbs in the same group:** *carry on, end up, give up, go on (continuing the
same thing), keep on, put off, take up, get through, look forward to, object to, be used to, get used
to, be worth, can't help, can't stand, can't bear, it's no use, it's no good, there's no point in,
have difficulty (in), spend time, waste time.*

> The trust **postponed replacing** the handrails.
> There is **no point in submitting** the form twice.
> We **spent three weeks checking** the archive.

### 12.3 Verbs followed by the to-infinitive

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| afford | agree | aim | appear |
| arrange | ask | attempt | beg |
| care | choose | claim | consent |
| decide | demand | deserve | expect |
| fail | happen | hesitate | hope |
| learn | long | manage | neglect |
| offer | plan | prepare | pretend |
| promise | prove | refuse | resolve |
| seem | strive | swear | tend |
| threaten | undertake | volunteer | vow |
| wait | want | wish | would like |

> The contractor **failed to notify** the office.
> The trust **agreed to fund** half the cost.
> She **appears to have misread** the figures. *(perfect infinitive — §6)*

### 12.4 Verbs followed by an object + to-infinitive

The object comes between the two verbs. This is the pattern that reported commands and requests use
(§9.6).

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| advise sb to | allow sb to | ask sb to | beg sb to |
| cause sb to | challenge sb to | command sb to | convince sb to |
| enable sb to | encourage sb to | expect sb to | forbid sb to |
| force sb to | get sb to | instruct sb to | invite sb to |
| order sb to | permit sb to | persuade sb to | remind sb to |
| request sb to | teach sb to | tell sb to | tempt sb to |
| urge sb to | want sb to | warn sb to | would like sb to |

**Watch `want` and `would like`:** English does **not** use a *that*-clause here. `*I want that you
come` is not English; it is `I want **you to come**`. This error is imported from many other
languages and it is worth its own line in the chapter. [H]

**`get` has two patterns with different meanings:** `get sb **to do** sth` = persuade them to do it;
`get sth **done**` = arrange for it to be done (§4.5). [H]

### 12.5 Verbs followed by an object + bare infinitive

Short and closed — see §6.1 for the full list. `make`, `let`, causative `have`, and the perception
verbs.

| Structure | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| make + object + V | The rules **make every visitor sign** in. | passive takes **to**: *every visitor **is made to sign*** |
| let + object + V | They **let us use** the north hall. | **no passive** — use *were allowed to use* |
| have + object + V | We **had the surveyor recheck** the readings. | causative |
| help + object + (to) V | She **helped us carry** the equipment. | *to* is optional and both are correct |
| see / hear / watch / feel / notice + object + V | I **heard the pump stop**. | the **complete** action |
| see / hear / watch / feel / notice + object + V-ing | I **heard the pump running**. | the action **in progress** |

### 12.6 Verbs that take either form with no real change of meaning

| Verb | Both patterns | Note |
|---|---|---|
| begin | begin to rain / begin raining | |
| start | start to rain / start raining | |
| continue | continue to rise / continue rising | |
| cease [FORMAL] | cease to operate / cease operating | |
| intend | intend to appeal / intend appealing | *to*-form is far commoner |
| propose | propose to appeal / propose appealing | |
| bother | didn't bother to reply / didn't bother replying | |
| can't bear | can't bear to watch / can't bear watching | |
| like, love, hate, prefer | like to walk / like walking | see the note below |

**Avoid two `-ing` forms in a row.** *It is **starting to rain*** is natural; *it is starting
raining* is not. When the first verb is already in a continuous form, use the to-infinitive. [H]

**[BrE/AmE] `like / love / hate / prefer`.** With these four, British English tends to use **-ing**
for general enjoyment (*I like walking*) and the **to-infinitive** for what you think is a good idea
or habit (*I like to check the figures twice*). American English uses the to-infinitive more freely
for both. Both are correct in both varieties, and the distinction is a tendency, not a rule. **After
`would`, however, only the to-infinitive is possible in both varieties:** *I'd like **to walk***,
never `*I'd like walking`. [M]

### 12.7 Verbs that take either form with a **real** change of meaning

This is the table the brief specifically asks for, and it is the highest-value half-page in §12. In
every row the two versions describe **different events**.

| Verb | + **-ing** | + **to-infinitive** |
|---|---|---|
| **stop** | stop **doing** = end the activity — *The crew **stopped painting** at four.* (they painted no more) | stop **to do** = interrupt something else in order to do this — *The crew **stopped to eat**.* (they stopped in order to eat) |
| **remember** | remember **doing** = a memory of a past action — *I **remember locking** the gate.* (I did it, and I can picture it) | remember **to do** = not forget a duty — *Please **remember to lock** the gate.* (the locking comes after the remembering) |
| **forget** | forget **doing** = have no memory of a past action; almost always negative — *I'll never **forget seeing** the wharf collapse.* | forget **to do** = fail to do something you should have — *He **forgot to lock** the gate.* |
| **regret** | regret **doing** = be sorry about something you did — *The trust **regrets appointing** him.* | regret **to say / to inform / to tell** = a formal, sympathetic way of delivering bad news **now** — *We **regret to inform** you that the application was unsuccessful.* [FORMAL] |
| **try** | try **doing** = experiment; see whether it solves the problem — *If the meter sticks, **try tapping** the casing.* | try **to do** = make an effort at something difficult — *We **tried to reach** the site before dark.* |
| **go on** | go on **doing** = continue doing the same thing — *She **went on speaking** for an hour.* | go on **to do** = finish one thing and move to the next — *She outlined the survey, then **went on to describe** the costs.* |
| **mean** | mean **doing** = involve, entail (the subject is usually a thing, not a person) — *Restoring the wharf would **mean closing** the road for a year.* | mean **to do** = intend — *I **meant to call** you yesterday.* |
| **need** | need **doing** = has a **passive** sense: something must be done to it — *The handrails **need repainting**.* | need **to do** = the subject has to do it — *We **need to repaint** the handrails.* |
| **want** | want **doing** = [BrE, informal] the same passive sense as *need doing* — *That gate **wants oiling**.* [SPOKEN] | want **to do** = wish to — *We **want to repaint** it.* |
| **come** | come **doing** = the manner of arriving — *He **came running** down the towpath.* | come **to do** = a gradual change of state — *I **came to see** why the design failed.* |
| **propose** | propose **doing** = suggest | propose **to do** = intend |
| **help** | can't help **doing** = be unable to stop yourself — *I **couldn't help noticing** the crack.* | help **(to) do** = assist — *She **helped (to) carry** it.* |
| **be afraid** | be afraid **of doing** = fear that something might happen to you — *She was afraid of **falling**.* | be afraid **to do** = too frightened to do it deliberately — *She was afraid **to climb** the ladder.* |
| **be sorry** | be sorry **for doing** = apologise for something you did | be sorry **to do / to hear** = regret on hearing news now |
| **used to** | **be used to doing** = accustomed to it (§5.5) | **used to do** = a past habit that has stopped |

**Authoring note.** Each of these rows is a genuine minimal pair and the theory chapter should render
them as two full sentences side by side, never as a formula. The formula `stop + -ing / stop + to` is
memorable and teaches nothing; the two sentences teach everything. [JUDGEMENT]

### 12.8 The `to` that is a preposition, not an infinitive marker

A small closed set of expressions ends in the **preposition** `to`, which means the verb after them
takes the **-ing** form, not the base form. This looks wrong to learners and is one of the most
reliable sources of error at every level.

| Expression | Correct | Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| look forward to | I look forward to **hearing** from you. | `*look forward to hear` |
| be / get used to | She is used to **working** outdoors. | `*used to work outdoors* (different meaning)` |
| object to | They objected to **paying** the surcharge. | `*objected to pay` |
| be opposed to | The trust is opposed to **selling** the site. | `*opposed to sell` |
| in addition to | In addition to **funding** the survey, they… | `*in addition to fund` |
| be committed to | We are committed to **reducing** waste. | `*committed to reduce` |
| be accustomed to [FORMAL] | accustomed to **working** long hours | |
| confess to | He confessed to **altering** the log. | |
| resort to | They resorted to **closing** the road. | |
| take to | She has taken to **cycling** to work. | |
| get round to [BrE] / get around to [AmE] | I never got round to **replying**. | |
| with a view to [FORMAL] | with a view to **extending** the lease | |
| when it comes to | when it comes to **funding**, … | |

**The test:** if you can put a **noun** after the `to` (*I look forward to the meeting*), then `to` is
a preposition and the verb must be `-ing`. If you cannot (*\*I want to the meeting*), it is an
infinitive marker and the verb is the base form. [H]

### 12.9 Verbs followed by a that-clause or a question word

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| verb + (that) + clause | say, think, believe, know, hope, expect, agree, admit, claim, decide, discover, explain, feel, find, notice, realise, suggest, suppose | She **realised (that)** the file was incomplete. |
| verb + object + (that) + clause | tell, remind, warn, inform, assure, persuade, convince | He **assured us (that)** the road was open. |
| verb + wh-word + clause | know, ask, wonder, decide, explain, forget, remember, understand, discuss, consider | Nobody **explained why** the span had been closed. |
| verb + wh-word + to-infinitive | know, ask, decide, learn, remember, forget, understand, explain, wonder *(only with `whether`)* | We had to **decide whether to appeal**. |

**`that` can be omitted** after most of these verbs in ordinary writing, and is usually kept in formal
academic writing for clarity. It cannot be omitted after *point out, argue, maintain* when the clause
is long or when omitting it makes the sentence ambiguous. [M]

### 12.10 Errors this section prevents

| Wrong | Right | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| `*She suggested me to apply.` | She suggested that I apply. / She suggested applying. | `suggest` never takes object + to-infinitive (§9.6) |
| `*I want that you check it.` | I want you to check it. | `want` takes object + to-infinitive |
| `*I look forward to hear from you.` | I look forward to hearing from you. | `to` is a preposition (§12.8) |
| `*He avoided to answer.` | He avoided answering. | `avoid` takes -ing |
| `*They decided going.` | They decided to go. | `decide` takes to-infinitive |
| `*She made me to sign.` | She made me sign. | `make` takes the bare infinitive |
| `*He was made sign.` | He was made to sign. | the passive of `make` restores the `to` |
| `*I enjoyed to visit Sandmouth.` | I enjoyed visiting Sandmouth. | `enjoy` takes -ing |
| `*She is interested to work there.` | She is interested in working there. | preposition + -ing |
| `*I'd like walking now.` | I'd like to walk now. | after `would`, only the to-infinitive |
| `*It's starting raining.` | It's starting to rain. | avoid two -ing forms in a row |

## 13. Comparatives and superlatives

### 13.1 The rule, by length of word

| Length of adjective | Comparative | Superlative | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One syllable** | + **-er** | **the** + **-est** | tall → taller → the tallest; cheap → cheaper → the cheapest |
| **Two syllables ending in `-y`** | `y` → `i` + **-er** | **the** + `i` + **-est** | early → earlier → the earliest; busy → busier → the busiest |
| **Two syllables, some others** | either | either | narrow → narrower / more narrow (§13.3) |
| **Two syllables, most** | **more** + | **the most** + | careful → more careful → the most careful |
| **Three or more syllables** | **more** + | **the most** + | expensive → more expensive → the most expensive |
| **Any adjective ending in `-ed`** | **more** + | **the most** + | tired → more tired; worried → more worried |

**Going the other way**, for any length: **less** + adjective, **the least** + adjective.
*less expensive*, *the least expensive*, *less useful*, *the least useful*. There is no `-er`
equivalent for downward comparison. [H]

### 13.2 The spelling changes

The same three changes that apply to `-ed` and `-ing` apply here (§14.4).

| Change | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| just add `-er` / `-est` | default | cheap → cheaper → cheapest |
| add `-r` / `-st` only | the adjective already ends in `-e` | large → larger → largest; safe → safer → safest |
| **double the final consonant** | one syllable, ending **consonant–vowel–consonant** | big → bigger → biggest; hot → hotter → hottest; thin → thinner → thinnest; sad → sadder → saddest |
| **no doubling** | the final letter is `w`, `x` or `y` | new → newer; low → lower; slow → slower |
| **no doubling** | there are two vowel letters before the final consonant | cheap → cheaper; clean → cleaner; loud → louder |
| **no doubling** | the word ends in two consonants | fast → faster; hard → harder; rich → richer |
| `y` → `i` | the word ends in a consonant + `y` | happy → happier; dry → drier *(also* dryer *)*; shy → shyer *(also* shier *)* |

### 13.3 Two-syllable adjectives that take both forms

For these, `-er/-est` and `more/most` are **both correct**. The `-er` form tends to sound slightly
more informal. This is a genuinely open area of English and the reference should present it as a
choice rather than inventing a rule.

| Group | Adjectives |
|---|---|
| ending in `-er` | clever, bitter, tender |
| ending in `-le` | simple, gentle, humble, noble, subtle |
| ending in `-ow` | narrow, shallow, hollow, mellow |
| ending in `-y` | happy, easy, busy, tidy, angry, friendly, likely, lively, lovely — *these strongly prefer* `-ier` |
| others | quiet, common, pleasant, polite, stupid, cruel, handsome, remote |

[M] — the membership of this list varies between reference works, which the chapter may say.

### 13.4 The irregular comparatives and superlatives

| Adjective / adverb | Comparative | Superlative | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **good** (adj) / **well** (adv) | **better** | **the best** | there is no `*gooder`, no `*more good` |
| **bad** (adj) / **badly** (adv) | **worse** | **the worst** | not `*worser`, not `*more bad` |
| **far** | **farther** / **further** | **the farthest** / **the furthest** | see below |
| **little** | **less** | **the least** | for uncountable amounts |
| **few** | **fewer** | **the fewest** | for countable things |
| **much / many** | **more** | **the most** | *much* for uncountable, *many* for countable, *more* for both |
| **old** | **older** / **elder** | **the oldest** / **the eldest** | see below |
| **late** | **later** | **the latest** / **the last** | *later* = in time; *the latest* = most recent; *the last* = final |
| **near** | **nearer** | **the nearest** / **the next** | *the nearest* = closest in space; *the next* = following in a sequence |

**`farther` / `further` [BrE/AmE] [M].** Both are used for physical distance in both varieties. Only
**`further`** is used for abstract or additional senses — *further information*, *further delay*,
*further to your email*. `*farther information` is not English. British English uses `further` for
physical distance too, and many British speakers never use `farther` at all; American English keeps
`farther` for distance more consistently. **Safe advice for a learner: use `further` for everything
except when you specifically mean physical distance, where either works.**

**`older` / `elder`.** `elder` and `eldest` are used **only about family members** and **only before a
noun**: *my elder sister*, *the eldest of three brothers*. You cannot say `*He is elder than me` —
that has to be `older`. `elder` is more British and slightly formal. [BrE/AmE] [M]

### 13.5 Adverbs

| Type | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Adverbs formed with `-ly` | **more / the most** | carefully → more carefully → the most carefully; quickly → more quickly |
| Adverbs with the **same form as the adjective** | **-er / -est** | fast → faster; hard → harder; late → later; long → longer; high → higher; deep → deeper; near → nearer; straight → straighter |
| `early` | `y` → `i` + `-er` | early → earlier → the earliest |
| `soon` | `-er` | soon → sooner → the soonest |
| Irregular | | well → better → best; badly → worse → worst; little → less → least; much → more → most; far → further/farther |

**[SPOKEN]** *quicker*, *slower*, *cheaper* and *louder* are used as adverbs in everyday speech
(*Can you drive quicker?*). This is perfectly normal spoken English, but **in IELTS Writing use
`more quickly`**, because the adverb form is the register-appropriate one. [M]

**`the` with superlative adverbs is optional:** *She finished (the) fastest.* Both are correct. [M]

### 13.6 The structures comparison is used in

| Structure | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **as** + adjective + **as** | equal | The coast road is **as fast as** the bypass. |
| **not as** + adjective + **as** | not equal | The ferry is **not as reliable as** the bridge. |
| **not so** + adjective + **as** | the same, slightly [FORMAL] and more [BrE] | The ferry is **not so reliable as** the bridge. |
| comparative + **than** | unequal | The bypass is **shorter than** the coast road. |
| **the same** (+ noun) **as** | identical | The new span is **the same length as** the old one. |
| **similar to** | close | The design is **similar to** the Marlow footbridge. |
| **different from / to / than** | unlike | see the note below |
| **twice / three times as** + adj + **as** | multiples | The new hall is **twice as large as** the old one. |
| **twice / three times the** + noun + **of** | multiples | It is **twice the size of** the old one. |
| **half as** + adj + **as** | fractions | It costs **half as much as** the alternative. |
| comparative + **and** + comparative | progressive change | The winters are getting **milder and milder**. |
| **more and more** + adj | progressive change | Access is becoming **more and more difficult**. |
| **the** + comparative, **the** + comparative | linked change | **The longer** the delay, **the higher** the cost. |
| **by far the** + superlative | emphasis | It is **by far the** cheapest option. |
| **one of the** + superlative + **plural noun** | membership | It is **one of the oldest bridges** in the county. |
| superlative + **in** + place / group | scope | the largest employer **in** the district |
| superlative + **of** + set or period | scope | the wettest **of** the four seasons |
| superlative + **(that) … have ever** | experience | the coldest winter **I have ever** recorded |
| **no** + comparative + **than** | emphatic equality | The repair was **no cheaper than** a replacement. |
| **nowhere near as** + adj + **as** | strong inequality | It is **nowhere near as** efficient. |

**[BrE/AmE] `different from / to / than`.** `different **from**` is standard and safe everywhere.
`different **to**` is common and accepted in British English. `different **than**` is common in
American English, especially before a clause (*different than I expected*). **Teach `different from`
as the default**, note the other two, and mark none of them as an error. [M]

**`fewer` and `less` [M].** The traditional rule is `fewer` with countable nouns (*fewer buildings*)
and `less` with uncountable nouns (*less traffic*). In speech, `less` is used with both by most
speakers, and this is not a recent development. **For IELTS Writing, follow the traditional rule** —
it is the one a formal reader expects, and `fewer` is never wrong where the noun is countable.
Note the fixed exceptions where `less` is standard even with countables: *less than five miles*,
*less than £200*, *less than three weeks* — because these are amounts, not counts.

**`than I` or `than me` [M].** *She is taller than **I** am* is safe and natural. *She is taller than
**me*** is standard informal English and used by everyone. *She is taller than **I*** (with nothing
after it) is the prescriptive form and now sounds stiff. **Recommendation: write `than I am`, which
is both correct and unremarkable.**

### 13.7 Words that can go in front of a comparative

Comparatives take their own set of intensifiers, and the everyday intensifier `very` is **not one of
them**.

| Strength | Word | Example |
|---|---|---|
| very strong | **much**, **far**, **a lot** [SPOKEN], **a great deal**, **considerably**, **significantly**, **substantially** | The second route is **far shorter**. |
| moderate | **rather**, **somewhat**, **appreciably** | The figures are **somewhat higher** than expected. |
| weak | **slightly**, **a little**, **a bit** [SPOKEN], **marginally** | The tide was **marginally lower**. |
| equality | **no**, **not any** | It was **no cheaper**. |

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| `*very bigger` | much bigger / far bigger |
| `*very more expensive` | much more expensive |
| `*more bigger` | bigger |
| `*more easier` | easier |
| `*the most cheapest` | the cheapest |
| `*He is the taller of the three.` | He is the tallest of the three. |

`very much better` **is** correct — `very` there modifies `much`, not the comparative. [M]

### 13.8 Superlative details worth stating

- **`the` is normally required** before a superlative when a noun follows: *the highest tower*. It is
  dropped after a possessive: *her best result*, *our largest site*. [H]
- **`one of the` + superlative takes a plural noun**: *one of the largest **ports** in Europe*, never
  `*one of the largest port`. [H]
- **Ordinal + superlative:** *the second largest*, *the third busiest*. [H]
- **Comparative for two, superlative for three or more** is a traditional rule that is worth teaching
  because formal readers still apply it: *the better of the two options*, *the best of the four*. In
  ordinary usage the superlative is often used for two, and that is not an error a learner should be
  penalised for. [M]
- **Absolute adjectives.** Some adjectives describe an all-or-nothing property — *unique, perfect,
  complete, impossible, dead, empty, equal* — and prescriptive style guides say they cannot be
  compared (`*more unique*`). Modern usage does compare several of them, especially *unique*, and no
  reader is misled. **Recommendation: avoid `more unique` in IELTS Writing** because some readers
  regard it as an error, and use *almost unique*, *virtually unique* or *rarer* instead. [M]

## 14. Spelling rules for endings

The three endings below are the ones that change most often, and each has a small closed set of
rules. Every rule here has been stated as *rule + examples + exceptions*, because a rule without its
exceptions produces confident errors.

### 14.1 Adding `-s` — third person singular verbs, and plural nouns

| # | Rule | Examples | Exceptions / notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Default: add **`-s`** | paints, writes, works, opens | |
| 2 | After a **hissing sound** — words ending `-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -z` — add **`-es`** | misses, washes, watches, mixes, buzzes, passes | the `-es` adds a **syllable** |
| 3 | **Consonant + `y`** → `y` becomes `i`, add **`-es`** | study → studies; carry → carries; try → tries; apply → applies | |
| 4 | **Vowel + `y`** → just add **`-s`** | play → plays; buy → buys; enjoy → enjoys; stay → stays | never `*plaies` |
| 5 | Verbs ending in **`-o`** → add **`-es`** | go → goes; do → does; echo → echoes; veto → vetoes | |
| 6 | Irregular verbs | have → **has**; be → **is**; do → **does** /dʌz/; say → **says** /sez/ | |

**Nouns ending in `-o` are genuinely unpredictable** and have to be learnt:

| Take `-es` | Take `-s` | Both accepted |
|---|---|---|
| potatoes, tomatoes, heroes, echoes, vetoes, torpedoes | photos, pianos, kilos, radios, studios, zoos, memos, logos, solos, avocados | volcanoes / volcanos; mosquitoes / mosquitos; tornadoes / tornados |

**Other plural noun rules worth having in the same chapter:**

| Rule | Examples | Exceptions |
|---|---|---|
| `-f` / `-fe` → `-ves` | leaf → leaves; knife → knives; wife → wives; half → halves; shelf → shelves; thief → thieves; wolf → wolves; life → lives | roof → roofs; chief → chiefs; belief → beliefs; cliff → cliffs; proof → proofs. Both: scarf → scarves/scarfs; hoof → hooves/hoofs; dwarf → dwarfs/dwarves |
| Irregular plurals | man → men; woman → women; child → children; person → people; foot → feet; tooth → teeth; goose → geese; mouse → mice; louse → lice; ox → oxen | |
| No change | sheep, deer, fish, aircraft, series, species, means, salmon, offspring, headquarters | *fishes* exists, meaning *species of fish* |
| Latin and Greek plurals — **important for academic writing** | analysis → analyses; basis → bases; crisis → crises; hypothesis → hypotheses; thesis → theses; criterion → **criteria**; phenomenon → **phenomena**; datum → **data**; medium → media; curriculum → curricula; bacterium → bacteria; nucleus → nuclei; stimulus → stimuli; radius → radii | Both accepted: index → indices/indexes; appendix → appendices/appendixes; formula → formulae/formulas; syllabus → syllabi/syllabuses; matrix → matrices/matrixes |

**`criteria` and `phenomena` are plural.** `*a criteria` and `*this phenomena` are errors; the
singulars are *criterion* and *phenomenon*. This is a high-frequency error in IELTS Writing. [H]

**`data`.** Formal academic writing treats it as plural (*these data show*); general usage, including
most journalism and much academic writing now, treats it as an uncountable singular (*this data
shows*). **Both are defensible; be consistent within one piece of writing.** [M]

### 14.2 Adding `-ed` — regular past simple and past participle

| # | Rule | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Default: add **`-ed`** | paint → painted; work → worked; open → opened | |
| 2 | Word already ends in **`-e`** → add **`-d`** only | close → closed; agree → agreed; live → lived | |
| 3 | **Consonant + `y`** → `y` becomes `i`, add **`-ed`** | study → studied; carry → carried; apply → applied | |
| 4 | **Vowel + `y`** → just add **`-ed`** | play → played; enjoy → enjoyed; destroy → destroyed | irregular exceptions: pay → **paid**; lay → **laid**; say → **said** |
| 5 | **One syllable, consonant–vowel–consonant** → **double** the final consonant | stop → stopped; plan → planned; rob → robbed; drop → dropped; beg → begged | |
| 6 | **No doubling** if the final letter is `w`, `x` or `y` | snow → snowed; fix → fixed; play → played | |
| 7 | **No doubling** if there are two vowel letters | rain → rained; seem → seemed; wait → waited | |
| 8 | **No doubling** if the word ends in two consonants | help → helped; work → worked; want → wanted | |
| 9 | **Two or more syllables:** double **only if the last syllable is stressed** | preFER → preferred; adMIT → admitted; ocCUR → occurred; comMIT → committed; reGRET → regretted; conTROL → controlled; eQUIP → equipped | VIsit → visited; OFfer → offered; HAPpen → happened; LIsten → listened; TARget → targeted; LImit → limited; deVELop → developed |

**Rule 10 — the British/American exception, and it is a big one. [BrE/AmE]**

A word ending in a single `-l` after a single vowel:

| | British English | American English |
|---|---|---|
| travel | travel**l**ed, travel**l**ing, travel**l**er | traveled, traveling, traveler |
| cancel | cancelled, cancelling | canceled, canceling |
| label | labelled, labelling | labeled, labeling |
| model | modelled, modelling | modeled, modeling |
| signal | signalled, signalling | signaled, signaling |
| quarrel | quarrelled, quarrelling | quarreled, quarreling |
| fuel | fuelled, fuelling | fueled, fueling |
| level | levelled, levelling | leveled, leveling |
| counsel | counselled, counselling | counseled, counseling |
| total | totalled, totalling | totaled, totaling |

**British English doubles the `-l` whether or not the last syllable is stressed. American English
follows the ordinary stress rule (rule 9), so it does not double when the stress is earlier.**

**Both varieties double when the stress *is* on the last syllable:** conTROL → controlled;
comPEL → compelled; exPEL → expelled; proPEL → propelled; reBEL → rebelled; paTROL → patrolled.

**Both varieties are accepted in IELTS. Pick one and be consistent.** [H]

**A few words that do not follow the stress rule cleanly** and should be checked rather than derived:
*format → formatted*; *combat → combated / combatted* (both accepted); *worship → worshipped* [BrE] /
*worshiped or worshipped* [AmE]; *kidnap → kidnapped* (standard in both now); *focus → focused*
(standard in both; *focussed* is an accepted British variant); *benefit → benefited* (standard in
both; *benefitted* is a variant). [M]

Identical to the `-ed` rules: drop a final `-e` (large → larger), change consonant + `y` to `i`

### 14.5 How the endings are pronounced

Spelling and sound diverge here, and learners who pronounce every `-ed` as a syllable are immediately
audible in the Speaking test.

**`-s` endings — three sounds:**

| Sound | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /s/ | after a voiceless sound: /p, t, k, f, θ/ | stops, paints, works, laughs |
| /z/ | after a voiced sound (most consonants and all vowels) | runs, opens, plays, rides |
| /ɪz/ — **an extra syllable** | after a hissing sound: /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/ | misses, washes, watches, judges, changes |

**`-ed` endings — three sounds:**

| Sound | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| /t/ | after a voiceless sound (but not /t/) | worked, stopped, washed, laughed, missed |
| /d/ | after a voiced sound (but not /d/) | played, opened, cleaned, rained, closed |
| /ɪd/ — **an extra syllable** | only after **/t/** or **/d/** | painted, waited, needed, decided, ended |

**The single most useful line here:** `-ed` is a separate syllable **only** after *t* or *d*.
*Worked* is one syllable, not two. *Painted* is two. [H]

## 15. Dependent prepositions

### 15.1 What a dependent preposition is, and why it has to be listed

Some words are simply followed by a fixed preposition. *Depend* is followed by *on*. *Interested* is
followed by *in*. *A reason* is followed by *for*. The preposition adds no meaning of its own — it is
part of the word — and **there is no rule that predicts it**. Different languages choose differently,
which is why this is a lifelong source of error even for advanced learners.

Two consequences for the reference:

1. **These belong in the vocabulary entry, not in a grammar rule.** The right way to learn *depend on*
   is as a single item, the way you learn a word.
2. **Remember §12.1's sub-rule:** after any of these prepositions, a following verb takes the **-ing**
   form. *interested in **working***, *insisted on **paying***, *the reason for **closing** the span*.

### 15.6 The Task 1 preposition set — `increase`, `rise`, `fall`, `decrease`

These four nouns take **`in`** before the thing that changed and **`of`** before the amount it changed
by. Getting them the wrong way round is one of the most visible errors in an IELTS Writing Task 1
answer, and it happens in nearly every unedited draft.

| Correct | Meaning |
|---|---|
| an increase **in** rainfall | *rainfall* is the thing that increased |
| an increase **of** 12% | *12%* is the size of the increase |
| a sharp rise **in** demand | |
| a rise **of** 4,000 units | |
| a fall **in** attendance | |
| a fall **of** almost a third | |
| a decrease **in** journey times | |
| a decrease **of** nine minutes | |

Both can appear together: *an increase **in** rainfall **of** 12%*. [H]

Related fixed prepositions from the same task type: *rise **from** X **to** Y*, *fluctuate **between**
X **and** Y*, *peak **at** X*, *level off **at** X*, *account **for** X%*, *compared **with / to**
X*, *by contrast **with** X*, *at a rate **of** X*.

### 15.7 Prepositions of time and place where British and American usage differ [BrE/AmE]

| Meaning | British | American |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday and Sunday | **at** the weekend | **on** the weekend |
| membership of a sports side | **in** a team | **on** a team |
| a range of days | Monday **to** Friday | Monday **through** Friday |
| receiving treatment | **in** hospital | **in** the hospital |
| studying for a degree | **at** university | **in** college |
| location on a road | **in** the street | **on** the street |
| a form | fill **in** a form | fill **out** a form |
| serving a group | cater **for** an event | cater **to** a group |
| speaking to someone about business | meet someone | meet **with** someone |
| a period of days | **for** the next few days | same |
| a house number | live **at** 14 Norland Row | same |

Both columns are correct English. IELTS accepts either; consistency within one answer is what a
reader notices. [M]

### 17.1 Where these facts come from, stated honestly

**No external source was fetched while writing this file, and no source is cited anywhere in it.**
Everything above is either standard descriptive grammar of English that any reference work states in
substantially the same terms, or an explicitly-marked judgement of mine. Citing a source I did not
open would violate the project's fourth hard rule, and inventing one would be worse than citing none.

What that means for the verify agent: **treat this file as a well-informed draft, not as an
authority.** The high-value verification targets, in order, are:

| Priority | What to check | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Every row of the irregular verb tables (§10) | 188 rows; a single wrong participle will be memorised by thousands of learners |
| 2 | The `needn't have` vs `didn't need to` distinction (§5.3) | genuinely subtle and stated confidently here |

Anything marked **[M]** is a place where I have deliberately declined to state a crisp rule because

picks a side.
