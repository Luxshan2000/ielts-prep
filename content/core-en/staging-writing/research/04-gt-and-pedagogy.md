## 0.1 Confidence marking — read this before you trust a number

Much of what is written online about GT letters is coaching folklore repeated between SEO sites.
Every substantive claim below is tagged:

- **[OFFICIAL]** — stated by ielts.org / British Council / IDP.
- **[RESEARCH]** — from a peer-reviewed study or meta-analysis, cited.
- **[CONSENSUS]** — agreed by many experienced teachers, no official confirmation. Safe to teach
  as *advice*, never as "the rule".
- **[FOLKLORE — DO NOT SHIP]** — widely repeated, unverifiable, and we must not assert it.

Three claims I found repeated everywhere and am explicitly refusing to ship:
"missing one bullet caps Task Achievement at band 5"; "examiners were retrained in 2025 to cap
memorised templates at band 4"; "register mistakes cost exactly one band". No official source
supports any of them. **[FOLKLORE — DO NOT SHIP].** The underlying direction of travel is real and
we teach that instead: an uncovered bullet is an uncovered task requirement, and unaddressed
requirements are what Task Achievement measures.

## 0.2 One-line thesis

For **letters**, the marks are lost in three predictable, teachable places — a bullet that is
mentioned but not extended, a register that drifts, and a sign-off that contradicts the greeting —
and all three are *checkable before the LLM is ever called*. For **writing pedagogy**, the
evidence says the same thing the speaking briefing said: **narrow the feedback, force the learner
to find the gap themselves, and attach a retry to every fix.** Everything below is downstream of
those two sentences.

# PART A — GENERAL TRAINING TASK 1: LETTERS

## 1. The task, as a set of facts

### 1.1 Format facts

| Fact | Detail | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| What the candidate is given | A short situation (2–4 sentences) plus **three bullet points** stating what the letter must do | [OFFICIAL] |
| Who it is addressed to | A named or generic individual, or an organisation | [OFFICIAL] |
| Minimum length | **150 words**; under-length is penalised | [OFFICIAL] |
| Suggested time | **about 20 minutes** | [OFFICIAL] |
| Weight | Task 1 is **one third** of the Writing band; Task 2 is two thirds | [OFFICIAL] |
| Form required | Continuous prose in full sentences. Notes and bullet points in the answer are not acceptable | [OFFICIAL] |
| Addresses | Not required; the instruction to begin with a specified greeting is normally given | [OFFICIAL] |
| Assessment | Four equally weighted criteria; task score is their average | [OFFICIAL] |
| Practical target length | **170–190 words** — comfortably clear of the floor without eating Task 2 time | [CONSENSUS] |

Sources: [IELTS GT Writing test format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-general-training-test/ielts-general-training-format-writing),
[IELTS Writing key assessment criteria](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf),
[IDP: how to write a letter](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-general-training-writing-task-1-write-a-letter).

**Author's note for prompt writing.** A prompt is a *situation*, not a topic. The situation must
supply enough concrete circumstance that a learner can invent specifics without inventing the
whole world: a timeframe, a relationship, and something that has gone wrong or needs doing. If a
learner has to ask "who am I to this person?", the prompt is under-specified and the register
becomes unmarkable.

### 1.2 The three bullets ARE the task specification

This is the single most important structural fact about the task and it is under-taught.

The bullets are not prompts for ideas. They are the **task requirements**, and Task Achievement is
defined as how fully, appropriately and relevantly the response fulfils the requirements set out in
the task **[OFFICIAL]**. So:

- Three bullets → three **functions** the letter must perform. A letter that performs two of them
  has not done the task, no matter how well written.
- Each bullet must be **covered and extended**. "Extended" means at least one further move beyond
  the bare statement: a detail, a consequence, a date, a number, a reason, a named preference. A
  sentence that merely restates the bullet in other words is *coverage without extension* and is
  the most common band-6 ceiling in this task. **[CONSENSUS, aligned with the official wording
  "fully… fulfils the requirements"]**
- Bullets are usually **balanced**: roughly equal treatment. A letter that spends 100 words on
  bullet 1 and 15 on bullet 3 is unbalanced even if all three are technically present.
- The **default architecture** is one paragraph per bullet, plus an opening purpose sentence and a
  closing move. That is 5 short paragraphs, not 3. **[CONSENSUS]**
- The bullets are usually, but not always, in the order the letter should follow. Where the third
  bullet is "say what you would like them to do", it *must* come last — it is the letter's payload.

**The extension test we should teach and can partly automate:** for each bullet, can the learner
point at (a) the sentence that does it and (b) a *different* sentence that adds something the
bullet did not say? If (b) is missing, the bullet is half-done.

### 1.3 The 20-minute budget

A defensible time plan for a letter **[CONSENSUS]**, worth shipping as content:

| Phase | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Read + decide | 2 | Who is the recipient? → register. What are the three functions? |
| Plan | 3 | One line of notes per bullet: the *specific* content, not the topic. Decide the greeting. |
| Write | 13 | Opening move → B1 → B2 → B3 → closing move → sign-off. |
| Check | 2 | The four-point check (§1.6). |

Note the asymmetry with Task 2 (§9.2): letters need *less* planning time and *more* checking time,
because the content is prescribed by the bullets and the losses are at surface level.

### 1.4 What Task Achievement rewards in a letter — clean-room paraphrase

The official criterion summary says Task Achievement assesses how fully, appropriately, accurately
and relevantly the response fulfils the task requirements in at least 150 words, and for Task 1
concerns how well the letter achieves its purpose **[OFFICIAL]**. Rendered as four sub-jobs an
author or evaluator can actually check:

1. **Coverage** — all three required functions are performed.
2. **Extension** — each is developed beyond a restatement, with relevant, plausible specifics.
3. **Format** — it is recognisably a letter: an opening that states the purpose or relationship, a
   body organised by function, a closing move, an appropriate greeting and sign-off.
4. **Tone** — a register appropriate to the recipient and purpose, **held consistently** from the
   greeting to the sign-off.

Point 4 living inside Task Achievement is the fact that makes register a *task* issue and not just
a style issue. Inappropriate tone is not a stylistic blemish; it is a failure to achieve the
letter's purpose. (Register also leaks into Lexical Resource, since criterion 3 assesses whether
vocabulary is used *appropriately* as well as accurately **[OFFICIAL]**.) This is the single
strongest justification for building a register checker (§W2).

The other three criteria behave in a letter as follows, in our own words:

- **Coherence and Cohesion** — logical order (usually: purpose → bullet order → close),
  paragraphing that maps to function, and linking that is present without being mechanical. In
  letters the commonest CC fault is *no paragraphing at all* — a 170-word wall.
- **Lexical Resource** — range and precision, and appropriacy to the register. The register-bearing
  vocabulary is the load-bearing part here: `inform / request / apologise` vs `tell / ask / say
  sorry`.
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — range and accuracy of structures. Letters naturally pull
  four structures worth teaching explicitly: polite modal requests (`would you be able to…`),
  present perfect for a running problem (`the heating has broken down twice`), conditionals for
  proposals and consequences (`if the repair could be done on a weekday…`), and reported speech for
  recounting prior contact (`I was told that an engineer would call`).

### 1.5 Under-length, copied language, and other mechanical losses

- Under 150 words is penalised under Task Achievement **[OFFICIAL]**; the mechanism is simply that
  a short answer cannot fully address the requirements.
- Language lifted verbatim from the prompt is not evidence of the candidate's own English. Our
  pre-check already flags a ≥20-word copied run (`05-writing-module.md §5`); for letters the
  temptation is to copy the situation paragraph into the opening, which is exactly the wrong place
  to spend 40 words. **[CONSENSUS]**
- Memorised, prefabricated openings and closings are the letter-shaped version of the memorised
  essay problem. The safe teaching line, and the one official sources actually support: a clear,
  natural, accurate letter beats one padded with memorised formality **[OFFICIAL — IDP]**. We do
  **not** assert a numeric cap.

### 1.6 The two-minute check protocol (shippable content)

Four questions, in this order, because they are ordered by cost-of-being-wrong:

1. **Sign-off matches greeting?** (§2.2 table.) Fastest fix, most avoidable loss.
2. **All three bullets — can I point at the sentence, and at the extension?**
3. **Any contraction / exclamation mark / slang in a formal letter, or any stiff Latinate
   formula in an informal one?** (§2.3 signal list.)
4. **Word count over 150 — and is there a paragraph break per function?**

## 2. Register: the three settings and how each is signalled

### 2.1 The recipient test — a decision procedure, not a vibe

Teach register as a *derivation from the recipient*, never as a feeling. The procedure:

```
Do I know this person's name?
├─ No  →  Do I know them personally?  (No, by definition)
│         → FORMAL, name unknown.  "Dear Sir or Madam," … "Yours faithfully,"
└─ Yes →  Is the relationship personal (friend, family, close colleague)?
          ├─ Yes → INFORMAL.  "Dear Nadia," / "Hi Tom," … "Best wishes,"
          └─ No  → Is it institutional/transactional (manager, official, company)?
                   ├─ Yes → FORMAL, name known.  "Dear Ms Adeyemi," … "Yours sincerely,"
                   └─ No, it's a known-but-not-close individual
                      (landlord, neighbour, tutor, a colleague you like)
                        → SEMI-FORMAL.  "Dear Mr Alvarez," … "Kind regards,"
```

**Important honesty note for the design agent.** "Semi-formal" is not an official IELTS category —
the public materials talk about personal, semi-formal and formal *styles* of writing, and IELTS's
own guidance has an article on semi-formal letters, so the label is legitimate and useful, but
nobody is marking "semi-formal" off a checklist **[OFFICIAL that the style exists; CONSENSUS on
where its boundaries are]**. What is being marked is *consistency and appropriacy*. That means:

- A letter that reads consistently formal when semi-formal was expected will rarely be penalised
  hard; it just reads slightly stiff.
- A letter that mixes registers is penalised, because the inconsistency itself is the fault.
- Therefore our teaching line is: **"pick the setting the recipient implies, and never move off
  it."** Not: "identify the one correct label."

Official guidance on the semi-formal case: use it for someone you know professionally, or for
something awkward with someone you know (apologising to a neighbour); keep openings and closings
"neither too formal nor too informal"; some contractions are fine; prefer `receive` over `get`;
use `suggest` / `recommend` for proposals; avoid both `it would be greatly appreciated if…` and
slang like `moan`; be consistent **[OFFICIAL —
[ielts.org semi-formal letters](https://ielts.org/news-and-insights/how-to-write-a-semi-formal-letter-for-ielts-general-training-writing-task-1)]**.

### 2.2 Greeting ↔ sign-off pairings — the canonical table

This is the highest-value single table in Part A. It is a fact, it is memorisable in 60 seconds,
and getting it wrong is a pure, unforced loss.

| Register | Greeting | Correct sign-off | Signature line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal, **name unknown** | `Dear Sir or Madam,` | **`Yours faithfully,`** | Full name (`Ravi Menon`) |
| Formal, **name known** | `Dear Mr Osei,` / `Dear Ms Bianchi,` / `Dear Dr Lam,` | **`Yours sincerely,`** | Full name |
| Semi-formal | `Dear Mr Alvarez,` / `Dear Mrs Cole,` (surname) | `Kind regards,` / `Best regards,` / `Yours sincerely,` | Full name, or first name if genuinely known |
| Informal | `Dear Nadia,` / `Hi Tom,` / `Hello Priya,` | `Best wishes,` / `All the best,` / `Take care,` / `Speak soon,` / `Love,` (family/very close only) | First name |

The mnemonic that actually sticks, in our own words: **the sign-off answers the greeting.** If you
did not name them, you cannot be sincere to them — you can only be faithful. If you named them,
you can be sincere.

**Wrong pairings to name explicitly as errors** (each is a real, frequent, avoidable loss):

| Wrong | Why it's wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `Dear Sir or Madam,` … `Yours sincerely,` | The classic. Sincerity requires a name. | `Yours faithfully,` |
| `Dear Ms Bianchi,` … `Yours faithfully,` | Inverse of the classic. | `Yours sincerely,` |
| `Dear Sir or Madam,` … `Best wishes,` | Register break at the last line. | `Yours faithfully,` |
| `Hi Tom,` … `Yours faithfully,` | Two registers in one letter. | `Best wishes,` |
| `Dear Sir,` (recipient's gender unknown) | Assumes a man. | `Dear Sir or Madam,` |
| `To whom it may concern,` | Reads as a circular, not a letter to a person. | `Dear Sir or Madam,` |
| `Respected Sir,` / `Dear Respected Madam,` | A South-Asian-English convention, not standard BrE/AmE letter usage. | `Dear Sir or Madam,` |
| `Yours truly,` / `Sincerely yours,` | American variants; harmless but off-register in a BrE-normed exam. | `Yours sincerely,` |
| `Dear Mr Ravi,` (first name after a title) | Title takes the surname. | `Dear Mr Menon,` or `Dear Ravi,` |
| `Yours Faithfully,` | Only the first word is capitalised. | `Yours faithfully,` |
| `Dear friend,` / `Dear my friend,` | Neither a name nor a convention. | `Dear Sam,` |
| Sign-off with no name at all | The letter is unsigned. | Add the name on its own line. |
| Any sign-off with **no comma**, in a letter that commas everywhere else | Punctuation inconsistency reads as carelessness. | Be consistent: comma after both, or after neither. |

**Micro-facts worth teaching:** BrE `Mr` / `Mrs` / `Ms` / `Dr` take no full stop; AmE `Mr.` does —
either is acceptable, but not both in one letter. `Dear Sir or Madam` is preferred to `Dear
Sir/Madam` in prose. Where the prompt supplies the greeting, **use the one supplied** — rewriting
it is a needless risk.

### 2.3 Register signals beyond the sign-off

Register is carried by ten things, not one. This list is directly convertible into a
teaching payload and into a deterministic checker (§W2).

| Signal | Formal | Semi-formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contractions** | none (`I am writing`) | occasional (`I'm writing`) | free (`I'm`, `don't`, `it's`) |
| **Verb stock** | Latinate: `request`, `inform`, `apologise`, `require`, `receive`, `resolve` | mixed | phrasal: `let you know`, `sort out`, `get`, `put up with`, `drop by` |
| **Modality / distance** | `I would be grateful if you could…`, `I should be obliged if…` | `Would you mind…`, `Could you possibly…` | `Can you…`, `Any chance you could…` |
| **Opening move** | states purpose in sentence 1: `I am writing to…` | states purpose but warmer | states relationship/news first: `It's been ages —` |
| **Sentence length** | longer, subordinated | medium | short, coordinated |
| **Hedging of complaint** | `the service has fallen short of what was advertised` | `it hasn't really worked out` | `it was a bit of a disaster` |
| **Exclamation marks** | never | rarely | fine, sparingly |
| **Questions to reader** | rhetorical or none | occasional | frequent (`How's the new job?`) |
| **Naming the reader mid-letter** | avoid | occasional | natural (`Honestly, Sam,`) |
| **Closing move** | `I look forward to your response.` | `Thanks in advance — do let me know.` | `Write soon!` / `Can't wait to see you.` |

**Register-carrying pairs worth shipping as drill items** (authored here, not lifted):

| Formal | Informal |
|---|---|
| `I am writing to enquire about…` | `I wanted to ask about…` |
| `I would be grateful if you could…` | `Could you…?` |
| `Please accept my apologies for…` | `I'm really sorry about…` |
| `Unfortunately, the situation has not improved.` | `Sadly, it hasn't got any better.` |
| `I would appreciate a response by 12 March.` | `Let me know by the 12th if you can.` |
| `I am unable to attend.` | `I can't make it.` |
| `Should you require further details…` | `If you need anything else…` |
| `I have enclosed a copy of the receipt.` | `I've stuck the receipt in with this.` |
| `Thank you for your assistance in this matter.` | `Thanks so much for sorting this out.` |
| `I wish to draw your attention to…` | `I wanted to flag something.` |

### 2.4 Register **drift** — where it actually happens

Drift is more common than outright wrong register, and it has a predictable geography. Data point
from teaching practice rather than research **[CONSENSUS]**, but it is consistent enough to encode:

1. **Bullet 3 in a formal letter.** The learner has held formality through the description, then
   the request arrives and the guard drops: `So please fix it soon!` after 120 words of careful
   prose.
2. **The closing sentence.** Formality is expensive to maintain; the last line is where fatigue
   lands: `Yours faithfully` preceded by `Thanks a lot!!`.
3. **The first line of an informal letter.** Learners trained on formal templates open a letter to
   a friend with `I am writing to inform you that…`, then relax into informality by paragraph two.
4. **Emotional peaks in a complaint.** Anger produces informality: `This is totally unacceptable
   and I am very angry.` Formal complaint register is *cold*, not hot.

**The teachable rule:** register is set once, at the greeting, and every later sentence must be
answerable to it. The check is mechanical — reread only the first and last sentence of each
paragraph; drift almost always shows there.

### 2.5 Consistency beats correctness

If a learner genuinely cannot decide between formal and semi-formal (a tutor, a landlord you've
met twice), the safe move is **the more formal of the two, held consistently**. Over-formality with
a known individual is a mild stylistic mismatch; inconsistency is a Task Achievement fault. This is
a rule of thumb, not an official position **[CONSENSUS]**.

## 3. Letter purposes: the catalogue

### 3.1 Purposes are made of MOVES

A *purpose* is not a topic — it is a recognisable sequence of communicative **moves**, each of
which needs its own functional language. This is the move-analysis tradition from genre-based
writing pedagogy, and it is exactly the right grain for a teaching payload: it gives the learner a
plan that is specific enough to follow and general enough to transfer.

Notation used below:
- **Moves** — the ordered functions a full answer performs.
- **Language** — 2–4 slotted frames per purpose. Frames carry `___` gaps deliberately (see §8.3:
  a frame without a gap is a script).
- **Bullet triad** — the shape the three prompt bullets typically take for this purpose. Useful to
  prompt authors.
- **Watch** — the failure this purpose specifically provokes.

All frames below are authored for this document.

### 3.2 The catalogue — 21 purposes

---

**P1 · COMPLAINT** — register: formal (organisation) or semi-formal (landlord, neighbour)

- **Moves:** state purpose + identify the transaction (what, when, reference) → describe the
  problem factually and specifically → state the *impact* on you → say what you have already tried
  → state the remedy you want → set a reasonable timeframe → close with an expectation of reply.
- **Language:** `I am writing to complain about ___, which I ___ on ___.` ·
  `The main difficulty is that ___.` · `As a result, I have had to ___.` ·
  `I would therefore ask you to ___ by ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** describe the problem · explain how it has affected you · say what you want done.
- **Watch:** heat. Formal complaint register is *cold and evidenced*. Also: learners describe the
  problem for 120 words and give the remedy 8 words — invert that weighting.
- Source direction for the move sequence: standard business-correspondence structure
  (purpose → background → problem → impact → remedy → deadline), see
  [British Council LearnEnglish](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/upper-intermediate-b2/a-letter-of-complaint),
  [IDP complaint-letter advice](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-general-training-advice-writing-complaint-letter).

---

**P2 · REQUEST FOR INFORMATION (enquiry)** — register: formal

- **Moves:** state purpose and where you heard of them → give your relevant situation in one line →
  ask specific questions (2–3, itemised in prose) → say why you need them by a certain point →
  thank + expectation of reply.
- **Language:** `I am writing to enquire about ___.` · `I would like to know whether ___.` ·
  `Could you also confirm ___?` · `I would be grateful for a reply before ___, as ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** explain who you are and why you are writing · say what you want to know ·
  explain when/why you need the information.
- **Watch:** vague questions. `Please send me all details` is one question pretending to be three.
  Ask for named things: price, dates, whether X is included.

---

**P3 · REQUEST FOR ACTION / A FAVOUR** — register: any

- **Moves:** state the request early → give the reason → minimise the imposition → offer something
  in return or acknowledge the trouble → make it easy to say yes (offer options) → thank.
- **Language:** `I was wondering whether you might be able to ___.` ·
  `The reason I ask is that ___.` · `I realise this is short notice, so ___.` ·
  `Whichever suits you better — ___ or ___ — is fine by me.`
- **Bullet triad:** explain the situation · say exactly what you are asking for · say how it would
  help / what you will do in return.
- **Watch:** the request is buried in paragraph three. In English request letters the ask comes
  early and is repeated at the close.

---

**P4 · APOLOGY** — register: any, most often semi-formal

- **Moves:** apologise unreservedly in sentence 1 → say what happened (explanation, *not* excuse) →
  acknowledge the effect on them → offer repair or compensation → say what you will do differently
  → apologise once more, briefly.
- **Language:** `I am writing to apologise for ___.` · `I should have ___, and I did not.` ·
  `I understand this must have ___ for you.` · `To put this right, I would like to ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** apologise and say what for · explain why it happened · say how you will make up
  for it.
- **Watch:** the explanation swallowing the apology. If the letter reads as self-defence, Task
  Achievement suffers because the purpose (apologising) was not achieved. Teach the ratio: one
  sentence of explanation, two of repair.

---

**P5 · INVITATION** — register: informal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** name the occasion and invite → give the logistics (date, time, place) → say why you
  want *them* there specifically → anticipate their practical concerns (travel, staying over,
  what to bring) → ask for a reply by a date.
- **Language:** `I'd love you to come to ___ on ___.` · `It's at ___, starting around ___.` ·
  `It wouldn't be the same without you, especially as ___.` · `Let me know by ___ so I can ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** invite them and give the details · explain why the event matters · tell them
  what they need to know / do.
- **Watch:** the "invitation with no logistics" — warm, friendly, and impossible to act on. A date
  and a place are content, and content is Task Achievement.

---

**P6 · ACCEPTING OR DECLINING AN INVITATION** — register: any

- **Moves (declining):** thank warmly → decline clearly and early → give a brief genuine reason →
  express real regret → propose an alternative → close warmly.
- **Language:** `Thank you so much for asking me to ___.` · `Sadly, I won't be able to make it.` ·
  `I'll be ___ that week.` · `Could we ___ instead, perhaps ___?`
- **Bullet triad:** thank them · explain why you cannot come · suggest another arrangement.
- **Watch:** never actually saying no. Learners hedge so hard the refusal is invisible. Decline in
  one unambiguous sentence, then soften around it.

---

**P7 · APPLICATION (job, course, volunteering, membership)** — register: formal

- **Moves:** name the position and where you saw it → state your current situation → give two or
  three specific qualifications with evidence → say why *this* organisation → state availability →
  offer references/documents → request an interview.
- **Language:** `I am writing to apply for the position of ___, advertised ___.` ·
  `I am currently ___, where I have ___ for the past ___.` ·
  `I am particularly drawn to ___ because ___.` · `I am available from ___ and would welcome the
  chance to discuss ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** say what you are applying for and why · describe your relevant experience ·
  say when you are available / what you would bring.
- **Watch:** adjective soup (`hardworking, motivated, punctual`). One evidenced claim beats five
  unevidenced ones — this is the Lexical Resource *precision* point in disguise.

---

**P8 · RESIGNATION / WITHDRAWAL** — register: formal

- **Moves:** state the resignation and the effective date in sentence 1 → give the reason briefly
  and without grievance → acknowledge something positive about the time there → offer a handover →
  thank → close.
- **Language:** `I am writing to give notice of my resignation from ___, with effect from ___.` ·
  `I have decided to ___, and so ___.` · `I am grateful for ___ during my time here.` ·
  `I am happy to ___ before I leave to ensure a smooth handover.`
- **Bullet triad:** say you are leaving and when · explain why · say what you will do to help with
  the handover.
- **Watch:** the missing date. A resignation without an effective date has failed its purpose.
  Also: grievance leaking in. Resignation register is warm and final.

---

**P9 · THANKS** — register: informal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** thank specifically in sentence 1 (name the thing) → say what difference it made,
  concretely → recall one detail that shows you noticed → reciprocate or offer something →
  close forward-looking.
- **Language:** `I wanted to write and thank you properly for ___.` ·
  `It made a real difference, because ___.` · `I still think about ___.` ·
  `The next time you're in ___, you must ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** thank them and say what for · explain how it helped you · say what you would
  like to do in return.
- **Watch:** genericity. `Thank you for everything, it was very kind` is not extension. One
  concrete detail is worth ten adjectives.

---

**P10 · EXPLANATION / JUSTIFICATION** — register: formal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** state the situation you are explaining → give the chronology, factually → separate
  what was in your control from what was not → state the current position → say what you propose.
- **Language:** `I am writing to explain why ___.` · `On ___, I ___, and shortly afterwards ___.` ·
  `Unfortunately, this was outside my control, as ___.` · `The position now is that ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** explain what happened · say why · say what you would like to happen now.
- **Watch:** blurring explanation into apology or complaint. An explanation is neutral: no sorrow,
  no anger. This distinction is itself worth a teaching note.

---

**P11 · GIVING INFORMATION / NEWS / AN UPDATE** — register: any

- **Moves:** state that you are updating them → give the headline first → then detail, ordered by
  what matters to *them* → say what it means for them → invite questions.
- **Language:** `I thought I should let you know that ___.` · `The main change is that ___.` ·
  `For you, this means ___.` · `Do get in touch if ___.`
- **Bullet triad:** say what has happened/changed · give the details · say what they should do.
- **Watch:** chronological dumping. Headline first, chronology second — this is a Coherence point
  the learner can execute mechanically.

---

**P12 · MAKING ARRANGEMENTS** — register: any

- **Moves:** name the thing being arranged → propose specifics (date/time/place) → offer at least
  two options → state your own constraints honestly → assign responsibilities → confirm how you
  will finalise it.
- **Language:** `I'm writing to sort out the arrangements for ___.` ·
  `Would ___ or ___ work better for you?` · `I'm tied up on ___, but otherwise I'm free.` ·
  `If you can ___, I'll ___.` · `Shall we confirm by ___?`
- **Bullet triad:** say what needs arranging · propose the details · say what you need them to do.
- **Watch:** arrangements with no decidable content. Two named options and a deadline is the
  minimum for this purpose to be *achieved*.

---

**P13 · RECOMMENDATION / REFERRAL** — register: formal (a person) or informal (a place)

- **Moves:** state who/what you are recommending and to whom → say how you know it → give two
  specific grounds with evidence → name one limitation honestly → state the recommendation plainly.
- **Language:** `I am writing to recommend ___ for ___.` · `I have known ___ for ___, in the
  capacity of ___.` · `What stands out is ___; for example, ___.` · `The one thing to be aware of
  is ___.` · `I recommend ___ without reservation.`
- **Bullet triad:** say who/what you are recommending · explain why · say what the reader should do.
- **Watch:** unqualified praise. A single honest limitation raises credibility and gives the letter
  a genuine second idea to develop.

---

**P14 · SUGGESTION / PROPOSAL FOR IMPROVEMENT** — register: formal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** establish your standing (user, member, resident) → describe the current situation
  neutrally → make the proposal concretely → give the benefit to *them* → acknowledge the cost or
  objection → ask for consideration.
- **Language:** `As a regular ___, I would like to suggest ___.` · `At present, ___.` ·
  `My suggestion is that ___ could be ___.` · `This would allow ___ without ___.` ·
  `I appreciate that ___, but even ___ would help.`
- **Bullet triad:** explain your connection · describe the problem/opportunity · make your
  suggestion.
- **Watch:** suggestion collapsing into complaint. The tone marker is that a suggestion faces
  forward: no `should have`, only `could`.

---

**P15 · ASKING FOR ADVICE** — register: informal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** state the decision you face → give the two or three options → say what you are worried
  about → say why you are asking *this* person → ask a specific question → say when you need to
  decide.
- **Language:** `I could really use your advice about ___.` · `The choice is basically between ___
  and ___.` · `What's holding me back is ___.` · `You went through something similar with ___, so
  ___.` · `What would you do?`
- **Bullet triad:** explain your situation · say what your options are · ask for their advice.
- **Watch:** asking for advice without stating the options — leaves the reader nothing to answer.

---

**P16 · GIVING ADVICE** — register: informal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** acknowledge their situation → give the advice plainly → justify from your own
  experience → hedge appropriately (it is *their* decision) → offer practical help → close warmly.
- **Language:** `I was sorry to hear about ___.` · `If I were you, I'd ___.` ·
  `When I ___, what worked was ___.` · `Obviously you know the situation better than I do, but ___.`
  · `Let me know if you'd like me to ___.`
- **Watch:** unhedged imperatives (`You must do this`). Advice-giving in English is heavily
  modalised; this is a genuine Grammatical Range opportunity (`I'd`, `you might want to`,
  `it may be worth`, `have you thought about ___ing`).

---

**P17 · CHASING / FOLLOW-UP TO AN IGNORED LETTER** — register: formal

- **Moves:** reference the earlier contact precisely (date, reference) → state that no reply came →
  restate the substance in two lines → escalate the request politely → set a firm deadline → state
  the next step if nothing happens.
- **Language:** `I wrote to you on ___ regarding ___, but have yet to receive a reply.` ·
  `To restate the position briefly: ___.` · `I must now ask that ___ by ___.` ·
  `If I do not hear from you by then, I will ___.`
- **Watch:** this purpose *requires* controlled firmness — the register lesson here is that formal
  English gets more polite as it gets more insistent, not less.

---

**P18 · NOTIFYING A CHANGE / CANCELLING / POSTPONING** — register: any

- **Moves:** state the change up front → give the reason briefly → apologise proportionately to the
  inconvenience → propose the alternative → confirm what happens to money/bookings/commitments →
  ask for confirmation.
- **Language:** `I am writing to let you know that I need to ___.` · `This is because ___.` ·
  `I am sorry for the inconvenience this will cause.` · `Would it be possible to ___ instead?` ·
  `Could you confirm that ___?`
- **Watch:** burying the change. The reader needs the headline in sentence 1; the reason can wait.

---

**P19 · ENQUIRY ABOUT A PURCHASE, LOST PROPERTY, OR A CLAIM** — register: formal

- **Moves:** identify the transaction/item precisely → describe the item or issue in identifying
  detail → state where and when → say what you want (return, refund, replacement, search) →
  give contact details and availability.
- **Language:** `I am writing regarding ___, purchased on ___ (order no. ___).` ·
  `The item is ___, with ___, and it also has ___.` · `I last had it ___.` ·
  `I would be grateful if you could ___.` · `I can be reached on ___ at any time.`
- **Watch:** insufficient identifying detail. This purpose is *the* one where concrete specifics
  are the task, not decoration — three physical details beat any amount of politeness.

---

**P20 · CONGRATULATIONS / GOOD WISHES / SYMPATHY** — register: informal or semi-formal

- **Moves:** react with genuine feeling in sentence 1 → say what you know and why it matters →
  personalise with a shared memory or a specific observation → offer something practical →
  close forward.
- **Language:** `I was delighted to hear that ___.` · `Nobody deserves it more, after ___.` ·
  `I still remember when you ___.` · `If there's anything I can do about ___, just say.`
- **Watch:** card-sentiment. Two lines of feeling plus one shared specific is the shape; five lines
  of feeling is under-developed, not warm.

---

**P21 · OFFERING HELP / VOLUNTEERING** — register: semi-formal or formal

- **Moves:** name what you are offering → say why you are able to (skills, availability) → state
  the limits of the offer clearly → propose a first step → make it easy to accept or decline.
- **Language:** `I would like to offer to help with ___.` · `I have ___ experience of ___, and I am
  free ___.` · `I should say that I can only ___.` · `Shall I ___ to begin with?` ·
  `Please don't feel obliged if ___.`
- **Watch:** vague generosity (`I can help with anything`). Bounded offers are useful offers, and
  boundedness is content.

### 3.5 A difficulty ladder for letters (maps to `difficulty` 1–3)

| Level | What makes it harder | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Single purpose, one register, generous invention room, friendly recipient | thanks + invitation to a friend |
| **2** | Single dominant purpose but a register that must be held; a recipient with authority; one bullet requiring a concrete proposal | complaint to a service provider |

with two competing purposes forces the learner to *sequence* functions and to modulate tone within
a single register — which is precisely what separates band 6 from band 7+ on this task.

## 4. How Task Achievement is actually lost — and the bullet/tone interaction

The four sub-jobs of §1.4 fail in a stable order. This is the "error watchlist" for letters.

| Rank | Failure | What it looks like | Cost | Fixable in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Bullet covered, not extended** | Bullet 3 gets one sentence that restates it | The commonest ceiling on this task | One rewrite |
| 2 | **Register drift** | Formal letter with `Thanks a lot!!` before `Yours faithfully` | TA (tone sub-job) + LR (appropriacy) | 2 minutes of checking |
| 3 | **Sign-off / greeting mismatch** | `Dear Sir or Madam` + `Yours sincerely` | Small but pure loss | 60 seconds, once, forever |
| 4 | **Missing bullet** | Bullet 2 never addressed | Direct hit on Task Achievement | Planning discipline |
| 5 | **No paragraphing** | 170 words, one block | CC | 10 seconds |
| 6 | **Under length** | 128 words | TA, explicitly | Planning + a fixed shape |
| 7 | **Essay-like structure** | `In conclusion, it can be said that…` in a letter to a friend | TA (format) + LR | Genre awareness |
| 8 | **Notes/bullets in the answer** | The learner reproduces the bullets as a list | TA (form) — explicitly not acceptable | One instruction |
| 9 | **Copied situation paragraph** | 40 words lifted from the prompt as the opening | Not counted as own language | One rewrite |
| 10 | **Wrong purpose** | Prompt asks to *apologise*; learner *explains* | TA — the letter did not achieve its purpose | Reading the verb in the situation |

**The bullet × tone interaction — the important, under-taught part.**

Bullets and tone are not independent. Each bullet is a *speech act*, and speech acts carry
different face-threat, so the same register has to be *modulated* bullet by bullet:

- In a formal **complaint**, bullet 1 (describe the problem) is low-threat and can be plain;
  bullet 3 (say what you want) is high-threat and needs the heaviest politeness machinery of the
  letter. Learners do the opposite: they front-load politeness in the description and go blunt on
  the demand.
- In a semi-formal **apology**, bullet 2 (explain why) is where over-explaining reads as
  excuse-making — tone must stay *shorter* here, not warmer.
- In an informal **invitation**, bullet 3 (what they need to know) is the only place where formal
  precision is welcome: dates and addresses are allowed to be plain.
- In an **application**, all three bullets are high-stakes but only bullet 2 (experience) may carry
  self-promotion; self-promotion in bullets 1 and 3 reads as pushy.

So the correct teaching claim is: **one register for the letter; per-bullet modulation inside it.**
That is a genuinely band-7-and-above skill and it is the kind of thing our teaching payload can
carry per prompt in a way no generic guide does.

## 5. What changes between band 5 and band 9 in a letter

The design agent should require the band ladder to tell the **same story**, so that only the
language differs — the speaking pack's standard. For letters, here is what should actually differ
at each rung. All exemplars below authored for this document; they are illustrative of the *kind*
of difference, and the authoring agent must write fresh ones per prompt.

| Band | Coverage | Extension | Register | Structure | Sentence-level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5** | All three bullets mentioned, one only glancingly | Restatement; almost no specifics | Attempted but drifting; sign-off may mismatch | 1–2 paragraphs or a wall | Simple sentences, frequent errors that make the reader work |
| **6** | All three covered, unevenly | Some detail, but at least one bullet is thin; a generic remedy | Mostly held; one or two lapses, usually late | Paragraphs present, roles unclear | Mix of simple and complex; noticeable errors, meaning clear |
| **7** | All three covered and balanced | Each bullet has one specific development; the request is concrete | Held throughout; greeting and sign-off pair correctly | Purpose sentence, one paragraph per function, closing move | Frequent error-free sentences; some flexible structures |
| **8** | Fully covered, purposeful | Every bullet is developed with a specific that only this situation could produce | Register is precise, including modulation across bullets | Organisation invisible because it is right | Wide range; errors rare and minor |
| **9** | Fully and economically | Extension feels natural, never bolted on; the letter would work in real life | Perfectly judged, including where to be brief | Effortless | Full range, precise collocation, rare slips |

**The illustrative sentence ladder** (same content, four renderings — this is the format the
teaching payload should use; annotate each with the criterion the change belongs to):

- **B5:** `The heating is broken two times. It is very cold and my children is sick. Please repair
  fast.`
- **B6:** `The heating has broken down two times this winter. It is very cold in the flat and my
  children have been ill. Could you please repair it soon?`
- **B7:** `The heating has broken down twice since December, most recently on 14 January. The flat
  is now cold enough that both of my children have been unwell, and I would be grateful if an
  engineer could attend within the next week.`
- **B8:** `The heating has failed twice since December, most recently on 14 January, and on each
  occasion it was several days before it was restored. With the flat this cold, both of my children
  have been unwell, so I would be grateful if you could arrange for an engineer to attend before
  the end of next week — any weekday morning would suit us.`

Note what changes: at 5→6 it is *accuracy*; at 6→7 it is *specificity plus polite modality*; at
7→8 it is *density of relevant detail and reader-consideration* (`any weekday morning would suit
us` is a Task Achievement move, not a language move). Teaching that the 7→8 jump is largely a
*content* jump, not a vocabulary jump, is one of the most useful things we can tell a learner.

## 6. What the evidence base says, in one table

| Intervention | Effect size (writing quality) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy instruction (explicitly teaching planning/revising procedures) | **0.82** | Graham & Perin, *Writing Next* / *J. Ed. Psych.* 2007 |
| Summarisation | **0.82** | ibid. |
| Peer assistance | 0.75 | ibid. |
| **Setting specific product goals** | **0.70** | ibid. |
| Word processing | 0.55 | ibid. |
| **Sentence combining** | **0.50** | ibid. |
| Inquiry (gathering concrete data before writing) | 0.32 | ibid. |
| Prewriting activities | 0.32 | ibid. |
| Process-writing approach | 0.32 | ibid. |
| **Study of models** | **0.25** | ibid. |
| **Traditional grammar instruction** | **−0.32** | ibid. |

Sources: [Writing Next research brief](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED499256.pdf),
[Graham & Perin meta-analysis](https://bridgestolearning2009.pbworks.com/f/graham&perin07.pdf).

Four conclusions we should let govern the whole module:

1. **Teaching a procedure beats teaching a rule.** The top intervention is strategy instruction —
   giving learners an explicit, repeatable procedure for planning and revising. Our teaching payload
   should therefore ship *procedures* ("read → classify recipient → set register → one line per
   bullet → write → four-point check"), not lists of tips.
2. **Decontextualised grammar teaching is actively negative.** −0.32. Whatever we build for
   Grammatical Range must be tied to the learner's own sentences (§12), never a grammar quiz.
3. **Models are weak on their own** (0.25) — much weaker than most IELTS prep implies. Models only
   pay off when the learner *does something* with them (§8).
4. **Specific goals are strong** (0.70). "Write a better letter" is not a goal. "This attempt:
   extend every bullet with one dated specific, and hold formal register to the sign-off" is.

## 7. Feedback that changes behaviour

### 7.1 The corrective-feedback evidence

- The **Truscott–Ferris debate** frames the field: Truscott argued error correction had no
  demonstrated benefit and could harm; Ferris argued the conclusion was premature and that the
  question was *which* correction, for *which* errors, delivered *how*. Two decades later the field
  has largely settled on Ferris's framing, with important caveats.
  ([ScienceDirect overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1060374320300254))
- **Focused feedback beats comprehensive feedback for the targeted structures.** Correcting a
  small number of error types produces measurable accuracy gains on those types; correcting
  everything produces attention dispersal and little durable gain.
  ([Ellis et al. 2008, *System*](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ804984);
  [Alimohammadi review](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/tpls/vol04/03/18.pdf))
- **Unfocused feedback is not worthless** — a 2025 study found gains on articles, prepositions and
  tense with effects surviving to delayed post-test — but the effects are smaller and slower.
  ([Nature *HSSC* 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05126-x))
- **Indirect (coded) feedback beats direct correction for long-term development,** because it
  forces the learner to do the repair and therefore to engage with the form. Direct correction wins
  on immediate revision accuracy; indirect wins on subsequent, new writing.
  ([JLE study on coded feedback](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/16039);
  [PMC comparative study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10830576/))
- **Automated feedback shows the transfer problem sharply:** error rates fall across drafts of the
  *same* task with little evidence of transfer to new tasks, and learner *trust* mediates uptake.
  ([Ranalli 2021, *JSLW*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S106037432100031X);
  [AWE research synthesis 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0346251X24001143))

### 7.2 Why "correct everything" produces no gain

Three mechanisms, all of which apply directly to an LLM that happily returns 15 annotations:

1. **Attention dispersal.** With 15 corrections, none of them is *the* thing to work on next. The
   learner reads, nods, and changes nothing. This is the same finding that made "one improvement
   only" the rule in the speaking briefing.
2. **No processing depth.** Direct correction can be absorbed by copying. Copying is not learning:
   it produces a clean second draft and an unchanged third task.
3. **Trust collapse.** LLM annotators produce false positives. A learner who finds two wrong
   corrections in a list of fifteen discounts the whole list — and the AWE-trust literature says
   discounting is exactly what happens.

### 7.3 The writing-feedback contract BandReady should adopt

Codify once, enforce in prompt, post-processing and UI:

1. **Open on exactly one primary fix.** Generate the full annotation set (it is a valuable archive
   and drives the drill queue), but the report's first screen shows one behaviour to change.
2. **That fix must be a behaviour with a retry attached.** "Extend each bullet with one dated
   specific" — with a button that starts a 5-minute targeted rewrite of just that. If a piece of
   feedback has no "try it now", it is a note, not coaching.
3. **Focused, rotating error target.** Each attempt targets **two** error types, chosen from the
   learner's own recurring profile. Everything else is collapsed behind "show all".
4. **Indirect first, direct on request.** Default rendering: underline + a plain-language code
   (`verb tense`, `word partner`, `article`, `register`), with the correction hidden behind a
   reveal the learner opens *after* attempting the repair inline. This is the single cheapest way
   to convert a passive report into a noticing task.
5. **Never claim certainty we don't have.** Annotations the anchoring pass could not verify go in a
   clearly-labelled "possible" list. Preserving trust is a measurable variable, not politeness.
6. **Bands are for benchmarks.** Exam-mode attempts and mocks show bands. Drills and targeted
   rewrites show *behaviour deltas* (bullets extended: 1→3; register lapses: 4→0), not band
   numbers. Kluger & DeNisi's finding that feedback drawing attention to the *self* can make
   performance worse is the reason.

**Report to owner — existing plan conflict.** `docs/plan/05-writing-module.md §6.2` asks the
evaluator for **5–15 annotated errors, 5–10 vocab upgrades, a paragraph-by-paragraph structure
analysis and a model-answer outline** — potentially 30+ discrete feedback items on one 180-word
letter. That is the failure mode in §7.2 exactly. The fix is presentational and cheap: keep
generating them, but the report must open on one, and the annotation layer must default to coded
(hidden-fix) rendering.

## 8. Model answers without producing template memorisation

### 8.1 The problem is real

Models score **0.25** in Writing Next — the weakest positive intervention measured, and barely a
third of strategy instruction. Meanwhile IELTS guidance is explicit that memorised, prefabricated
language is counterproductive because it stops being evidence of the candidate's own English
**[OFFICIAL]**. A model answer handed over before the attempt is, in practice, a template.

### 8.2 What makes a model teach instead of script

The exemplar literature is unusually clear that the *value is in the comparison*, not the text:

- Exemplars make tacit standards accessible in a way rubrics alone cannot, and empirically improve
  learners' understanding of assessment criteria.
  ([Hendry & Anderson, *From copying to learning*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241683445_From_copying_to_learning_Using_exemplars_to_engage_students_with_assessment_criteria_and_feedback);
  [integrative review, *Nurse Education Today*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0260691718301102))
- Exemplars trigger **noticing** — conscious attention to a linguistic problem and to a way of
  solving it — which is the mechanism by which the comparison produces learning.
  ([EAP exemplar synthesis 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/29984475.2025.2495271))
- The gain is substantially larger when the exemplar is accompanied by **dialogue** about *why* it
  is good, rather than presented as a finished object to admire.
  ([exemplars + discussion study, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6129159/))

### 8.3 The five rules for our model answers

1. **Gated.** The model is unavailable until the learner has submitted an attempt. Non-negotiable,
   and already the speaking module's rule.
2. **Same story, different language.** The band ladder must render *the same content* at 5/6/7/8/9
   so the learner cannot conclude "band 8 means better ideas". Only the language and the density of
   relevant detail change. (§5 shows what actually differs.)
3. **Span-annotated by criterion.** Every difference between rungs is tagged TA / CC / LR / GRA and
   carries a one-line reason. An unannotated model teaches admiration; an annotated one teaches
   attribution.
4. **Comparison before explanation.** The first screen after unlocking is not the model — it is a
   *find-the-difference* task: "Here is band 6 and band 7 of the same paragraph. Name two things
   that changed." Only then do we show our answer. This is the noticing step and it is where the
   effect size lives.
5. **Frames have gaps; canned sentences are shown as negatives.** Any reusable language we ship is
   slotted (`I would be grateful if you could ___ by ___`) and sits **beside** a plausible canned
   version labelled *avoid* (`I am writing this letter to bring to your kind attention the
   following matter of great importance`). The contrast is the teaching. A frame without a gap is a
   script.

## 9. Planning

### 9.1 The evidence

- **Pre-task planning** reliably improves **fluency** and **syntactic complexity** in L2 writing;
  effects on **accuracy** are inconsistent. The mechanism is working-memory relief: ideas settled
  in advance free attention for lexical and syntactic formulation.
  ([Ellis & Yuan 2004](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/231965961_The_Effects_of_Planning_on_Fluency_Complexity_and_Accuracy_in_Second_Language_Narrative_Writing);
  [planning-time study, *APJSFLE* 2016](https://sfleducation.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40862-016-0015-6))
- **Unpressured on-line planning** (time to monitor while writing) is what tends to help
  **accuracy**, whereas pre-task planning helps complexity and fluency. They are different levers.
  ([Wu & Ellis 2023, *Language Teaching Research*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688231197575))
- But note the meta-analytic number: **prewriting activities score only 0.32**, while **strategy
  instruction scores 0.82**. The gap says that *planning helps most when the learner has been
  taught a planning procedure*, not when merely given planning time.

### 9.2 What this means for a 40-minute Task 2

The honest synthesis, and what we should teach **[RESEARCH + CONSENSUS]**:

| Phase | Minutes | Non-negotiable output |
|---|---|---|
| Decode the question | 2 | The task verb (*to what extent* / *discuss both* / *what problems, what solutions*), and the exact scope nouns |
| Plan | 5 | A position sentence + two body-paragraph seeds, each with **one specific example already chosen** |
| Write | 28 | — |
| Check | 5 | Personal error list + the position sentence still matches the conclusion |

Five minutes, not ten. The reason to cap it: the 0.32 vs 0.82 gap means extra planning *time*
buys little, but a better planning *procedure* buys a lot — so we spend our effort on the
procedure, not the clock. Beyond about 5 minutes the marginal minute is worth more inside the
28-minute writing window as on-line planning, which is what actually helps accuracy.

### 9.3 What a useful Task 2 plan looks like

A useful plan is **decisions already made**, not topics listed. Four lines, in this order:

```
POSITION : one sentence, the thing I will still be saying in the conclusion
BODY 1   : claim → because → EXAMPLE (named, specific, already chosen)
BODY 2   : claim → because → EXAMPLE   (or: concession → rebuttal)
RISK     : the one word/structure I always get wrong on this topic
```

The test of a plan: **could a stranger write the essay from it?** If the plan says
"Body 1: education", nobody can. If it says "Body 1: online courses widen access — because the
constraint is travel time, not tuition — example: a rural nurse doing a part-time degree at night",
anyone can. That contrast is teachable in 30 seconds and is worth a permanent piece of UI.

For a **letter**, the plan is smaller and different: one line per bullet, each naming a *specific*
(a date, a number, a name, a preference), plus the greeting decision. Three lines and a word.

### 9.4 Plan-vs-execution audit

The plan is already stored (`outline_text`, excluded from scoring, passed to the evaluator as
context). We are not using it. The highest-value use: after scoring, tell the learner **which
planned element never made it into the essay**. "You planned a concession in body 2. There is no
concession in what you wrote." That is a Coherence insight no generic feedback can produce, it is
nearly free, and it makes planning visibly consequential — which is how you get learners to keep
doing it.

## 10. Redrafting: making the rewrite loop teach

### 10.1 The evidence

- Learners who **revise a corrected draft before attempting a new task** make the greatest gains,
  and the add-on effect is strongest for those who keep the corrected draft in front of them while
  writing the new task.
  ([*System* 2021 revision study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X20307016))
- **Explicit revision-strategy instruction works fast** — as little as eight minutes of
  instruction produced markedly better second drafts than uninstructed revision.
  ([Sengupta 2000, *System*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X99000639))
- The **transfer gap** is the danger: automated-feedback studies show error rates dropping across
  drafts of the same task with little carry-over to new tasks.
  ([Ranalli 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S106037432100031X))

### 10.2 The rewrite loop we should build

The existing loop (prefilled previous text + annotations panel + diff + band delta) is a **patching
machine**: it optimises for making the red marks go away. Three changes convert it into a learning
loop:

1. **Name the targets before the rewrite starts.** The rewrite screen opens with two explicit
   goals (from §7.3 rule 3) and nothing else. Not fifteen annotations — two goals. This is
   Writing Next's 0.70 product-goal finding applied directly.
2. **Add a third step: a NEW prompt with the corrected draft visible.** After rewrite-and-rescore,
   the loop must offer *a different prompt of the same purpose and register*, with the corrected
   letter pinned beside it. That is precisely the condition that produced the largest gains in the
   revision literature, and it is the only step that addresses transfer.
3. **Score the loop on behaviour, not band.** The delta strip should lead with "bullets extended
   2→3", "register lapses 4→0", "targeted error type: 6 instances → 1" and show the band delta
   second. Band deltas across a rewrite are noisy and invite gaming.

**The three-step ladder, named:** `Draft → Targeted rewrite → Fresh transfer task`. A prompt is
only "mastered" when step 3 clears, not when step 2 does.

## 11. Timed versus untimed practice

### 11.1 The evidence

Timed conditions reduce **length** and **content quality** but do not reliably damage **accuracy**
or **syntactic complexity**; untimed conditions allow planning, organising and editing, producing
longer and better-organised texts.
([Effect of timing on quantity and quality of test-takers' writing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327671277_The_effect_of_timing_on_the_quantity_and_quality_of_test-takers'_writing);
[Linking adverbials in timed vs untimed essays](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340224238_The_Use_of_Linking_Adverbials_in_L2_Writing_Timed_vs_Untimed_Essays))

The practical reading: **time pressure attacks content and structure first.** Which means the
things that die under the clock are exactly the things Task Achievement and Coherence measure —
so a learner who only ever writes untimed will never discover their real weakness, and a learner
who only ever writes timed will never build the control that survives the clock.

### 11.2 The sequencing rule

Consolidate under no pressure, then impose speed. Concretely, per new task type or new letter
purpose:

| Stage | Condition | Goal | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · **Learn** | Untimed, model gated, phrase help on | Get the shape right at all | All bullets extended; correct register held |
| 2 · **Consolidate** | Generous timer (30 min for a letter, 55 for an essay), help on | Get it right without help | Two consecutive attempts meeting stage-1 criteria |
| 3 · **Compress** | Exam timer, help off, spellcheck off | Get it right under pressure | One exam-mode attempt at target band |
| 4 · **Transfer** | Exam timer, unseen prompt of the same purpose | Prove it wasn't the prompt | Same result on a prompt never seen |

This ladder is cheap to build (mode + timer are already per-attempt fields) and it turns "practice
mode vs exam mode" from a toggle into a curriculum.

### 11.3 Full mock parity

Speaking has a full mock under enforced conditions. Writing does not, and it should: **Task 1 +
Task 2 in one 60-minute block, one submit, no prompt preview, one shared clock.** This is a
genuinely different skill from two separate attempts, because the real losses in the exam are
allocation losses — a candidate who spends 30 minutes on the letter has thrown away marks on the
task worth twice as much. Any mock report must therefore lead with the *time-allocation* verdict
before the bands.

## 12. Sentence-level work: what actually raises Grammatical Range and Accuracy

### 12.1 What works and what doesn't

- **Sentence combining: 0.50.** Consistently effective from primary school to university at
  producing syntactically mature sentences, and one of the eleven Writing Next recommendations.
  ([Writing Next](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED499256.pdf);
  [Keys to Literacy on sentence combining](https://keystoliteracy.com/blog/sentence-combining/))
- **Traditional grammar instruction: −0.32.** Teaching grammar as a separate subject *reduced*
  writing quality in the meta-analysis. This is the most important negative result in the field and
  it should stop us from ever shipping a decontextualised grammar quiz.
- **Coded error correction with learner self-repair** improves accuracy and, unlike direct
  correction, tends to carry into new writing.
  ([JLE coded-feedback study](https://jle.hse.ru/article/view/16039))

The synthesis: **manipulate sentences, don't study rules — and manipulate the learner's own
sentences wherever possible.**

### 12.2 The four drills, tied to the learner's script

**D1 · Combine.** Take 2–4 short sentences the learner actually wrote and ask for one sentence.
Source them automatically: any run of consecutive sentences under ~9 words in their own text is a
candidate. Accept multiple correct answers; show two alternative combinations afterwards and name
the structure each used (relative clause / participle / subordination).

> From a learner's own letter: `The heating broke. It was in December. An engineer came. He did
> not fix it.` → `The heating broke down in December, and although an engineer came out, he did not
> manage to fix it.`

**D2 · Transform.** Give one of the learner's own sentences and a named target structure. Six
targets carry most of the value for IELTS writing: relative clause, participle clause, conditional,
passive for process/impersonality, cleft (`what matters is…`), and nominalisation (`the failure to
repair it`).

> `The company changed the timetable, so my journey is now longer.` → *as a participle clause* →
> `Having changed the timetable, the company has left me with a longer journey.` → *as a cleft* →
> `What has made my journey longer is the change to the timetable.`

**D3 · Correct (coded, self-repair first).** Take the learner's own errors of the two targeted
types, present them with a code and no fix, and require a repair attempt before the reveal. The
research reason for withholding the fix is in §7.1.

**D4 · Upgrade the register.** Letter-specific and unique to this module: give the learner's own
sentence and a target register, and ask for the rewrite. This drills the §2.3 signal list against
their own output, which is exactly the tie-to-own-script condition the evidence favours.

> `Please fix it soon!` → *formal* → `I would be grateful if the repair could be carried out within
> the next week.`

### 12.3 The rule that keeps these honest

**Every drill item must be seeded from the learner's own text where one exists**, and from the
prompt's authored watchlist otherwise. A generic transformation exercise is grammar instruction
with a nicer skin, and grammar instruction scored −0.32.

## 13. The practice loop, assembled

Putting §§6–12 together, the loop a serious learner should be walked through per prompt:

```
1  READ + CLASSIFY   recipient → register; task verb → purpose        (procedure, taught)
2  PLAN              4 lines (essay) / 3 lines + greeting (letter)    (specific goals)
3  WRITE             timed per the §11.2 ladder stage
4  SELF-CHECK        the learner's own 4-point check, before submit   (noticing)
5  SELF-RATE         pick your own band per criterion, then reveal    (self-assessment)
6  REPORT            ONE primary fix + 2 targeted error types
7  NOTICE            find-the-difference against the next band up     (exemplar comparison)
8  DRILL             2–4 items seeded from their own sentences        (sentence combining)
9  REWRITE           against 2 named goals, corrected draft visible
10 TRANSFER          fresh prompt, same purpose, corrected draft pinned
```

Steps 4, 5, 7 and 10 are the ones currently missing from BandReady and are, per the evidence, where
most of the learning is.

## 15. On-screen behaviour, feature by feature

**W1 · Bullet-coverage tracker.** *Editor:* the three bullets render as a persistent checklist in
the prompt panel; the learner ticks one as they finish it (self-monitoring, not scoring).
*Report:* a three-row strip above everything else — for each bullet, one of `covered & extended`
(green) / `mentioned only` (amber) / `not addressed` (red), each with the exact sentence from the
learner's text that does the work, anchored with the existing offset machinery, and — for amber —
the one question that would have extended it (`what would "soon" be as a date?`). For Task 2 the
same widget renders the parts of the question instead of bullets. This is the single most useful
screen in the module and it costs one extra field in the evaluator's JSON.

**W2 · Register & sign-off checker.** Runs locally in the pre-check, before any token is spent.
Deterministic, no LLM: detect greeting form, detect sign-off form, look them up in the §2.2 table;
count contractions, exclamation marks, and hits from a small register-marked word list. If a
mismatch is found, the pre-check modal says exactly what and why — `You opened "Dear Sir or Madam"
and closed "Yours sincerely". With no name, the pairing is "Yours faithfully."` — with **Fix it**
(returns to the editor with the sign-off selected) and **Submit anyway**. Cheap, catches the most
avoidable loss in the whole task, and works offline.

**W3 · One-fix-first report.** The feedback screen opens on a single card: the primary fix as a
*behaviour*, one sentence of why, the learner's own sentence that shows it, and a **Try this now**
button that starts a targeted rewrite. Bands sit below, annotations below that behind
`Show all 12 notes`. No change to the evaluator — this is pure presentation, and it is the highest
impact-to-cost ratio item on the list.

**W4 · Coded annotations.** Annotated spans render underlined with a short code badge
(`tense`, `article`, `partner`, `register`) and **no fix visible**. Clicking opens an inline input:
"rewrite this span". On submit, the learner's repair is compared with the model's fix (exact,
then normalised, then "different but plausible" via a cheap local heuristic → falls back to
showing both). Only then is the explanation revealed. A `Show fixes` escape hatch exists for
learners who want it, off by default. Directly implements §7.1's indirect-feedback finding.

**W5 · Plan skeleton + audit.** The outline scratchpad gains a per-task-type skeleton (§9.3),
pre-filled as ghost text with the four labelled lines (essay) or three bullets + greeting
(letter). It stays free text — never a form. *Report:* a "plan vs execution" line naming any
planned element that never appeared, and any major element in the essay that was never planned.
The plumbing already exists: `outline_text` is stored and already passed to the evaluator.

**W6 · Band ladder.** Per prompt, five renderings of the same answer at bands 5/6/7/8/9 telling the
**same story**, each span-annotated against TA/CC/LR/GRA with a one-line reason per span, plus an
explicit "what changed from the rung below" note for each step. Gated behind an attempt. *UI:* a
band slider over one text; moving it re-renders and flashes the spans that changed. Expensive to
author; nothing else brings the module to the speaking bar.

**W7 · Find-the-difference gate.** When the learner unlocks the model, the first screen shows one
paragraph at their band and the same paragraph one band up, with a text input: "name two things
that changed". They answer, *then* our annotations appear, with theirs listed alongside and
matched where possible. Ten seconds of friction that converts a 0.25-effect-size activity into a
noticing task.

**W8 · Per-prompt error watchlist.** Authored content, exactly like the speaking pack's: two or
three errors *this specific prompt* provokes, each with `wrong` / `right` / `why` / `criterion`.
Shown **before** the attempt in practice mode (forewarning) and used **after** submission to
prioritise which of the LLM's annotations get surfaced first. For a repairs letter that is
present-perfect-vs-past-simple and countability; for a car-free-cities essay it is
`traffics`/`the traffic` and unhedged overgeneralisation.

**W9 · Targeted rewrite.** Replaces the current free rewrite. The screen opens with exactly two
goals stated at the top and a 5-minute suggested timer; the previous annotations panel is collapsed
by default. On resubmit, the delta strip leads with goal-completion, not band.

**W10 · Sentence-combining drill.** Post-report: "You wrote four short sentences in a row here."
Shows them, asks for one sentence, then displays two alternative combinations with the structure
each used named. Item selection is deterministic and local (consecutive sentences under a length
threshold within one paragraph); only the model answer needs an LLM call, and it can be batched
with the evaluation.

**W11 · Transformation drill.** Learner's own sentence + a named target structure from the six in
§12.2. Their attempt is checked for the structural marker (a relative pronoun, an `-ing`/`-ed`
participle onset, `if`/`unless`, a passive auxiliary, `what … is`, a nominalisation), then compared
against a model. The structural check is a local regex; only the model rewrite needs the LLM.

**W12 · Register-upgrade drill.** Letters only. Their own sentence, a target register, their
rewrite, then a comparison that names which of the §2.3 signals changed. This drill exists nowhere
else in the IELTS-prep market and it is directly aimed at the #2 loss in §4.

**W13 · Practice ladder.** The prompt card shows a four-pip progress indicator
(Learn / Consolidate / Compress / Transfer, §11.2). The *Start attempt* modal defaults to the next
stage rather than to a mode toggle. Curriculum "mastery" means stage 4 cleared.

**W14 · Live time plan.** The editor timer gains phase segments from the authored time plan
(`plan → write → check`), rendering as a thin segmented bar. At each boundary a single unobtrusive
line appears for 4 seconds — `Planning time is up — start writing.` No modal, no blocking.
Overtime in a phase is recorded and reported.

**W15 · Target language bank.** Per prompt: 4–6 slotted frames grouped by the *move* they perform
(§3.1), each with a slot hint, plus one plausible canned sentence labelled **avoid** beside each
group. Surfaced in the existing practice-mode phrase-help drawer. **Copy to clipboard only, never
auto-insert** — the existing plan already has this rule and it is the right one.

**W16 · Self-rate before reveal.** After submit and before the report renders, four short
criterion descriptions and four band selectors. The learner picks; then the report opens with both
numbers side by side and one line on the largest gap. Costs one screen; makes the descriptors
learnable instead of decorative.

**W17 · Personal error profile.** Aggregate annotation `type` and code across all scored attempts.
Renders on the writing home as "your three recurring issues" and seeds the two targeted error types
for every new attempt (§7.3 rule 3). Also the source of the drill queue.

**W18 · Full writing mock.** One 60-minute session, Task 1 then Task 2, a single clock, no preview
of Task 2 while writing Task 1, one submit for both. The report leads with a **time allocation**
verdict — minutes spent on each task against the 20/40 target and what that cost — then two band
sets and the exam-weighted overall (T2 double). Mirrors the speaking full-mock and is the only
context in which we should show a Writing band estimate.

**W19 · Transfer step.** After a successful targeted rewrite, offer a *different* prompt with the
same purpose/register/genre, with the corrected letter pinned in a collapsed side panel. This is
the specific condition that produced the largest gains in the revision literature and it is
essentially free: prompt selection by metadata plus a pinned panel we already have
(`RewriteReference.tsx`).

**W20 · Structure plan per prompt.** Authored paragraph roles *for this prompt* — not a generic
skeleton. For a letter: `opening: state purpose + your relationship to the service in one
sentence` / `¶1: bullet 1 — the two failures, each with a date` / etc. Rendered in practice mode
before writing and used as the reference for the plan-vs-execution audit (W5).

## 17. Problems and conflicts to report

1. **`docs/plan/05-writing-module.md §6.2` requests 30+ feedback items per submission.** Directly
   contradicts §7 of this briefing. Recommendation: keep generating, change the presentation
   (W3, W4). No schema change needed.
2. **`05-writing-module.md §9` sets the model-answer band selector to 7/8/9 defaulting to 8, and
   `scoring/writing.py` hard-codes `MODEL_ANSWER_BANDS = (7, 8, 9)`.** The band-ladder feature
   (W6) needs **5 and 6** as well — a band-5 learner shown a band-8 model has been shown a
   different species of text. Recommendation: extend the tuple to `(5, 6, 7, 8, 9)` and default the
   selector to *one band above the learner's current score*, not a fixed 8.
3. **`gt_task1` genres are the three registers, so purpose has nowhere to live.** `scoring/writing.py
   TASKS['gt_task1']['genres'] = ('formal','semi_formal','informal')`. The 21 purposes in §3.2 are
   at least as important as register for teaching and for prompt-bank coverage. Recommendation:
   keep `genre` = register (don't break the scorer) and carry `purpose` as a new top-level or
   teaching-payload key. `WritingPromptRow` allows extra keys, so this is additive.
4. **No writing mock exists** while speaking has one (W18). This is the clearest parity gap and it
   is the only place a Writing band estimate is defensible.
5. **`outline_text` is captured, stored and passed to the evaluator, and nothing is ever done with
   it.** Free feature sitting on the floor (W5).
6. **The prompt bank has one instance of each chart kind and 4 letters covering 3 purposes.** Even
   before teaching payload, the bank cannot support the §11.2 practice ladder — stage 4 (transfer
   to an unseen prompt of the same purpose) is impossible when there is only one prompt per
   purpose. Minimum viable: **3+ prompts per (purpose × register) combination we ship**, and at
   least 3 per chart kind.
7. **Widely repeated claims I have refused to encode** (see §0.1): the "band 5 cap" for a missed
   bullet, the "2025 examiner retraining / band 4 template cap", and any numeric penalty attached
   to register error. If another agent's briefing asserts these, this is the disagreement.
